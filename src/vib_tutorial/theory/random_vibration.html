Random vibration

Wind, a road, a rocket's engines: many inputs are random, never the same twice, but their statistics are steady. A stationary random signal is described by its power spectral density (PSD) G(f), its mean square per hertz: the RMS² is the area under it. A flat PSD across a band is band-limited white noise. A random shaker test is usually specified as such a band, giving the base's acceleration PSD in g²/Hz. For example, 0.01 g²/Hz from 20 to 2000 Hz has an area of 0.01 × 1980 = 19.8 g², so its RMS is √19.8 = 4.4 g (written 4.4 Grms).

The response

A linear system turns a random input into a random output, Gaussian if the input is, with PSD

Gy(f) = H(f) Gu(f) H(f)H,

H the frequency response from the input to the output: here (K − ω²M + iωC)−1 applied to the input's forces, read off as a displacement, an acceleration (−ω² × displacement) or a spring's force. Inputs that are independent add their PSDs. The response's RMS is √∫Gy df, and its running value, the cumulative RMS, climbs a step at each mode: how high each step is says how much that mode contributes. On a shaken base the chain moves relative to the base: in those coordinates the base's acceleration ag pushes each mass with −m ag, and the absolute acceleration is the relative one plus ag.

Miles' equation

A lightly damped mode amplifies its input by Q = 1/2ζ over a band about 2ζ fn wide. If the input is flat across that band, the area under the peak is (π/2) fn Q times the input there, and a single mass on a shaken base has an absolute acceleration of

aRMS = √((π/2) fn Q Ga(fn)),

Miles' equation, the most used formula in random vibration. On the chain each mode is such a system in its modal coordinate, driven by the modal force φTf: its RMS follows, each response takes the mode shape's share of it, and the modes combine by the square root of the sum of their squares. The Miles tab lists them. It is good where the response is resonant and the modes are well apart. It misses what isn't resonant: a forced mass's acceleration far above the modes is the force over the mass (the mass line), modes outside the band, and a band edge that cuts across a peak.

How often, how high

A linear system's response to a Gaussian input is Gaussian: 68.3 % of the time within ±1σ, 95.4 % within ±2σ, 99.73 % within ±3σ, its skewness 0 and kurtosis 3. How often it does things comes from the PSD's spectral moments mk = ∫ fk G df. Rice: it crosses zero upwards ν₀ = √(m₂/m₀) times a second, a level a ν₀ e−a²/2σ² times, and has νp = √(m₄/m₂) peaks a second. Their ratio, the irregularity factor α = ν₀/νp, says how narrow its band is: at 1, one peak per cycle, their heights Rayleigh distributed; towards 0, many small peaks riding on the big swings, some below zero, their heights nearly Gaussian.

The largest peak in a time T grows only slowly with T. Counting each upward crossing as independent, Davenport finds its expected value σ(√(2 ln ν₀T) + 0.577/√(2 ln ν₀T)): about 3.2σ over a hundred cycles, 4.1σ over three thousand, 5.1σ over 270,000. "3σ" is not a maximum: a long life sees more. A narrow-band response's crossings come in clumps, on the swells of its slowly varying envelope, so there are fewer independent chances than ν₀T, and Davenport overestimates the largest peak, most over short times. Vanmarcke counts the clumps, through the band's width δ = √(1 − m₁²/m₀m₂). The Statistics tab compares both with the record's own largest values, stretch by stretch.

Estimating it from a record

Welch's method cuts the record into blocks, windows each (Hann), Fourier transforms them and averages |Y|². Two errors trade against each other:

  • Bias: the lines are fs/block apart, and the window smears each over a few. A peak narrower than that comes out low and wide. Put four or more lines across the half-power width 2ζfn.
  • Random error: one block's estimate scatters by 100 %; averaging n blocks cuts it to 1/√n.

Fine lines need long blocks and many averages need many blocks: a lightly damped mode needs a long record. The RMS itself is less fussy, a whole area: its scatter is about 1/(2√(B T)) for a record T long, B the response's bandwidth.

How this page computes it

The simulated record comes from the exact update, its input synthesized to have the PSD asked for, so the differences between the record's estimates and the exact PSD are the estimates', not the simulation's. The record holds 2.5 samples per period of the band's top: enough to describe it, since it has nothing above the band, but not to see its peaks. It is interpolated eightfold before they are counted.
