Random vibration

Wind, a road, a rocket's engines: many inputs are random, never the same twice, but their statistics are steady. A stationary random signal is described by its power spectral density (PSD) G(f), its mean square per hertz at the frequency f: the RMS σ is the root of the area under it,

\sigma^2 = \int_0^\infty G(f)\,\mathrm{d}f

A flat PSD across a band is band-limited white noise. A random shaker test is usually specified as such a band, giving the base's acceleration PSD in g²/Hz. For example, 0.01 g²/Hz from 20 to 2000 Hz has an area of 0.01 × 1980 = 19.8 g², so its RMS is √19.8 = 4.4 g (written 4.4 Grms).

The response

A linear system turns a random input into a random output, Gaussian if the input is. Each frequency's share of the input passes through the frequency response H, the output's amplitude per unit input, so the mean squares multiply by |H|2. With several inputs, a matrix of them:

G_y(f) = H(f)\,G_u(f)\,H(f)^{\mathsf H}

Here H is (K − Ω2M + iΩC)−1 applied to the inputs' forces, at Ω = 2πf, read off as a displacement, an acceleration (−Ω2 times the displacement) or a spring's force. Inputs that are independent add their PSDs. The response's RMS is the root of the area under Gy, and its running value, the cumulative RMS, climbs a step at each mode: how high each step is says how much that mode contributes. On a shaken base the chain moves relative to the base: in those coordinates the base's acceleration ag pushes each mass with −miag, and the absolute acceleration is the relative one plus ag.

Miles' equation

A lightly damped mode amplifies its input by Q = 1/2ζ over a band about 2ζfn wide. If the input is flat across that band, the area under the peak of |H|2 works out to (π/2)fnQ times the input there (exactly, for the motion relative to the base; the absolute acceleration's is 1 + 4ζ2 times that). So a single mass on a shaken base has an acceleration of miles

a_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\,f_n\,Q\,G_a(f_n)}

the most used formula in random vibration. On the chain each mode is such a system in its modal coordinate, driven by the modal force φrTf: on a shaken base that is −Γrag, its participation factor times the base's acceleration. Its RMS follows, each response takes the mode shape's share of it, and the modes combine by the square root of the sum of their squares (SRSS). The Miles tab lists them. It is good where the response is resonant and the modes are well apart. It misses what isn't resonant: a forced mass's acceleration far above the modes is the force over the mass (the mass line), modes outside the band, and a band edge that cuts across a peak.

How often, how high

A linear system's response to a Gaussian input is Gaussian: 68.3 % of the time within ±σ, 95.4 % within ±2σ, 99.73 % within ±3σ, its skewness 0 and kurtosis 3. How often it does things comes from the PSD's spectral moments,

m_k = \int_0^\infty f^k\,G(f)\,\mathrm{d}f

Rice rice found that it crosses zero upwards ν0 times a second, a level b fewer times, and has νp peaks a second:

\nu_0 = \sqrt{m_2/m_0}, \qquad \nu_p = \sqrt{m_4/m_2} \nu_b = \nu_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-b^2/2\sigma^2}

Their ratio, the irregularity factor αi = ν0/νp, says how narrow its band is. At 1 there is one peak per cycle, their heights Rayleigh distributed. Towards 0 there are many small peaks riding on the big swings, some below zero, their heights nearly Gaussian.

The largest peak in a time T grows only slowly with T. Counting each upward crossing as independent, Davenport davenport finds its expected value

\sigma\Bigl(\sqrt{2\ln\nu_0 T} + \frac{0.577}{\sqrt{2\ln\nu_0 T}}\Bigr)

about 3.2σ over a hundred cycles, 4.1σ over three thousand, 5.1σ over 270,000. "3σ" is not a maximum: a long life sees more. A narrow-band response's crossings come in clumps, on the swells of its slowly varying envelope, so there are fewer independent chances than ν0T, and Davenport overestimates the largest peak, most over short times. Vanmarcke vanmarcke counts the clumps, through the band's width δb = √(1 − m12/m0m2). The Statistics tab compares both with the record's own largest values, stretch by stretch.

Estimating it from a record

Welch's method welch cuts the record into blocks, windows each (Hann), Fourier transforms them and averages |Y|2. Two errors trade against each other:

  • Bias: the lines are Δf = fs/block apart, and the window smears each over a few. A peak narrower than that comes out low and wide. Put four or more lines across the half-power width 2ζfn.
  • Random error: one block's estimate scatters by 100 %; averaging nd blocks cuts it to 1/√nd.

Fine lines need long blocks and many averages need many blocks: a lightly damped mode needs a long record. The RMS itself is less fussy, a whole area: its scatter is about 1/(2√(BT)) for a record T long, B the response's bandwidth.

Test specifications

A random vibration test drives a shaker so that the acceleration at the item's mounting follows a profile: a PSD given by breakpoints joined by straight lines on log–log axes, each sloping some s dB per octave. On such a line G = G1(f/f1)p with the exponent p = s/(10 log102) = s/3.01, and its area is

\int_{f_1}^{f_2} G\,\mathrm{d}f = \frac{G_1 f_1}{p + 1}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{f_2}{f_1}\Bigr)^{p+1} - 1\Bigr]

(or G1f1 ln(f2/f1) at s = −3.01 dB/oct). Its Grms is the root of the segments' areas added up.

  • Where a profile comes from: enveloping. Measured PSDs from the service environment (many flights, many roads) are covered by a few straight lines that lie on or above all of them, and a margin is added for a qualification test. The envelope is conservative by construction: it puts the worst of each band together.
  • Tolerance bands. The shaker's controller measures the control PSD, by Welch's method as above, and must keep it within a band round the profile (a few dB either side), with the overall Grms close to the profile's. Wider alarm and abort limits stop the test before it can damage the item. The estimate's own scatter (1/√nd) is why the band can't be tight at fine resolution.
  • Notching. A shaker table is far stiffer than the structure the item is mounted on in service. In service, the item's own resonance pulls its mounting point's motion down (an antiresonance of the mounting, as the item holds it), but the table holds the profile regardless, and over-tests the item at its resonances. Notching lowers the input in narrow bands round them, by limiting a response, or the force between table and item scharton, to what service would give.
  • Sine-on-random. Rotating machinery (propellers, rotors, engines) adds tones to a broadband background. The test adds sines, fixed or swept, to the random profile. A sine of amplitude A has a mean square of A2/2, and independent parts add their mean squares: σ2 = σrandom2 + ΣA2/2.

How long to run, and how to shorten a long life into a short test at a higher level, is a question of fatigue damage: the Fatigue page, next.

How this page computes it

The simulated record comes from the exact update, its input synthesized to have the PSD asked for, so the differences between the record's estimates and the exact PSD are the estimates', not the simulation's. The record holds 2.5 samples per period of the band's top: enough to describe it, since it has nothing above the band, but not to see its peaks. It is interpolated eightfold before they are counted.

Worked example

The page opens on the default chain (four 1 kg masses, 400 N/m springs and 2 N·s/m dampers) on a base shaken at 0.01 g²/Hz from 0 to 10 Hz, an input of √(0.01 × 10) = 0.316 Grms, looking at the last mass's acceleration. Every mode's damping is ζr = ωr/400, so frQr = fr/2ζr = 200/2π = 31.83 Hz for every mode, and every mode's Miles factor is the same:

\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2} \times 31.83 \times 0.01} = 0.707\ \text{g}

Mode r responds at the last mass with that times |Γrφ4r|, its share of the base's push and of the last mass's motion: 1.241, 0.333, 0.120 and 0.028, so 0.878, 0.236, 0.085 and 0.020 g. Their SRSS is √(0.8782 + 0.2362 + 0.0852 + 0.0202) = 0.913 g, against the exact 0.911 g from H G Hᴴ: Miles is 0.2 % high. Mode 1 is almost all of it.

The response's moments give ν0 = 1.40 crossings a second and νp = 2.47 peaks a second, αi = 0.565: mode 1's swings with mode 2's wiggles on them. Over the Statistics tab's 60 s stretches, ν0T = 83.8, and Davenport expects the largest peak at 3.17σ = 2.89 g; Vanmarcke, counting the clumps (δb = 0.43), at 3.05σ.

A test profile, by way of the formula above: 0.01 g²/Hz at 20 Hz rising at +3 dB/oct (p = 1, near enough) to 0.04 g²/Hz at 80 Hz, flat to 500 Hz, then falling at −6 dB/oct (p = −2) to 2000 Hz. The three areas are (0.01 × 20/2)(42 − 1) = 1.5, 0.04 × 420 = 16.8 and (0.04 × 500/(−1))(4−1 − 1) = 15 g², 33.3 g² in all: 5.77 Grms.
