Stepping an equation of motion

Most structures are solved in time by stepping Mẍ + Cẋ + Kx = f(t) with a fixed Δt. A scheme replaces the derivatives with differences, and each choice gets the motion wrong in its own way. For a linear system the error is easy to see: let the scheme step a free, undamped oscillator. Its exact motion is a circle in (x, ẋ/ω); one step of any scheme is a fixed 2 × 2 (or 3 × 3) matrix, the amplification matrix, and its eigenvalues λ = ρe±iθ say everything:

  • ρ > 1: the motion grows every step, the scheme is unstable at this Δt.
  • ρ < 1: it shrinks: numerical damping, ζ̄ = −ln ρ / |ln λ|.
  • θ ≠ ωΔt: the period is wrong, period elongation (T̄ − T)/T = ωΔt/θ − 1.

All of these depend only on Ω = ωΔt = 2πΔt/T, so each scheme's behaviour is a set of curves against Δt/T (the tabs on the right).

Central difference

ẍn ≈ (xn+1 − 2xn + xn−1)/Δt²: explicit (no equations to solve when M is diagonal), second order, no numerical damping, and the period comes out short. It is stable only for ωΔt ≤ 2, for the highest ω in the model: Δt ≤ Tmin/π. That is the Courant condition of explicit crash and blast codes: a fine mesh has a very fast highest mode, so the step has to be tiny however slow the motion of interest.

Newmark and HHT-α

Newmark assumes how the acceleration varies over a step: xn+1 = xn + Δt ẋn + Δt²[(½ − β)ẍn + βẍn+1], ẋn+1 = ẋn + Δt[(1 − γ)ẍn + γẍn+1], and the equation of motion at n + 1 closes it (an implicit scheme: a linear solve per step).

  • Average acceleration (γ = ½, β = ¼, the trapezoidal rule): unconditionally stable, no numerical damping, second order; the period comes out long, by about (ωΔt)²/12.
  • Linear acceleration (γ = ½, β = ⅙): more accurate, but stable only for ωΔt ≤ 2√3.
  • γ > ½ damps, but drops to first order. HHT-α (Hilber, Hughes and Taylor) instead evaluates the stiffness and damping forces at tn+1+α, with γ = ½ − α, β = (1 − α)²/4, α ∈ [−⅓, 0]: still second order and unconditionally stable, with damping that grows with Ω. It removes the unresolved high modes' noise and barely touches the low ones: the default in many finite-element codes.

Runge–Kutta

Classical RK4 on the first-order form ż = Az + Bf is fourth order: very accurate in period at small steps. It is explicit, so conditionally stable (ωΔt ≤ 2√2 for an undamped oscillator), and it damps: ρ < 1 at any step. Explicit Euler, zn+1 = zn + Δt żn, is the cautionary tale: for an undamped oscillator ρ = √(1 + Ω²) > 1 at every Δt, and the energy grows every step.

The exact update

For a linear model there is a better way. Over a step the free motion is exactly zn+1 = eAΔtzn; if the force is taken as linear between samples (a first-order hold), its part is exact too. This FOH update is what the Simulation page and the One mass pages use: stable and exact in period and amplitude at any Δt, for any stiffness (ρ = e−ζωΔt, the oscillator's own). Its only error is in the force between samples, which is second order in Δt. It needs the matrix exponential, which is cheap for small models and why big nonlinear codes still use Newmark or central difference.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator, the step and the schemes (Things to try, top left).

  • One step of a scheme on a free oscillator is a matrix: its eigenvalues give stability (ρ ≤ 1), period error and numerical damping, all functions of ωΔt.
  • Explicit schemes are conditionally stable, and the limit is set by the fastest mode in the model: ωΔt ≤ 2 for central difference.
  • Newmark's average acceleration is stable at any step but lengthens the period; HHT-α adds damping that grows with frequency, removing unresolved modes.
  • Stable is not accurate: resolve the modes you care about with ten or more steps a period.
  • For a linear model the FOH update is exact at any step, apart from the force between samples.
