The Jeffcott rotor

A disc on a flexible, massless shaft between two bearings is the simplest model of a rotating machine. H. H. Jeffcott's 1919 paper (and A. Föppl's of 1895, hence also the Laval or Föppl–Jeffcott rotor) used it to explain why a turbine can run smoothly above the speed at which its shaft whirls most violently. Here the bearings stand on flexible supports, springs and dampers in the horizontal (x) and vertical (y) directions, and the disc can tilt as well as move sideways.

The notes come in seven parts: the fundamentals; how the response is measured; what drives the rotor once per revolution, and balancing; gravity, and shafts whose stiffness turns with them; stability; rub; and the numerics. Each section gives the idea, its equation and where that comes from, then the numbers on the defaults, and ends with where to find it on the page. The Things to try follow the first six parts, and the notation is listed last.

1. Fundamentals

Degrees of freedom

The shaft spins about its axis z. In each plane, x–z and y–z, four numbers describe its bending: the journal displacements at bearings A and B, the disc's displacement, and the shaft's slope at the disc. That makes 8 degrees of freedom (DOFs) and, in state space, 16 states. The shaft's shape anywhere follows from them, since between the stations a massless beam bends as a cubic. (A shaft with mass needs more DOFs: see The shaft's own mass below.)

Whirl in complex coordinates

Each point of the shaft moves in its own plane, so one complex number holds both of its displacements: w = x + iy. The spin turns x toward y, so w = rweiωt (ω > 0) is a circle traced the way the shaft spins: a forward whirl. w = rwe−iωt is a backward one. A force that turns with the shaft is written the same way, fx + ify, a constant times eiφ, with φ the angle the shaft has turned.

Any orbit at one frequency is an ellipse, and any ellipse is a forward circle plus a backward one:

w = w_f\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t} + w_b\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}

Its semi-axes are |wf| + |wb| and ||wf| − |wb||, and it is traced forward when |wf| > |wb|. The whirl ratio (|wf|² − |wb|²)/(|wf|² + |wb|²) is +1 for a forward circle, −1 for a backward one and 0 for a straight line. A probe in x alone cannot tell forward from backward, since x = Re w is the same for w and its mirror image. Two probes at right angles can: their orbit, or the full spectrum of w, which puts forward whirl at positive frequencies and backward at negative.

The classic case: disc at midspan

With the disc at midspan on identical bearings, the problem is symmetric about the disc, so the disc's translation and its tilt are uncoupled. The translation sees the shaft's midspan stiffness ks = 48EI/L³ in series with the two supports in parallel, 2kb. With light journals,

\omega_c = \sqrt{k_{eq}/m}, \qquad \frac{1}{k_{eq}} = \frac{1}{k_s} + \frac{1}{2k_b}

With the defaults (d = 10 mm, L = 0.5 m, steel), ks = 37,700 N/m and kb = 50,000 N/m, so ωc = 165.5 rad/s, 1580 rpm. The full model, with its 0.1 kg journals, puts it at 1584 rpm. Flexible supports lower the critical speed: on rigid bearings it would be 1854 rpm.

Unbalance response: heavy spot and high spot

The disc's centre of mass sits a distance e from the shaft's axis: an unbalance U = me. Spinning at Ω, it pulls the shaft round with a force meΩ² that turns with it. So the disc whirls once per revolution (synchronous, 1X), forward, and in a circle if the rotor is isotropic.

Take the classic case as one mass m on a spring k with a damper c, the heavy spot at the keyphasor mark, so that φ = Ωt. In w = x + iy the two planes' equations become one:

m\ddot{w} + c\dot{w} + kw = me\Omega^2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega t}

Its steady state is a forward circle, w = XeiΩt. With r = Ω/ωc and the damping ratio ζ = c/(2mωc) = c/(2√(km)),

X = \frac{me\Omega^2}{k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}c\Omega} = \frac{er^2}{1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta r}

So the whirl's radius is |X| = er²/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²), and the phase lag, −arg X, has tan(lag) = 2ζr/(1 − r²).

The heavy spot (where the unbalance is) leads the high spot (where the shaft is displaced most) by a phase lag. The lag is 0° well below the critical speed, about 90° near it, and 180° well above it. Above the critical speed the shaft deflects away from the heavy spot. As r → ∞ the disc spins about its own centre of mass, and the shaft's centre whirls on a circle of radius e. This self-centring is what lets machines run supercritically.

The disc seen end on, from beyond bearing B (z toward you), whirling about the bearings' centre O. The keyphasor mark is at φ from +x, with the heavy spot at it here (in general at ψ from it). The high spot, the rim's point farthest from O, lags the heavy spot: here by 60°, below the critical speed.

With the defaults (e = 20 µm) the disc whirls 2.2 µm at 500 rpm, with 2° of lag. At the 1584 rpm critical the lag is 88°, and the peak, 217 µm, comes at 1589 rpm. At 3000 rpm the whirl is 28 µm, with 176° of lag. At r = 1 the equation gives X = e/(2iζ): a lag of 90° and a whirl of e/(2ζ). The first mode's ζ is 0.046, so e/(2ζ) = 215 µm, within 1% of the peak.

The lag reaches 90° at 1586 rpm, not quite at the critical, and the peak comes later still. The peak is late even for one mass on a spring: an unbalance response peaks at r = 1/√(1 − 2ζ²), just above the critical. The lag is late because the damping is at the supports, in series with the shaft, not at the disc. As the speed rises the dampers stiffen the supports a little, so the rotor is not quite the one-mass oscillator above.

The gyroscopic effect

A spinning disc has angular momentum IpΩ along its axis. Tilted by the slopes, the axis points along (θx, θy, 1), so the angular momentum has a sideways part IpΩ(θx, θy). As the disc whirls and its tilt changes, that part changes at IpΩ(θ̇x, θ̇y), and changing an angular momentum takes a moment, about an axis at right angles to the change. So tilting the disc in one plane takes a moment in the other. With the tilt's own inertia, Idθ̈, the slopes' equations are:

I_d\ddot\theta_x + \Omega I_p\dot\theta_y = M_x, I_d\ddot\theta_y - \Omega I_p\dot\theta_x = M_y

So the gyroscopic term multiplies the velocities and sits beside the damping: Mq̈ + (C + ΩG)q̇ + Kq = f. But G is skew-symmetric, so the power it absorbs, q̇TΩGq̇, is zero. It does no work, and so it cannot damp anything. Instead it couples the two planes' tilts and makes the natural frequencies depend on the speed.

Each whirl mode splits into a forward whirl (the same way as the spin) and a backward whirl. In a forward whirl the gyroscopic moment resists the tilting, as if the shaft were stiffer, so the frequency rises with speed. In a backward whirl it does the opposite. For the disc's tilt on its own (on a rigid support of stiffness kθ):

I_d\,\omega^2 \mp I_p\Omega\,\omega - k_\theta = 0

The forward root meets the 1X line, ω = Ω, where (Id − Ip)Ω² = kθ. That has no solution whenever Ip ≥ Id: for a disc, or a solid cylinder shorter than √3 times its radius. Then an unbalance can never make the tilting mode resonate forward. For a thin disc Ip = 2Id, and the forward tilting frequency tends to 2Ω. A long rotor (Id > Ip) does have a forward conical critical speed.

Why the classic case hides it. At midspan, on identical bearings, the disc whirls without tilting, so the gyroscopic moment never enters the first mode. Only the disc's own tilting (conical) mode, high above, splits. Move the disc off centre, or make the bearings different, and the translation and tilt couple. Then the first mode splits too, and its critical speed moves.

The two whirls are a spinning top's two precessions (see From the top to the rotor, Gyroscope page): the forward one its fast precession, near IpΩ/Id, the backward one its slow precession.

The Campbell diagram and critical speeds

Plotting each damped natural frequency against the spin speed gives the Campbell diagram. The unbalance turns once per revolution, so it drives at Ω/2π: the 1X line. Where a whirl frequency crosses the 1X line, the unbalance drives that mode at its own natural frequency: a critical speed. In an isotropic rotor the unbalance is a purely forward rotating force, so it drives only the forward branches. The backward crossings are critical only for an anisotropic rotor, or for other excitations.

A schematic Campbell diagram. Spinning, a mode splits into a forward branch and a backward one. The unbalance drives the forward crossing of the 1X line, the critical speed. The backward crossing matters only on an anisotropic rotor. Also draw adds the 2X, 3X and ½X lines.

Other excitations come at other multiples of the speed:

  • 2X: a shaft stiffer one way than the other (a keyway, a crack) flexes twice per revolution under its own weight, and misalignment pulls twice per revolution. Where a forward branch meets 2X, at about half its critical speed, such a rotor has a secondary critical (see The asymmetric shaft).
  • 3X: a breathing crack's stiffness changes with 3X in it too, so its weight drives 3X near a third of the critical speed (see The cracked shaft).
  • ½X: the oil in a plain journal bearing circulates at about half the shaft's speed, so its instability begins near ½X (see Stability).

The overhung rotor

Fans, pump impellers and many small turbines carry their disc outside the bearings, on an overhang ℓ (a/L > 1 past bearing B, or below 0 past A). The shaft is then a span between the bearings and a cantilever beyond, and at the disc its stiffness is 3EI/(ℓ²(L + ℓ)). Pushed sideways, the overhung disc tilts as much as it moves: in the first mode its slope is about 1/ℓ of its displacement. So the gyroscopic moment, small for a disc between the bearings, acts on the first mode in full, and the forward and backward criticals separate strongly.

The Overhung rotor preset puts a 3 kg disc of radius 150 mm 100 mm past bearing B, on supports of 200,000 N/m. At rest its first mode is 14.5 Hz (870 rpm). Spinning, the forward whirl stiffens and the backward one softens: 21.4 and 8.6 Hz at 1284 rpm, 28.9 and 3.1 Hz at 5000 rpm. The backward branch meets the 1X line at 664 rpm, and the forward one at 1284 rpm. That is 48% above the critical speed the same rotor would have without the gyroscopic effect (870 rpm, with Ip = 0), and above the textbook estimate that ignores the tilt (1046 rpm). The unbalance drives only the forward crossing, so that is the one critical speed it shows. The disc's conical mode (53 Hz at rest) has a forward branch rising faster than 1X (Ip = 2Id), so it is never critical.

Anisotropic supports and backward whirl

Supports stiffer vertically than horizontally are common: gravity, foundations and oil-film bearings all make them so. They split each mode into a horizontal one and a vertical one, with two critical speeds, and the orbits become ellipses. Between the two criticals the horizontal response already lags by more than 90° while the vertical one does not yet. There the shaft whirls backward, against its spin, while the unbalance still turns forward. A shaft whirling backward is bent back and forth once per revolution as it spins: a fatigue loading that a forward synchronous whirl does not cause.

The shaft's own mass: beam finite elements

Jeffcott's shaft has no mass: it only holds the disc. That keeps the model small, because a massless shaft bends as a cubic between the stations, and four DOFs in each plane describe it exactly. A shaft with mass, spread along it, also has its sections' rotary inertia and their gyroscopic moment. Its shape is then no longer set by the stations alone, so it is cut into beam finite elements. Each bends as a cubic between two nodes, and each node has a displacement and a slope in each plane.

A light shaft changes little. The defaults' steel shaft weighs 0.31 kg beside the 1 kg disc, and it takes the first critical speed from 1584 to 1458 rpm. The old rule adds 17/35 of a simply supported shaft's mass to the disc, which gives 1477 rpm. (The rule is Rayleigh's method, taking the shaft's shape as its static deflection under a load at the disc.)

But a shaft has bending modes of its own, and a heavier one brings them down into the speed range. In the Long shaft preset (a 2 kg disc on a 1 m, 20 mm shaft of 2.47 kg) the critical speeds are 1589 and 6582 rpm. The massless model puts the first at 2146 rpm, 35% high, and it has no second: its next modes are the journals bouncing on their supports, heavily damped. Gravity shows the difference too: the disc sags 375 µm, not the massless model's 200 µm.

Consistent or lumped mass. The consistent mass matrix spreads each element's mass with the same cubic shapes as its stiffness. It is a Rayleigh–Ritz model, so at rest and undamped its frequencies are never too low, and they come down as elements are added. On the Long shaft the second critical comes out at 7393, 6591 and 6582 rpm with 2, 4 and 8 elements. A lumped mass matrix, half of each element's mass at each end, is simpler but has no such bound. Here it gives 8187, 6733 and 6620 rpm, because with few elements much of the mass sits on the bearings, where it hardly moves.

2. Measuring the response

Run-up and coast-down: Bode and polar plots

On a real machine the response is measured as the speed changes. A keyphasor, a once-per-turn mark on the shaft, starts each revolution as it passes +x. (The heavy spot is at the mark unless you move it.) A tracking filter takes the 1X component over one revolution:

V = \frac{1}{\pi}\int x(\varphi)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varphi}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi, \qquad x \approx |V|\cos(\varphi - \text{lag})

|V| is the 1X amplitude. The phase lag is the angle the shaft turns from the keyphasor to the probe's positive peak. The Bode plot draws both against speed. The polar plot draws V as a point, its angle the lag (clockwise, against the rotation). In steady state, through a lightly damped critical speed, V traces very nearly a circle, lowest (lagging 90°) near the critical. A probe in y reads 90° more lag than one in x on a circular orbit, since the shaft turns 90° more before the high spot reaches it.

Swept through a critical at a finite rate, the response differs from the steady state in four ways:

  • The transient peak is lower than the steady-state one, and lower the faster the ramp. The whirl builds up with the mode's time constant, 1/(ζωc). A ramp at Ω̇ (rad/s²) crosses the half-power band 2ζωc in 2ζωc/Ω̇. So the ramp is slow enough to reach the steady-state peak only when Ω̇/ωc² is well below 2ζ². With the defaults (ζ = 0.046, 2ζ² = 0.0043, the peak 217 µm at 1589 rpm), sweeping 800 to 2400 rpm, a run-up at 100 rpm/s (Ω̇/ωc² = 0.0004) peaks at 100% of it. At 500 rpm/s (0.0019) it peaks at 95%, at 2000 rpm/s at 78%, and at 5000 rpm/s (0.019) at about 60%.
  • The peak comes after the critical speed on a run-up, and before it on a coast-down. The response lags the sweep. At 500 rpm/s the run-up peaks at 1672 rpm (82 rpm late) and the coast-down at 1498 rpm (92 rpm early). At 5000 rpm/s they peak some 350 rpm late and 250 to 350 rpm early. (That fast, the speed changes by 160 rpm in one revolution near the critical, so the tracked curve is coarse: one point per revolution.) The coast-down peaks lower, at 87% of the steady-state peak at 500 rpm/s and about 50% at 5000 rpm/s, because its peak comes at a lower speed, where the unbalance force UΩ² is smaller.
  • Beating just past the critical. The whirl built up at the critical speed rings on as free vibration at the natural frequency ωc, while the unbalance keeps driving at Ω. The two beat at Ω − ωc, faster as the speed moves on. So the 1X amplitude wobbles after the peak. The largest displacement over each revolution (the dots) wobbles more, since it includes the free vibration in full. On the polar plot the tracked vector loops around the steady-state circle.
  • The parameter that matters is Ω̇/ωc², against ζ. Measure time in units of 1/ωc, and a single mode accelerated through resonance has only two parameters left: ζ and the dimensionless acceleration Ω̇/ωc². So the ratio of the transient peak to the steady-state one, and how far the peak is shifted, depend on these alone. F. M. Lewis first worked this out (1932, Vibration during acceleration through a critical speed), and much later work on passage through resonance builds on it.

On the page. Run up and coast down ramps to the first speed and waits for the start-up transient to die away. It then runs up to the second speed and back at the ramp rate, tracking each revolution. Tick Hold earlier sweeps to compare ramp rates. The summary under the Bode plot gives each sweep's peak against the steady-state one, and how many rpm away from it the peak came.

Waterfall and cascade plots

A spectrum analyser on a machine takes the spectrum of a block of signal, then of the next, and so on. Stacked against time the spectra make a waterfall plot; against the speed during a run-up or coast-down, a cascade. The unbalance makes a ridge along +1X that moves with the speed and peaks at the critical. Whatever sets the rotor ringing (the passage through the critical, a tap, an instability) shows at the free whirl frequencies. These stay put, or move only as the gyroscopic effect moves them, while the 1X ridge sweeps past. That is how a natural frequency is told from a forced response on a running machine.

A measured Campbell diagram. Tap the disc every half second through a run-up, and each tap rings both whirls of each mode at the speed it came at. So the peaks trace the Campbell diagram's branches. On the Overhung rotor preset, from rest to 2,500 rpm, the first mode's backward whirl falls from 14.5 to 5.7 Hz and its forward whirl rises to 25.5 Hz. The 1X ridge grows where it crosses the forward branch, at the critical speed (1284 rpm), and peaks just after it, as on the Bode plot. On a full spectrum the backward branch lies on the other side of zero, so the +1X ridge never meets it. The backward crossing on the Campbell diagram (664 rpm) is not a critical for an isotropic rotor.

Block length. A block of Tb seconds resolves frequencies 1/Tb apart: 2 Hz for 0.5 s. A longer block resolves more finely, but while the speed ramps at Ω̇ it changes by Ω̇Tb over the block, smearing the 1X ridge. That is 100 rpm (1.7 Hz) at 200 rpm/s, but 2500 rpm (42 Hz) at 5000 rpm/s, where the ridge is a smear. Analysers get round this by order tracking: sampling at fixed shaft angles, from the keyphasor, so that 1X stays in one bin whatever the speed. The Bode plot's once-per-revolution 1X vector is that kind of measurement.

3. Once-per-revolution forces and balancing

Other 1X sources: a bent shaft and a skewed disc

Unbalance is not the only thing that turns with the shaft. Anything fixed to the rotor and off its axis drives it once per revolution, and two more are common.

A bent shaft (initial bow). A shaft can keep a bend: a thermal bow after a hot shutdown, a sag left after standing, or a bent repair. Say it is δb at the disc, in the shape a load at the disc would give. Its elastic force is Kshaft(q − qbow), so the bow acts as a force Kshaftqbow turning with the shaft, kδb at the disc. Unlike the unbalance's meΩ², it does not grow with speed. For the classic Jeffcott rotor, with the heavy spot at the mark,

(k - m\Omega^2 + \mathrm{i}c\Omega)\,X = me\Omega^2 + k\delta_b\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta_b}

with βb the bow's angle from the mark. So:

  • At low speed the probes read the bow itself. The shaft just carries its bend round: the slow-roll runout. The bow's forces on the disc and the journals balance, so the bearings carry nothing. The 1X response starts at δb, not at zero, and on the polar plot the curve starts at the bow vector, not at the origin.
  • Well above the critical the disc's whirl dies away. The disc stays on the bearings' axis, and the shaft bends round it to cancel the bow. The unbalance's response, by contrast, tends to e.
  • With an unbalance, the angle between them decides. In phase they add: with the defaults and δb = 20 µm, the peak doubles to 435 µm. Opposite (βb = 180°), the bow's response falls with speed while the unbalance's grows, and they cancel at one speed, where meΩ² = kδb in the classic case. Choose δb = e, opposite, and the disc's centre of mass sits exactly on the bearings' axis. Then nothing whirls at any speed, and the probe reads a steady 20 µm, the bow.

Measured from its bent shape, the shaft's deflection Y = X − δbeiβb obeys (k − mΩ² + icΩ)Y = (me + mδbeiβb)Ω² − icΩδbeiβb. Relative to its bow, the shaft sees the disc's mass carried round on the bow: an unbalance mδb. The last term is a damper on the disc itself dragged round the bow; here the damping is at the bearings, which the bow doesn't move, so it drops out. The probe adds the bow itself, as runout. With the disc at midspan the bow is flat there, so the unbalance is all of it, and one weight can take out every force the bow makes, at every speed, but not the runout. That is why the slow-roll vector is subtracted from the 1X readings before balancing (see Single-plane balancing below).

Off centre, the bow also tilts the disc, by the bowed shaft's slope θb there. The disc's inertia carried round on that tilt is a skew (see A skewed disc below), a moment (Id − Ip)θbΩ², and one weight cannot cancel a moment at every speed. On the defaults' light disc it is tiny: at a/L = 0.3, a 20 µm bow balanced at 1200 rpm leaves at most 0.08 µm beyond the runout up to 6000 rpm, and none at all with Id = Ip.

A skewed disc (couple unbalance). A disc mounted out of square has its principal axis tilted by τ from the shaft's. Its inertia acts on its own axis, so spinning it takes a moment (Id − Ip)τΩ² that turns with the shaft. That is the unbalance force's form, with U replaced by (Id − Ip)τ. A thin disc (Ip > Id) is pushed toward square to the spin axis: as Ω → ∞ the shaft bends at the disc by −τ, and the disc spins flat. A long rotor (Id > Ip) is pushed the other way, and its forward conical mode can then meet the 1X line. At midspan on identical bearings the skew only tilts the disc. Move the disc off centre and the tilt and the translation couple, so the skew moves the disc too. A static unbalance (the mass off the axis) can be corrected by one weight in one plane. A couple unbalance needs two weights in two planes, equal and opposite.

Single-plane balancing

Balancing puts a weight on the rotor to cancel its unbalance. Where the heavy spot is cannot be seen, and the phase lag between it and the high spot depends on the speed. So it is found from measurements, by influence coefficients:

  1. Turn slowly and read the 1X vector: the slow roll Vs, runout that no unbalance causes (a bow, or on a real machine a scratch or a magnetic spot on the probe's track).
  2. Run at the balancing speed and read the vector as found, V0.
  3. Stop, add a known trial weight T (mass × radius, at an angle from the keyphasor mark), run again and read V1. The rotor is linear, so the weight's effect is V1 − V0, and the influence coefficient is α = (V1 − V0)/T: microns, and degrees of lag, per g·mm of weight.
  4. Take the trial off, put on the correction Wc = −(V0 − Vs)/α, then run and check.

All the quantities are vectors (complex numbers). A reading's angle is its phase lag from the keyphasor, and a weight's is its angle on the rotor. A trial weight should change the reading by a good fraction (30% or more) without making the rotor unsafe to run. For an unbalance at the disc alone, the one weight balances the rotor at every speed, since unbalance and weight both make forces growing as Ω² at the same place.

Why the slow roll is subtracted. Take the defaults with a bow of 20 µm at 90°, read at the disc, balancing at 1200 rpm. The disc reads 20 µm at 100 rpm, 52 µm at 1200 rpm and 293 µm at the critical. With the slow roll subtracted, the correction is 1.41 g at 20 mm, 225°. It cancels both the unbalance and the bow's mδb, and from then on the disc reads about 20 µm at every speed: the runout alone, with no force behind it. Balanced on the raw reading instead, the weight (1.95 g at 243°) zeroes the reading at 1200 rpm by leaving the rotor out of balance: 162 µm at the critical, 41 µm at 3000 rpm. A bowed rotor cannot be made to read zero at every speed with one weight.

Taking the slow roll too fast spoils the balance. At 300 rpm the reading already holds some unbalance response, so the weight comes out a little light (1.37 g instead of 1.41 g). At the critical the disc then moves 8.6 µm beyond its runout, against 0.9 µm with the slow roll taken at 100 rpm. The total readings there hide this: 16.6 µm looks better than 19.5 µm only because the leftover unbalance's response happens to partly cancel the runout. Judge a balance by the motion beyond the slow roll, |V − Vs|, not by the total.

What one plane cannot do. A weight in the disc's plane makes a force, and a skewed disc makes a moment. Off centre, both move the disc. So a single weight can cancel the reading at the balancing speed, but not at every speed. With a/L = 0.3 and a skew of 0.5°, balanced at 1200 rpm, the 72 µm at the critical falls only to 5.7 µm, and 9 µm is left at 6000 rpm. A couple unbalance needs two-plane balancing, with weights in two planes and the influence coefficients forming a 2 × 2 matrix.

Two-plane balancing

A rigid body's unbalance is a force and a couple, so cancelling it takes two weights in two planes, and two probes to see it. The procedure is the single-plane one with vectors. Read the slow roll Vs and the readings as found, V0 (one per probe). Put the trial weight T in plane 1 alone and read V1, then move it to plane 2 and read V2. Each trial run gives a column of the influence matrix:

\alpha_{ij} = \frac{(V_j - V_0)_i}{T},

and the correction

W_c = -\alpha^{-1}(V_0 - V_s)

That is a weight for each plane, with both trials taken off. With more probes than planes, the same formula gives the least-squares weights.

Where the planes are matters. Take the same rotor as above (a/L = 0.3, skew 0.5°, the default 20 g·mm unbalance), with probes at the two bearings, balanced at 1200 rpm and read at the disc. Every weight below is a mass at a 20 mm radius.

  • On the disc's faces, 40 mm apart: 7.32 g at 180° and 6.32 g at 0°. They are nearly equal and opposite, so mostly a couple. Together they make a force and a moment on the disc, the same form as the unbalance and the skew, so they cancel both exactly. The rotor is then balanced at every speed: the 72 µm at the critical falls to nothing, as does the 13.6 µm at 6000 rpm.
  • On the shaft, 50 mm either side of the disc: 3.23 g and 2.22 g, almost what faces 100 mm apart would need (3.23 g and 2.23 g). But the shaft between them and the disc bends, so they are not quite a force and a couple on the disc. Both probes read zero at 1200 rpm, and 2.7 µm is left at the critical, 1.2 µm at 6000 rpm.
  • On the shaft near the bearings (20 and 480 mm): small weights, 1.25 g and 0.33 g, since the planes are far apart. But the forces are now far from the unbalance. The probes read zero at 1200 rpm, yet at the critical the disc still reads 12.8 µm. A flexible rotor balanced away from its unbalance is balanced only at the balancing speed. (With more planes it can be balanced at more speeds: modal balancing puts a set of weights against each mode in turn.)

Planes close together are hard to tell apart. A weight in one face of the disc moves the probes almost as one in the other face does, so the columns of α are nearly parallel. Their difference, which is what the couple acts through, is small, and dividing by it inflates the weights and any error in the readings. The condition number of α measures this: 27.6 for faces 40 mm apart, 11 at 100 mm, 1.5 for planes near the bearings. With 1% random error in every reading (the simulation's readings have none), faces 40 mm apart leave a median of about 18 µm at the critical instead of 0, and faces 100 mm apart about 8 µm. Real balancing machines put the planes as far apart as the rotor allows.

4. Gravity, and stiffness that turns

Gravity on a horizontal rotor

Laid horizontal, the rotor carries its own weight. The disc's mg bends the shaft, and the bearings' share of it, with the journals' own weight, compresses the supports. With the defaults the disc sags 378 µm and the journals 118 µm, nineteen times the unbalance's 20 µm eccentricity. The rotor whirls about that sagged position, so its orbits sit below the bearings' centre. A DC-coupled proximity probe, which reads the gap, sees the orbit offset by the sag. An AC-coupled one sees only the vibration.

The model is linear, so the sag is a static deflection, qs = (K + ΩH)−1fg, and the vibration about it is exactly what it would be without gravity. In particular gravity alone makes no whirl, however different the supports' stiffness vertically and horizontally: a constant force on a linear rotor only moves its equilibrium. There are three exceptions, each below. A shaft stiffer one way than the other bends more under its weight twice per turn, a 2X excitation (The asymmetric shaft). A crack is opened and shut by the weight as the shaft turns (The cracked shaft). And a fluid-film bearing's stiffness depends on the load it carries (The fluid-film bearing).

The sag need not be straight down. Where the stiffness is not symmetric, the weight also pushes sideways. With cross-coupled bearings, kxy = 15,000 N/m, the journals settle 17° from the vertical in the direction of rotation, much as a fluid-film journal sits at an attitude angle. With internal damping the shaft turns through its own sag, bent the same way at every angle, and the damping resists that. With ci = 20 N·s/m the disc moves sideways by 14 µm at 1000 rpm, 42 µm at 3000 rpm and 78 µm at 6000 rpm.

The asymmetric shaft: a two-pole rotor

The rotor of a two-pole generator has slots for its winding cut along two sides, so it is stiffer bending one way than the other. A keyway or a flat does the same on a smaller scale, and a crack that opens and closes is a cousin. Bending toward its stiff axis the shaft has EI(1 + Δ), and across it EI(1 − Δ). The stiff axis is at βs from the keyphasor mark, and it turns with the shaft. So, seen from the bearings, the stiffness changes twice per revolution: K(φ) = K + ΔK(2(φ + βs)). The equations have a periodic coefficient, and three things follow.

Two critical speeds, and an unstable band between them. Seen from axes turning with the shaft, the stiffness stands still (and so does everything else, on isotropic supports). But the centrifugal force −mΩ² and the Coriolis force join in. To hold a deflection along an axis, the shaft needs more stiffness there than mΩ². Below the soft axis's critical speed it has it both ways. Between the two axes' critical speeds the centrifugal force beats the soft axis but not the stiff one, so a bend along the soft axis grows. It turns with the shaft, so from the bearings it is a growing forward whirl locked at exactly 1X.

For the classic Jeffcott rotor on rigid bearings, with xr and yr along the stiff and soft axes, undamped:

m(\ddot{x}_r - 2\Omega\dot{y}_r - \Omega^2x_r) + k(1 + \Delta)x_r = 0, m(\ddot{y}_r + 2\Omega\dot{x}_r - \Omega^2y_r) + k(1 - \Delta)y_r = 0

The −Ω² terms are the centrifugal force and the 2Ω terms the Coriolis force. Try est: the determinant is a quadratic in s², and its constant term is (k(1 + Δ) − mΩ²)(k(1 − Δ) − mΩ²). Between the axes' critical speeds, k(1 − Δ) < mΩ² < k(1 + Δ), that is negative, so one root s² is positive. Its s is real and positive: a bend that grows without turning in these axes. Outside the band both roots s² are negative, and the undamped rotor neither grows nor decays.

Undamped, that band runs exactly between the critical speeds of round shafts as stiff as each axis: with Δ = 0.15 and undamped supports, 1479 to 1645 rpm. Damping narrows it, and below a threshold asymmetry there is none: Δ = 0.123 with the defaults' 100 N·s/m supports, 0.178 with 400 N·s/m. Unlike the instabilities of Stability, it ends: past the stiff axis's critical speed the rotor is stable again. Mapped against Δ and the speed, it is an Ince–Strutt chart, like a Mathieu equation's. It has one tongue between the two critical speeds, its tip lifted off Δ = 0 by the damping. The Two-pole rotor preset (Δ = 0.15) has critical speeds of 1491 and 1665 rpm, and it is unstable from 1527 to 1631 rpm. Tapped at 1580 rpm, its whirl grows ×e every 0.56 s at 26.3 Hz: the speed, not a natural frequency.

The gravity critical. Laid horizontal, the shaft's weight is a constant force on a stiffness that turns, so the rotor never settles. Its deflection swings twice per revolution about a mean sag: a 2X whirl, largest where 2Ω meets a natural frequency, near half the critical speed. The preset's disc whirls 447 µm at 2X at 785 rpm, more than its mean sag of 384 µm. Run up and coast down from 500 to 1200 rpm at 200 rpm/s, and the 2X reaches 93% and 94% of the steady-state peak. A 2X peak at half the first critical speed on a run-up is the classic signature of a two-pole generator (or of a cracked shaft). Balancing does nothing to it.

The unbalance angle. With an asymmetric shaft, the 1X response depends on where the heavy spot sits from the stiff axis, not only on its size. Take Δ = 0.10, the supports' damping holding it stable, and the 20 g·mm unbalance. Along the stiff axis the disc's orbit peaks at 827 µm (1589 rpm), and along the soft axis at 848 µm (1576 rpm). At 45° between them it has two lower peaks, 201 µm at 1547 rpm and 195 µm at 1624 rpm. A round shaft peaks at 217 µm. So the response is no longer a complex number times the unbalance: turn the heavy spot by 90° and the response does not turn by 90°.

Balancing it. The response is still linear, but in the unbalance's two parts separately. A reading is

V = \alpha_+ U + \alpha_-\bar{U}

with Ū the complex conjugate of U, and α− = 0 on a round shaft. One trial weight cannot find two coefficients, so the single influence coefficient of Single-plane balancing is wrong. Two trials at right angles, T and iT, reading V1 and V2, find both:

\alpha_+ = \frac{(V_1 - V_0) - \mathrm{i}(V_2 - V_0)}{2T}, \alpha_- = \frac{(V_1 - V_0) + \mathrm{i}(V_2 - V_0)}{2\bar{T}}

The correction then solves α+Wc + α−W̄c = −ΔV, with ΔV = V0 − Vs. With its conjugate that is two equations for Wc and W̄c:

W_c = \frac{\alpha_-\overline{\Delta V} - \bar{\alpha}_+ \Delta V}{|\alpha_+|^2 - |\alpha_-|^2}

Take the rotor above (Δ = 0.10) with the heavy spot at 45°, read at the disc and balanced at 1200 rpm with a 0.5 g trial at 20 mm. One trial, at 0°, gives 1.18 g at 237°. The check run still reads 9 µm, and through the critical the orbit peaks at 283 µm, worse than the 201 µm before. Two trials, at 0° and 90°, give 1.00 g at 225°, exactly opposite the unbalance. The orbit then peaks at 0.7 µm, left by the slow roll's own small response at 100 rpm. (One trial does work when it happens to lie in line with the heavy spot, at the same angle or opposite: then Wc = −U exactly.) The Balancing tab takes one trial per plane, so the two-trial method is for working by hand.

How it is worked out. In axes turning with the shaft, on isotropic supports, the rotor is time-invariant, so its modes are exact. Each is seen from the bearings as a forward and a backward circle, at s ± iΩ, and is listed at the one with more kinetic energy. On anisotropic supports the modes come from Hill's method: the Floquet exponents of the periodic system, from its harmonics up to ±3 × 2Ω. The steady states (the unbalance's 1X, gravity's mean sag and 2X) come from harmonic balance. The turning stiffness ties each harmonic n to n ± 2. That closes at once on isotropic supports (1X stays 1X, and the sag gives a forward 2X), and is cut at ±6X otherwise. The time stepping splits each step: half a step of the rest (the exact update), the kick of the turning stiffness on the velocities at mid-step, and the other half step. It agrees with the harmonic balance to about 10−5.

A Floquet mode's frequency is defined only up to a multiple of the stiffness's harmonic spacing Ωs (2Ω here, Ω for a breathing crack). The same mode can be listed at s + inΩs for any whole n, and its ζ changes with the n chosen, but its growth rate, Re s, does not. So on the stability map of a shaft whose stiffness turns, the onset (ζ = 0) holds whichever harmonic is listed. Read the size of ζ away from zero as a growth rate.

The cracked shaft

A fatigue crack grows across a shaft from its surface, often where the bending stress is highest, at a shoulder or a shrink fit. Its depth from the surface is ac, up to half the shaft's diameter d.

A hinge. Where it is open, the crack lets the shaft's slope jump across it: Δθ = κMb under a bending moment Mb. Its compliance κ comes from fracture mechanics (Dimarogonas and Papadopoulos). Cut the crack into strips across its front, each an edge crack in a strip as tall as the shaft there, and add up the energy each releases as the crack grows, (1 − ν²)KI²/E. Bending that pulls the mouth open (the moment along the crack's front) loads every strip. Bending across it loads one half in tension and the other in compression. So the crack gives far more one way. For ac/d = 0.4 in the 10 mm shaft, κo = 1.45 × 10−4 rad/(N·m) opening it, and κa = 0.60 × 10−4 across.

The figure across counts the strips on the compressed half too, as though open. That holds only while the bending that opens the crack (on the rotor, the weight's) keeps the whole front in tension, the vibration's smaller bending only adding to it on one side and taking from it on the other.

On the shaft the hinge is condensed out exactly onto the stations. With the shaft whole, the stations' displacements q make a bending moment bhTq at the crack. Let the slope jump by Δθ there, and the shaft, resisting the jump with a stiffness kh, carries bhTq − khΔθ. The hinge carries the same moment, in series, so Δθ = κ(bhTq − khΔθ). Δθ has no mass of its own, so it follows q at once: Δθ = κbhTq/(1 + κkh). Put back, it takes

\frac{b_h b_h^{\mathsf{T}}\,\kappa}{1 + \kappa k_h}

off the stations' stiffness. A crack with no compliance (κ = 0) takes nothing, and a free hinge (κ → ∞) takes bhbhT/kh. Open, the crack makes an asymmetric shaft, its two axes along and across the mouth, softened.

Breathing. A real crack is shut most of the time, its faces pressed together, unless the bending stretches its side of the shaft. On a horizontal rotor the shaft's weight bends it far more than its vibration does (weight dominance, as in Mayes and Davies' model), so the weight sets it. It is fully open as its mouth turns to the side the sag stretches (the bottom, between the bearings), and shut half a turn later. With its mouth at φ + βc from +x, it is open by

\sigma(\varphi) = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - \sin(\varphi + \beta_c)\bigr)

That is 1 with the mouth pointing down (−90°), and 0 pointing up. Where the weight bends the shaft the other way, stretching its top (near and past the bearing of an overhung rotor), the sign flips: σ = (1 + sin(φ + βc))/2. That opening changes once per revolution, and it multiplies a stiffness that already changes twice per revolution. So the stiffness has 1X, 2X and 3X in it, not only the asymmetric shaft's 2X.

It changes even in axes turning with the shaft, because the weight does not turn. So the modes come from Hill's method, and the steady states from harmonic balance with every harmonic tied to its neighbours ±1, ±2 and ±3 (cut at ±6X). The time stepping kicks the velocities with the whole turning stiffness at each mid-step. On a vertical rotor nothing opens it: a breathing crack stays shut, invisible.

What it does. The Cracked shaft preset has a crack 40% through the shaft at the disc, laid horizontal. On average over a turn it takes 3.6% off the disc's stiffness along its mouth and 1.7% across, so the critical speed splits a little, to 1562 and 1574 rpm. The weight then drives three whirls the round shaft doesn't have:

  • A 2X, 37.6 µm at 783 rpm, half the critical speed, as the asymmetric shaft's.
  • A 3X, 18.6 µm at 522 rpm, a third of it. The asymmetric shaft has none, so a 3X peak at a third of the critical on a run-up points to a crack rather than to slots or a keyway.
  • A 1X: with no unbalance at all, the disc whirls 107 µm at the critical. It adds to the unbalance's, so the 1X vector changes as the crack grows. (On a machine, a slowly drifting 1X vector is the other warning sign.) With the heavy spot at the crack's mouth the disc whirls 164 µm at the critical, against 54 µm uncracked; with the mouth opposite it, 52 µm.

Held open, the crack has no 3X and twice the 2X, 75.3 µm at 775 rpm: it is then an asymmetric shaft. The 2X and 3X grow fast with the depth: at ac/d = 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 the 2X peaks at 9.5, 21.8, 37.6 and 51.5 µm.

5. Stability

Cross-coupling and internal damping

Everything so far is forced vibration. The unbalance drives the rotor, and however hard it shakes at a critical speed, every free whirl dies away. Two effects can instead make a free whirl grow by itself, and both are stiffnesses that are not symmetric.

Cross-coupled stiffness. In a fluid-film bearing (or a seal, or an impeller's clearance) the fluid is dragged round by the shaft. So pushing the journal in x raises the pressure ahead of it and pushes it in y too: fx = −kxyy, fy = +kxyx. On a circular forward orbit of radius rw, that force always points along the orbit, the way the journal moves. It does work on the whirl, 2πkxyrw² per cycle, where a damper c takes out 2πcωrw². So the forward modes lose damping and the backward ones gain it, and once kxy passes cω the forward mode grows. When all the damping is at the bearings, as here, and the orbits are circles, this balance is exact, with ω the whirl frequency at the onset.

As kxy rises, the forward mode's ζ falls, 0.046 → 0.033 → 0.019 → 0.004 at 0, 5,000, 10,000 and 15,000 N/m, and its frequency slips from 26.4 Hz to 26.13 Hz. The threshold with the defaults is kxy = 16,419 N/m at both bearings, exactly cω for their 100 N·s/m at 26.13 Hz.

Oil whirl and oil whip. In a real fluid-film bearing kxy is not constant. The oil film is dragged round at a fraction λ of the shaft's speed, so the bearing's damping c acts on the journal's motion relative to the swirling oil. Oil carried round at λΩ moves at λΩJq, with J turning x toward y, so

f = -c(\dot{q} - \lambda\Omega Jq) = -c\dot{q} + \lambda c\Omega Jq

The second term is fx = −λcΩy, fy = +λcΩx: a cross-coupling kxy = λcΩ that grows with the speed. The oil sticks to the turning journal and to the still bearing, so λ is about ½: exactly ½ in short-bearing theory, and just under it (about 0.42 to 0.48) in real plain bearings. If a bearing's dampers differ in x and y, the same algebra with the damping matrix C gives the cross-coupled stiffness −λΩCJ. The page takes kxy = λcΩ with c the mean of the two, which is the same for equal dampers.

The energy balance above then reads λcΩ against cω. The forward whirl at ω grows once λΩ > ω, the oil's swirl outrunning the whirl, whatever the damping. With λ = ½ the rotor goes unstable at twice its first natural frequency. The Oil swirl λ = ½ preset's onset is 3136 rpm, twice the forward whirl's 26.1 Hz, and just there the whirl is at exactly ½X. Faster, it stays locked at the natural frequency (26.3 Hz, 0.32×, at 5000 rpm, growing ×e every 0.22 s): oil whip, its ratio to the speed falling as the speed rises.

With a constant λ the whirl turns straight into whip at the onset. On real machines oil whirl often comes first: a limit cycle near 0.45X that follows the speed up, and locks into whip once it reaches the natural frequency. Raising the damping does not help, since it raises the cross-coupling with it. Real machines use bearings that break up the swirl (tilting pads, pressure dams), or lower λ with anti-swirl devices. A real oil film also stiffens as the journal moves off-centre, so the whirl settles to a limit cycle about the size of the clearance, where a linear model grows for ever. The page's journal bearings show both (The fluid-film bearing, below).

Internal (rotating) damping. Damping in the shaft itself (material hysteresis, friction in shrink fits, splines and couplings) resists the bending rate that the spinning shaft sees, not the one seen from the bearings. In a forward whirl at ω the shaft is bent toward the orbit's centre, and that bend turns at ω while the shaft turns at Ω. So, seen from the shaft, the bend moves at ω − Ω. Below the critical speed (ω > Ω) internal damping damps the whirl like any other damping. Above it the shaft turns faster than it whirls, and the bend moves backward through the shaft. The internal damping force then points forward along the orbit, and it drives the whirl.

That is the swirl's algebra again, with the whole shaft turning (λ = 1): the damper acts on q̇ − ΩJq, the bending rate seen from the shaft. In fixed coordinates it gives ordinary damping ciq̇ plus a cross-coupling kxy = ciΩ, the ΩH of the equations. On a forward circle at ω, an external damper ce and the ciq̇ take out 2π(ce + ci)ωrw² per cycle, and the cross-coupling puts in 2πciΩrw². The whirl grows once ceω + ci(ω − Ω) < 0. For the classic Jeffcott rotor on rigid bearings, with ce at the disc, the whirl is at ωn, so

\Omega_{onset} = \omega_n\Bigl(1 + \frac{c_e}{c_i}\Bigr)

It is always above the critical speed, and with no external damping exactly at it. (On flexible supports only the shaft's share of the motion is damped internally, so the onset rises further.) This was the cause of the supercritical whirl of early built-up rotors, studied by Kimball and Newkirk in the 1920s. The model makes the internal damping viscous, a force in proportion to the bending rate. Real material damping is closer to hysteretic, a fixed loss per cycle whatever the rate, and the onset speed depends on which is assumed.

With the defaults and ci = 20 N·s/m the rotor is stable up to 3,910 rpm. 10 N·s/m moves the onset up to 6,220 rpm, and 40 N·s/m down to 2,760 rpm. Below the critical, internal damping helps: at rest the first mode's ζ rises from 0.046 to 0.078 with ci = 20 N·s/m.

The stability map draws the damping ratio of each mode against speed, or its log decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²). The onset speed is where the first forward mode crosses zero. Industry practice (API 684, 2nd edition, 2005) asks for δ ≥ 0.1 at the running speed; the defaults' first mode has δ = 0.29. (The criterion is for the modes in and near the running range: the defaults' lightly damped conical mode, at 315 Hz, is far above it.)

Beyond the onset a tap, or just the unbalance's start-up transient, sets off a whirl that grows exponentially at the mode's own natural frequency, not at the speed: a subsynchronous whirl. With ci = 20 N·s/m at 5,000 rpm it is at 26.9 Hz (0.32×), growing ×e every 0.28 s, while the unbalance still turns at 83 Hz. No balancing removes it. The full spectrum transforms w = x + iy of the orbit, forward whirl at positive frequencies and backward at negative. The unbalance shows at +1X, and the instability as a growing line below it on the forward side. The model is linear, so the whirl would grow for ever. In a machine it grows until something rubs, or the bearing's nonlinearity holds it to a limit cycle.

The fluid-film bearing

A plain journal bearing has no balls or rollers. The journal turns in a sleeve a little larger than itself, and the gap, the radial clearance cr (a thousandth or two of the radius), is full of oil. Turning, the journal drags the oil round with it into the narrowing side of the gap. The oil, squeezed, rises in pressure and holds the journal off the sleeve. Reynolds' equation gives that pressure. In a bearing short beside its diameter (Lb/Db up to about ½) the oil escapes mostly out of the ends, and the pressure is a parabola along the bearing: short-bearing (Ocvirk) theory. Where the pressure would go below zero the oil cavitates instead, so only the half of the film that narrows carries load (the π-film).

At rest the journal lies on the bottom of its clearance. As the speed rises the film lifts it. Since the film's pressure peaks ahead of the thinnest point, it also pushes the journal sideways, in the direction of spin. The journal settles off the load line by the attitude angle χ, at an eccentricity ε, its offset over cr. The Sommerfeld number S = (ηNLbDb/W)(Rb/cr)², N in rev/s, and the bearing's proportions set ε. In short-bearing theory ε depends only on S(Lb/Db)², the modified Sommerfeld number: at S = 1, halving Lb moves ε from 0.32 to 0.60. The more speed and viscosity, or the less load, the larger S, and the closer the journal to the centre, at nearly 90° to the load. Every short bearing travels the same path, the locus, only at a different pace.

A plain journal bearing, its clearance much exaggerated. The load W pushes the journal down and the film pushes it sideways, the way it spins. It settles on the locus at the eccentricity ε, at the attitude angle χ from the load line. The film is thinnest along the line of centres.

The default bearing (Db = 10 mm, Lb = 5 mm, cr = 15 µm, oil of η = 30 mPa·s, 5 N) has ε = 0.80 at 100 rpm, 0.44 at 1,000 rpm and 0.22 at 3,000 rpm, its thinnest film 11.7 µm there.

For small motions about that point the film acts as springs and dampers, eight coefficients: f = −KΔq − CΔq̇, with K = (W/cr)K̄(ε) and C = (W/(crΩ))C̄(ε), K̄ and C̄ dimensionless. The film's sideways push makes K far from symmetric: at 3,000 rpm, kxy = 1.36 MN/m but kyx = −1.81 MN/m. Its skew part is the cross-coupling above. For the short bearing it is exactly kxy − kyx = ½Ω(cxx + cyy), at every load: the oil swirls at half the shaft's speed, the swirl ratio λ = ½. As the speed rises the cross-coupling grows with it, while the direct stiffness and damping level off.

So a rotor on such bearings has a threshold speed. Lund worked it out for a rigid rotor of mass mb per bearing. At the threshold it whirls at ωw, neither growing nor decaying, so s = iωw solves its characteristic equation. The real and imaginary parts give

m_b\omega_w^2 = K_{eq} = \frac{k_{xx}c_{yy} + k_{yy}c_{xx} - k_{xy}c_{yx} - k_{yx}c_{xy}}{c_{xx} + c_{yy}} \omega_w^2 = \frac{(K_{eq} - k_{xx})(K_{eq} - k_{yy}) - k_{xy}k_{yx}}{c_{xx}c_{yy} - c_{xy}c_{yx}}

A rotor heavier than Keq/ωw² is unstable. But the coefficients change with the speed, through ε, so the threshold has to be searched for. At each speed, find ε, the coefficients, Keq and ωw, and compare Keq/ωw² with mb; the Fluid-film bearing tab steps up the speed until mb exceeds it, then bisects. K scales as W/cr and C as W/(crΩ), so ωw/Ω depends on ε alone. For the default bearing, Keq/ωw² is 195 kg at 1,000 rpm (ε = 0.44, ωw = 0.52Ω) and 9.0 kg at 5,000 rpm (ε = 0.14, 0.51Ω). Lightly loaded, ωw tends to half the speed: oil whirl. With the default bearing carrying its load as weight (0.51 kg), the rotor goes unstable at 21,324 rpm and whirls at 0.50×. Heavily loaded bearings (ε above 0.756) are stable at any speed. That is a result of short-bearing theory with the π-film; other bearings and film models put the limit elsewhere. It is one reason a lightly loaded bearing is the one that whirls. A flexible rotor goes unstable much sooner, near twice its first critical speed: the oil whip above.

On the rotor. Each film carries its share of the rotor's weight. On a midspan disc that is half of it and its own journal's; on an overhung one the far bearing is pulled up instead. So the two may sit at different points of the locus. Each film is linearized about where its journal settles, f = W − Kf(q − qJ) − Cfq̇. Kf(Ω) and Cf(Ω) join the rotor's stiffness and damping, and the constant p = W + KfqJ joins its loads, so the rotor rests with its journals on the locus. Below the speed where ε = 0.9 the journal is nearly on its bottom (mixed lubrication), and the film is taken as it is there.

The Journal bearings preset is the default rotor laid horizontal on the default bearing. The film, 1 MN/m or so, is far stiffer than the shaft (38 kN/m at the disc), so the critical speed is close to the rigid-bearing one: 1845 rpm, against 1854 rpm on rigid bearings. The film damps it little (ζ = 0.007). The film is not isotropic, so the backward whirl crosses 1X too, at 1853 rpm, even more lightly damped (ζ = 0.002). The unbalance excites both, and through the pair the disc's orbit is an ellipse.

Linearized, nothing holds the response inside the clearance. At the critical the journals' steady 1X orbits reach about 28 µm (their half-length), almost twice the 15 µm clearance. A real journal would touch the bearing long before that, so read them as the linear model's prediction only.

A little below twice the critical, at 3576 rpm, the forward whirl grows at 30.5 Hz (0.51×). This is oil whip from the bearing itself, not from a chosen λ. It is locked at 30.7 Hz at 5000 rpm (0.37×), growing ×e every 0.85 s. Long before it reaches the size of the disc's whirl, it fills the 15 µm clearance. A vertical rotor's bearings carry no load, and an unloaded plain bearing has no direct stiffness, only the swirl's cross-coupling. On them the rotor whirls at any speed, which is why vertical machines use tilting-pad or preloaded bearings.

The whole film. The linearization holds for small motions about where the journal rests. Off there the film's force is far from linear: at ε → 1 the film between journal and wall goes to nothing, and its stiffness and damping grow without bound, so a journal never reaches the wall. The short bearing's whole force has a closed form too. The film carries pressure over half the bearing: the half where the gap, the oil dragged into it at half the speed and squeezed by the journal, closes. That half turns with the journal's velocity, set by its speed along the line of centres and how fast it whirls across it against Ω/2. Over it, with ϑ the angle from the line of centres, the force is integrals of sin²ϑ, sin ϑ cos ϑ and cos²ϑ over (1 − ε cos ϑ)³. In the angle ϑe with tan(ϑe/2) = √((1 + ε)/(1 − ε)) tan(ϑ/2), the eccentric anomaly of an orbit, each is a short trigonometric polynomial.

A journal circling the centre at exactly half the speed leaves the film nothing to squeeze or drag, and it carries no load at all. That is where oil whirl lives. The film pushes a slower whirl along and holds a faster one back, so past twice its natural frequency, where the rotor's whirl is slower than half the speed, the film feeds it. Tick Nonlinear film under the bearing supports and the time response takes the films' whole force (Numerics), while the Campbell diagram, stability and the steady-state curves stay those of the linearized films.

On the Journal bearings preset, run up through the critical at 500 rpm/s: linearized, the journals come within 1% of the wall (ε = 0.99); whole, the films hold them to ε = 0.63, while the disc's peak is much the same, 0.66 mm. Past the onset the films keep the journals in their clearance, but the shaft, far softer, bends on: its whirl grows ever more slowly, but does not stop, until it would rub. On the Oil whirl preset, upright, the whirl at 3500 rpm (below twice the first natural frequency) is at half the speed, 29.2 Hz. It settles to a limit cycle with the journals circling past ε = 0.9 and the disc about 0.16 mm off centre. At 5000 rpm it is locked at 30.8 Hz instead: oil whip, which grows on.

Active magnetic bearings

An active magnetic bearing holds the journal with no contact at all. On each axis two electromagnets face each other across the journal, each an air gap h0 from it. Across a gap h, a magnet carrying a current i pulls with Λi²/h², Λ = μ0Nc²Ap/4 for Nc turns and pole faces of area Ap (μ0 the permeability of free space), so the nearer the journal, the harder it pulls. Both coils carry a bias current i0, and the control current ic is added to one and taken from the other. For small motions the pair's force is

f = k_i\,i_c + k_m x, k_i = \frac{4\Lambda\,i_0}{h_0^2}, \qquad k_m = \frac{4\Lambda\,i_0^2}{h_0^3}

km is a negative stiffness: left alone, the journal falls onto a magnet. The force can reach kii0 = kmh0, where one magnet's current runs out: that is the bearing's load capacity. The default bearing (h0 = 1 mm, i0 = 1 A, Ap = 400 mm², Nc = 200) has km = 20.1 kN/m, ki = 20.1 N/A and a capacity of 20.1 N.

A sensor reads x, and a controller sets ic = −Gc(s)x through a power amplifier: Gc(s) = (kp + kI/s + kds/(1 + s/ωlp))/(1 + s/ωa), a PID with its derivative filtered at ωlp and the amplifier's bandwidth ωa. The bearing then pushes back with −Kmb(s)x, Kmb = kiGc(s) − km. The proportional gain has to beat the magnets first: kikp − km is what is left as stiffness, 50.3 kN/m by default, and the derivative gives kikd = 101 N·s/m of damping. The integral makes the stiffness infinite at 0 Hz: under a steady load the current keeps growing until the journal is back at the centre. On a horizontal rotor the journals stay centred and the shaft alone sags.

So the bearing's stiffness and damping are not constants but depend on the frequency. At ω, Kmb(iω) = kmb + iωcmb: the spring and damper that would push the same. Where the controller leads the motion, cmb > 0. The integral lags it at low frequency, and the derivative's filter and the amplifier lag it at high frequency, so cmb is positive only in a band: 14.1 to 1297 Hz for the default bearing. Outside it the bearing feeds a mode. A mode that sits out there unstable, or moves out there as the speed changes, grows.

That is how a controller's phase lag destabilizes a rotor. A gyroscopic rotor's forward modes rise with the speed. On the Magnetic bearings preset (the disc overhung past B, the amplifier slowed to 400 Hz, which brings the band's top down to 739 Hz) the disc's forward tilting mode reaches 739 Hz at 8894 rpm, 4.99× the speed, and is unstable past it: at 12,000 rpm it whirls at 778 Hz and grows ×e every 0.41 s. The onset is exactly where the mode crosses the band's edge. A faster amplifier (1000 Hz) keeps the rotor stable to 20,000 rpm; a slower one (300 Hz) leaves the mode outside the band even at rest. Backward modes fall with the speed, toward the integral's lag at the bottom.

The controller has states of its own (the integral, the filter, the current), so the rotor's state grows past [q, q̇] by three per axis. The time stepping is still the exact update, with them in A; the steady states take Kmb at each harmonic. The model is linearized about the centred journal and the bias current, with the sensor at the journal. A real bearing also saturates (the current cannot pass i0 one way), and its rotor drops onto backup bearings, at half the air gap, when control is lost. The page pauses there.

6. Rub

Rotor–stator rub

Seals and casings sit close round a rotor. A ring round the disc, a radial clearance δc larger than it, does nothing until the disc's centre moves δc off the ring's centre. Past that, by δp, the ring pushes the disc back toward its centre with FN = kringδp + cringδ̇p, never pulling (kring and cring are the ring's mount; the rim and the ring are rigid), and its friction drags the rim against its slip over the ring with μFN. The slip is the rim's speed RdΩ, Rd the disc's radius, plus the disc's centre moving along the ring, vt:

f = -F_N\hat{n} - \mu F_N\,\mathrm{sat}(R_d\Omega + v_t)\,\hat{t}, \mathrm{sat}(v) = \frac{v}{\sqrt{v^2 + v_0^2}}

n̂ points out from the ring's centre to the disc's, t̂ along the ring in the direction of spin, and v0 = 1 cm/s smooths Coulomb's step at zero slip, so that a rim rolling on the ring is held there by a steep drag rather than a jump. The force is not linear in anything, so the ring is in the time response only: the Campbell diagram, the stability map and the steady-state curves are the rotor's without it.

Partial rub. A ring offset from the rotor, or a rotor sagging toward its ring, is reached on one side only, and the disc bounces off it. Each hit is a kick, so the orbit's spectrum has harmonics of the speed. Above twice a critical speed the disc's free whirl, kicked once a revolution, can lock to every other kick instead, so the orbit closes every two revolutions: ½X, and further up ⅓X, the rub's signature sub-harmonics. On the Partial rub preset (horizontal, a 385 µm seal round a disc that sags 378 µm) the disc brushes the ring's bottom once a turn above the critical and every other turn past about 4000 rpm: at 4400 rpm the full spectrum's tallest line is ½X, 36.7 Hz.

Full rub and backward whirl. Held against the ring all round, the disc whirls forward with the unbalance, and the ring is a spring kfull = kring(1 − δc/rd) at the disc on an orbit of radius rd. Its friction acts along t̂: −μkfull, a cross-coupled stiffness, and since the rim slips forward over the ring (RdΩ is far faster than the whirl) it pushes the disc backward round the ring. Enough of it flips the whirl backward. The disc then whirls at its frequency on the ring, √((k + kring)/m), k the rotor's own stiffness at the disc, far above the speed, and the friction keeps feeding it until the rim rolls round the ring without slipping: |ω|rd = RdΩ. So the orbit's radius is RdΩ/|ω|, set by the speed and not by the clearance: dry whirl, the most violent rub there is. On the Full rub preset (a 30 µm ring, μ = 0.3) the whirl flips at 1545 rpm, to 227 Hz backward, and at 2000 rpm the orbit is 7.3 mm, the ring pushed back with 15 kN.

The Newkirk effect: a rub that bows the shaft

The rub's friction heats the shaft where it touches, and a shaft hotter on one side grows longer there and bows toward it. Newkirk (1926) saw what follows: below the critical speed the disc's high spot is close to its heavy spot, so the rub heats the high spot, the bow pushes the high spot further out, the rub gets harder, and the vibration grows without limit. Here the heat is the drag's work on the slip, Ph = μFN(RdΩ + vt), taken on the shaft where it touches (from the mark, turning with it), and the bow δT at the disc follows it with a first-order lag:

\tau_T\dot{\delta}_T = a_T P_h - \delta_T

aT is the bow per watt once it has settled, τT how slowly it does. The bow loads the shaft as a residual bow does (section 3), turning with it. Because the rotor's response lags the bow a little, each turn of the loop moves the high spot a little behind the hot spot: the 1X vector grows and turns against the spin, its lag growing, the Newkirk spiral. Above the critical the rotor's response to the bow is nearly opposite to it: the bow pulls the high spot away from the hot spot, the rub eases, and it heals itself. So the effect depends on which side of the critical the rotor runs, not on how hard it rubs. On the Newkirk effect preset (a seal 15 µm round the disc, set 10 µm low, aT = 120 µm/W, τT = 5 s, so much faster than a real shaft's minutes) the 1X at 1300 rpm, 0.82 of the critical, grows from 10 µm past 100 µm in 15 s, its lag from 13° to 40°. Run at 3200 rpm, the bow built going through the critical fades to 2.4 µm, and the disc runs at 5.9 µm.

The ring is drawn round the disc in the 3D view and on its orbit. A ring needs a round shaft, with the oil films linearized.

7. Numerics

Why state space

Real modes and modal superposition need symmetric M and K, damping in proportion to them, and modes that do not change with the speed. Here ΩG is skew-symmetric and changes with the speed, and the support damping is not proportional. So the modes are complex and depend on the speed. The first-order form Ż = A(Ω)Z + Bu handles all of it. The eigenvalues of A give each mode's frequency, damping and whirl direction, and the time response is stepped exactly.

As on the other pages, each step is the exact first-order-hold matrix-exponential update, Zk+1 = ΦZk + Γ0uk + Γ1uk+1, with Φ = eAΔt for a step Δt. It is stable however stiff the shaft. While the speed ramps, A(Ω) is updated every 2 ms of simulated time, and the unbalance force follows the exact angle φ, including the Ω̇ term. The ramp's gyroscopic term IpΩ̇θ is left out: at 5000 rpm/s it is about 0.03% of the disc's tilt stiffness.

Stepping the whole film

The films' whole force is not linear, so no one Φ steps it. Each step is still the exact update, of the films linearized, with the rest of their force, the remainder rf, as a load on the journals, linear over the step as the loads are:

Z_{k+1} = \Phi Z_k + \Gamma_0(u_k + r_{f,k}) + \Gamma_1(u_{k+1} + r_{f,k+1})

rf,k+1 depends on Zk+1, so it is found by Newton's method. It has to be: a film damps its journal in microseconds, far faster than the step. That speed is also the trap. If the exact update carried the films' damping at the journals' rest, the remainder near the wall, many times stiffer, would make the update ring from step to step and throw the journal through the wall. So the update carries Cb instead: the damping of the whole film, uncavitated, where the journal is. The film's damping is a sum of positive terms over the half carrying pressure, so it is never more than Cb, whatever the velocity. The remainder is then never stiffer than the update, and the step stays second order in Δt. As the journal moves round, Cb is taken afresh, and Φ with it.

The film's force is also minus the gradient, in the journal's velocity, of its dissipation Ψ, which is convex. So each step's journal velocities are where a convex function is least, and a Newton step that does not lower it is cut back. That keeps Newton right where the film's force bends sharply: at the half-speed whirl, which oil whirl circles. The step need only resolve the spin and the force on the journals, so it is coarser than the linear films' (10 steps a period of the fastest mode), but each costs a hundred times more. Past what a frame can compute, the page runs slower than the speed set.

Stepping the rub

The ring's force goes in as the films' remainder does: a load on the disc, linear over the step, Zk+1 = ΦZk + Γ0(uk + rk) + Γ1(uk+1 + rk+1), with rk the ring's force at step k and rk+1 found by Newton's method, since it depends on Zk+1. That is the trapezoidal rule on the ring's force: a rim rolling on the ring is held there by a drag of μFN/v0, hundreds of kN·s/m in dry whirl, which would ring an explicit step. The step also resolves the disc bouncing on the ring, √((k + kring)/m), as well as the rotor's own modes. Checked against the full rub's steady state above (a frictionless one and one with μ = 0.1, to a part in 106) and against a step four times finer. The steps run in Rust, at well under a microsecond each, so even a full rub runs in real time.

Keeping a shaft with mass fast

Every beam element adds modes. The highest are at tens of kHz, far above anything the rotor does at these speeds, but the time stepping would still have to resolve them. So the model is reduced the way the Substructuring page reduces a substructure (Craig–Bampton). The stations' DOFs are kept as they are. The rest of the shaft moves in the static shape they give it, which is all a massless shaft ever does, plus its lowest modes with the stations held, up to 1000 Hz. Those modes' amplitudes ξ join q, after the stations. The reduced model's modes are within 0.2% of the full element model's below 500 Hz, and within 1% up to 1000 Hz (60,000 rpm, the top of the speed range). With a massless shaft there are no such modes, and the reduction is exactly the Jeffcott model.

  • Spin splits each mode into a forward and a backward whirl (the gyroscopic effect), so the critical speeds are where the 1X line crosses the Campbell diagram's forward branches.
  • Balancing measures the 1X vector with and without a trial weight; the influence coefficients then give the weight that cancels it, plane by plane.
  • Gravity only moves the equilibrium on a round shaft, but on an asymmetric or cracked one it drives 2X, with a peak at half a critical.
  • Cross-coupled stiffness (oil films, seals, internal damping) feeds the forward whirl and sets an onset speed above which the rotor is unstable, whatever its balance.
