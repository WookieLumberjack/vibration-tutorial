The S–N curve

A part loaded with constant-amplitude cycles of stress range S fails after N(S) cycles, and over the range that matters a straight line in log–log does, Basquin's law basquin:

N S^b = K

Welded steel details have b ≈ 3 (a detail category is the range they survive for two million cycles); smooth machined parts 5 to 10. A steep curve means the largest cycles do almost all the damage: a range 10 % higher does 1.1b times the damage.

Many steels also have an endurance limit: below some range a smooth specimen lasts for ever under constant amplitude. A random load blurs it, because the large cycles start a crack that the small ones then grow, so design codes bend the curve instead of ending it. Eurocode 3's curves en1993-1-9 run at b = 3 from the detail category SC at 2 × 10⁶ cycles to the constant-amplitude limit at 5 × 10⁶, then at b = 5 to a cut-off at 10⁸, below which a range does no damage. The knee and the cut-off follow from the slopes:

S_D = (2/5)^{1/3} S_C = 0.737\,S_C S_L = (5/100)^{1/5} S_D = 0.549\,S_D

Miner's rule

Under cycles of many ranges, each cycle at S uses up 1/N(S) of the life, and failure is predicted when the damage

D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i}

reaches 1 (Palmgren's rule palmgren, made known by Miner miner). It ignores the order of the cycles and is only good to a factor of two or so in practice, but it is what every code uses.

Mean stress

A cycle that swings about a tensile mean Sm does more damage than one of the same amplitude Sa about zero: tension opens the crack. S–N curves are measured fully reversed, so a cycle with a mean is converted to the fully reversed amplitude Sar that does the same damage. On a plot of amplitude against mean (Haigh's diagram), the cycles of equal life lie on a line from Sar at zero mean to the ultimate strength Su at zero amplitude: straight in Goodman's form goodman, a parabola in Gerber's gerber.

\text{Goodman: } S_{ar} = \frac{S_a}{1 - S_m/S_u} \text{Gerber: } S_{ar} = \frac{S_a}{1 - (S_m/S_u)^2}

Goodman is the safer of the two for steels, Gerber nearer to tests on ductile metals. A compressive mean helps, but the corrections are usually not given credit for it. Welded joints are the exception that sets the codes: the weld's residual stress is near yield, so the real mean is high whatever the load's, and their S–N curves are on the range alone.

Notches

A hole, a fillet or a thread concentrates the nominal stress by its elastic stress concentration factor Kt, from tables or a finite-element model peterson. Fatigue feels a little less: a sharp notch's peak is over so small a volume that its gradient helps, so the fatigue notch factor is

K_f = 1 + q\,(K_t - 1)

with the notch sensitivity q between 0 (none) and 1 (all of it), larger for strong steels and blunt notches. The local stress range is Kf times the nominal one, so the damage goes up Kfb times. A welded detail's category already holds its notch.

Rainflow counting

A random stress isn't made of cycles, so they have to be found. Rainflow counting (Matsuishi and Endo matsuishi-endo) pairs each reversal with the one that closes a hysteresis loop with it: the small ripples riding on a large swing become small cycles, and the large swing is still counted at its full range. The three-point form astm-e1049: keep a stack of reversals; when the latest range X is at least the one before, Y, then Y is a cycle (half a cycle if it holds the start) and its two points go. What is left at the end counts as half cycles.

Damage from the PSD

Without a record, everything has to come from the stress PSD G(f) through its moments mk, as on the Random vibration page: the RMS σ = √m0, the up-crossings ν0 = √(m2/m0) and the peaks νp = √(m4/m2) a second, and the irregularity factor αi = ν0/νp (often written α2): 1 when every peak is followed by a zero crossing (a narrow band), smaller when ripples ride on the swings. Over a time T the damage is the number of cycles times the mean damage of one, E[Sb]/K.

Narrow band bendat-1964: each up-crossing is one cycle whose amplitude Sa is Rayleigh in σ, so the range S = 2Sa and E[Sab] = (√2σ)bΓ(1 + b/2), with Γ the gamma function:

\frac{D}{T} = \frac{\nu_0\,(2\sqrt2\,\sigma)^b\,\Gamma(1 + b/2)}{K}

It is exact for a narrow band; for a broad one it counts every ripple as a full swing and is conservative.

Dirlik's range distribution dirlik is an empirical fit to rainflow counts of many simulated processes: in Z = S/2σ, an exponential and two Rayleigh terms whose weights D1, D2, D3 and scales Q, R come from m0, m1, m2 and m4. Then D/T = νpE[Sb]/K over it. It is usually within 10 % of rainflow on the record, and the method most codes now recommend.

\frac{D}{T} = \frac{\nu_0\,(2\sqrt2\,\sigma)^b\,\Gamma(1 + b/2)}{K}

The record

The page synthesizes a Gaussian record with the PSD (each line with a random phase) and counts it. A long record is what the spectral methods stand for; a short one scatters from seed to seed, because a few large cycles carry most of the damage.

The fatigue damage spectrum

To compare two vibration environments (a road and a test, a flight and a shaker), feed each to a row of single oscillators, one at each natural frequency fn with the same damping ζ, and work out the fatigue damage each would take. That curve against fn is the fatigue damage spectrum (FDS) lalanne-4, as the shock response spectrum is for peaks. With Miles' RMS and the narrow-band damage, an oscillator under a base PSD Ga accumulates

D(f_n) \propto f_n\,T\,\Bigl(\frac{G_a(f_n)}{\zeta f_n^3}\Bigr)^{b/2}

(its stress taken in proportion to its relative displacement). A test is fair if its FDS covers the service one's at every fn. Because the damage goes as T Gb/2, a test can be shortened by raising its level: equal damage needs

\frac{T_t}{T_s} = \Bigl(\frac{G_s}{G_t}\Bigr)^{b/2}

so with b = 8, a test a tenth as long as service needs the PSD 102/8 = 1.78 times as high, 2.5 dB. The steeper the S–N curve, the less level buys time: it is only fair while the higher level doesn't cause failures that service never would.

Worked example

The page opens on one 2 Hz mode with ζ = 2 %, 20 MPa RMS, on a welded detail: b = 3 and an 80 MPa range at two million cycles, so K = 2 × 10⁶ × 80³ = 1.024 × 10¹² MPa³. The moments give ν0 = 1.99 and νp = 2.09 a second, αi = 0.95: a narrow band. The narrow-band damage rate is

\frac{D}{T} = \frac{1.99 \times (2\sqrt2 \times 20)^3 \times \Gamma(2.5)}{1.024\times 10^{12}} = \frac{1.99 \times 181\,000 \times 1.329}{1.024\times 10^{12}}

4.68 × 10⁻⁷ a second, a life of 1/(4.68 × 10⁻⁷) = 2.13 × 10⁶ s, 24.7 days. Dirlik's gives 25.1 days, and the 600 s record's rainflow count 25.1 days too: the narrow-band formula is 1.4 % pessimistic, as it should be on a nearly narrow band.

If the detail had a hole in it with Kt = 2.5 and q = 0.8, Kf = 2.2 and the life would fall 2.23 = 10.6 times, to 2.3 days. A smooth part with a mean of 200 MPa, an amplitude of 100 MPa and Su = 600 MPa needs an S–N curve's fully reversed 150 MPa by Goodman, 112.5 by Gerber.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the stress, the curve and the record (Things to try, top left).

  • Basquin's S–N curve, NSb = K, makes damage go as the stress to the power b: the RMS must be right.
  • Miner's rule adds n/N over the cycles; rainflow counting finds the cycles in a random record.
  • A tensile mean, a notch and the knee of a two-slope curve all change the damage before any counting does.
  • The narrow-band formula is exact for one narrow mode and conservative for broad or multi-modal stress; Dirlik's formula handles any band.
  • The fatigue damage spectrum compares environments by the damage they do; equal damage trades time for level as TGb/2.
