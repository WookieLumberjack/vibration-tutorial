Why substructure?

Real structures are built from components: an engine on a frame, a wing on a fuselage, a payload on a launcher. Dynamic substructuring models each component on its own, reduces it to a handful of coordinates, and then joins the reduced components at their interfaces de-klerk. The assembled model is much smaller than the full one, and a component can be changed or re-analysed without touching the others. Component mode synthesis (CMS) does the reduction with each component's own mode shapes.

The page starts with the whole chain as one substructure, A, whose only boundary DOF is the loaded tip. That is the classic single-component reduction: learn the basis, the kept modes and the constraint mode on it first.

Then click a mass under Interfaces at to cut the chain there into two substructures, A (grounded) and B (free end). The interface mass belongs to both: A owns its mass and the springs to its left, B owns the springs to its right. (The free-interface methods split the interface mass half and half instead, so that each substructure has mass wherever it can move.) Click more masses to cut the chain into three or more substructures, A, B, C, ... from the ground: each interface is shared by the two on either side of it, and each substructure keeps its own number of modes.

The Method selector chooses how each substructure is reduced: Craig–Bampton (fixed interface, the next few sections) or Rubin and MacNeal (free interface, from Free-interface methods on). The Compare methods tab runs all three on the same cut.

What is a basis? (the key idea)

Every method on this page, and modal analysis itself, rests on one idea: describing the motion with a different set of shapes. It is worth getting this clear before looking at any Craig–Bampton matrix.

Coordinates are amplitudes of shapes

A displacement of the chain is a vector x of N numbers. Any such vector can be built as a sum of N independent basis vectors (shapes) v1 … vN, each multiplied by an amplitude cj:

x = v_1 c_1 + v_2 c_2 + \dots = V c, \qquad V = [\,v_1\ \ v_2\ \cdots\,]

The amplitudes c are the coordinates in that basis. The physical DOFs are just one choice: V = I, where shape j is "move mass j by 1 m, hold all the others". Nothing makes that choice special. It is convenient for building M and K, but usually a poor way to describe the motion, because every mass moves in every mode.

An everyday analogy: a point on a map can be given as (east, north) or as (distance, bearing) along a road. Same point, different coordinates. And a vibrating guitar string is naturally described by the amplitudes of its sine-shaped harmonics, not by the displacement of each point.

What a change of basis does to the equations

Substitute x = Vc into Mẍ + Cẋ + Kx = f and premultiply by VT:

(V^{\mathsf{T}} M V)\,\ddot c + (V^{\mathsf{T}} C V)\,\dot c + (V^{\mathsf{T}} K V)\,c = V^{\mathsf{T}} f

Why VT? Energy. The kinetic energy is ½ẋTMẋ = ½ċT(VTMV)ċ and the strain energy is ½cT(VTKV)c, so the new matrices describe the same energies in the new coordinates. VTf is the work the force does through each shape (a generalized force). This is also the principle of virtual work, or a Galerkin projection.

That gives a practical way to read any transformed matrix:

  • Diagonal entry (VTKV)jj = vjTKvj: twice the strain energy of shape j at unit amplitude, a "generalized stiffness".
  • Off-diagonal entry vjTKvk: how much shapes j and k interact elastically. Zero means they are stiffness-orthogonal: they can move independently without exchanging strain energy. The same holds for M (inertia coupling) and C.

Modal analysis is already a change of basis

Choosing V = Φ, the system's own mass-normalized mode shapes, is the best-known example. Those shapes happen to be orthogonal through both M and K, so VTMV = I and VTKV = diag(ω2): every off-diagonal term vanishes and the equations uncouple. That is why modal coordinates are so useful, not a coincidence of the solver.

A small example (2 masses, 1 kg and 400 N/m each)

K = [800 −400; −400 400], M = I. True frequencies: 1.967 and 5.150 Hz.

  1. A full, different basis. Take v1 = [1, 1] ("move together") and v2 = [1, −1] ("move apart"). Then VTKV = [400 400; 400 2000] and VTMV = [2 0; 0 2]. The matrices look nothing like K and M, and the off-diagonal 400 says the two shapes are coupled, yet solving gives exactly 1.967 and 5.150 Hz. A square (complete) basis only rewrites the problem; it cannot change the answer.
  2. Truncate it. Keep only v1: one equation, ω2 = 400/2, f = 2.25 Hz, 14% high. The masses are now forced to move together, which is a constraint, so the structure is stiffer and the frequency can only go up (the Rayleigh quotient, ω2 = vTKv/vTMv).
  3. Choose a better shape. Use the static deflection under a tip load instead, v = [0.5, 1]: ω2 = 200/1.25, f = 2.01 Hz, only 2.3% high with a single coordinate. (The true mode is [0.618, 1].) A static shape captures most of the low-frequency motion. That is exactly why Craig–Bampton uses static constraint modes.

Reduction = a tall basis

When V has fewer columns than rows (N × m, m < N), x = Vc restricts the motion to combinations of m shapes: the model can only move along those "rails". Everything then depends on whether the true motion lies (nearly) inside that set of shapes. The MAC in the comparison table measures exactly that for each mode. Adding shapes can only enlarge the set, which is why the frequencies converge from above ritz.

The Craig–Bampton basis, and why it is chosen this way

Inside a component, any motion can be split into two parts:

  • Motion forced by the boundary. If the boundary moves slowly, the interior simply follows statically. The constraint modes Ψ describe this exactly: column j is the static shape when boundary DOF j moves by 1 and the others are held.
  • The component's own vibration relative to that. With the boundary held, what is left is a free vibration of the clamped component, so it is described by its fixed-interface modes Φ, lowest first, since low-frequency loading excites them most.

Together they are complete: keep every fixed-interface mode and T is square and invertible, so nothing is lost. The design has three further consequences:

  • The boundary coordinates stay physical. Every fixed-interface mode is zero on the boundary, and each constraint mode is 1 on one boundary DOF and 0 on the others. So the coordinate multiplying constraint mode j is the displacement xb,j. This is what makes assembly trivial: two components are joined simply by giving their interface the same xb. Modal coordinates of two separate components cannot be joined like that.
  • K̂ is block diagonal. The constraint modes are static, so they do no elastic work against the fixed-interface modes: φT(KiiΨ + Kib) = 0. The two families are stiffness-orthogonal.
  • M̂ is not. They are not mass-orthogonal: when the boundary accelerates, the inertia of the interior drives the component's modes. M̂qb is that inertial coupling, and it is how the components talk to each other dynamically. (For C, see Damping under Properties worth knowing.)

The Basis tab plots every column of the current T as a shape along the chain. For the 8-mass chain of Things to try, cut at m4 with one mode each, there are four: A's first clamped mode (over m1–m3, zero elsewhere), B's first clamped mode (over m5–m7), a "tent" for x4 (rising linearly from the ground to m4, falling to m8, the static shape of both components when the interface moves) and a ramp for x8 (zero up to m4, rising to the tip). Every motion the reduced model can make is a combination of those four shapes.

Boundary (master) and interior DOFs

  • Boundary DOFs xb are kept as physical displacements. Here they are the interface masses, each shared by the substructures either side of it, and the last mass of the chain, where the force is applied.
  • Interior DOFs xi are every other mass. Each belongs to exactly one substructure and is replaced by a few modal coordinates q.

Why is the force limited to the last mass? A force on a boundary DOF enters the reduced model unchanged (f̂ = TTf = [0; fb]) and the response there is read directly, so any difference from the full model is caused by the reduction and not by how the load was projected. A force on an interior DOF is allowed in Craig–Bampton, but it only reaches the model through the kept modes and the constraint modes. In practice, the DOFs that are loaded, measured or connected to other components are made masters.

Step by step

The Matrices tab shows every one of these steps with the current numbers.

  1. Partition each component into interior and boundary DOFs (for lumped masses Mib = 0): \begin{bmatrix} M_{ii} & M_{ib} \\ M_{bi} & M_{bb} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \ddot x_i \\ \ddot x_b \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} K_{ii} & K_{ib} \\ K_{bi} & K_{bb} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ x_b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} f_i \\ f_b \end{bmatrix}
  2. Fixed-interface normal modes. Clamp the boundary (xb = 0) and solve Kiiφ = ω2Miiφ. Keep the k lowest, mass-normalized, as Φk. These capture the component's own dynamics.
  3. Constraint modes. Move one boundary DOF by 1 m, hold the others, and let the interior find its static equilibrium: Ψ = −Kii−1Kib. One column per boundary DOF. They capture how the component deforms when its boundary moves, including rigid-body motion.
  4. Transform. xi = Φkq + Ψxb, so \begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ x_b \end{bmatrix} = T \begin{bmatrix} q \\ x_b \end{bmatrix}, \quad T = \begin{bmatrix} \Phi_k & \Psi \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat M = T^{\mathsf{T}} M T and K̂ = TTKT. Because Ψ is a static solution, the stiffness decouples: \hat K_{qq} = \Lambda_k = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_r^2), \qquad \hat K_{qb} = 0 \hat K_{bb} = K_{bb} - K_{bi} K_{ii}^{-1} K_{ib} K̂bb is the component's static stiffness seen from its boundary (a Schur complement, the same as Guyan condensation). The mass keeps a coupling term: \hat M_{qq} = I, \qquad \hat M_{qb} = \Phi_k^{\mathsf{T}}(M_{ii}\Psi + M_{ib}) \hat M_{bb} = M_{bb} + M_{bi}\Psi + \Psi^{\mathsf{T}} M_{ib} + \Psi^{\mathsf{T}} M_{ii} \Psi
  5. Assemble. The reduced components share their boundary DOFs: the interface x is the same coordinate in A and B, so their boundary entries add, exactly as element matrices add in the original assembly. Compatibility (A and B move together at the interface) is automatic. The modal coordinates stay private to their component.
  6. Solve and recover. Solve K̂η = ω2M̂η for the reduced model, then recover the physical shape x = Tη for comparison with the full model's modes (frequency error and MAC).

Hurty and Craig–Bampton

Hurty introduced component mode synthesis hurty. He described each component's motion with rigid-body modes, "redundant" constraint modes and fixed-constraint normal modes, treating the statically determinate boundary DOFs differently from the redundant ones. Craig and Bampton simplified this craig-bampton: treat every boundary DOF the same way, with one constraint mode each. Rigid-body motion is already contained in the constraint modes, so no separate treatment is needed. The result is the method used here, often called Hurty/Craig–Bampton, and still the industry standard (for example for coupled-loads analysis of spacecraft on launchers).

Other CMS families use free-interface component modes instead (MacNeal, Rubin, Craig–Chang), which are closer to what a modal test measures but need residual-flexibility corrections. They are next.

Free-interface methods: MacNeal and Rubin

Why a free interface?

Craig–Bampton clamps each component's boundary. That is easy in a computer and hard in a laboratory: nobody can hold a satellite's interface ring perfectly still while shaking it. What a modal test measures is a component hanging freely (on soft bungees), so its free-interface modes. They also belong to the component alone, not to how it will be held, so a supplier can deliver them without knowing the rest of the structure.

Free modes alone converge badly

Solve Kφ = ω2Mφ for the whole substructure, boundary free, and describe its motion with the lowest few free modes (Hou's hou and Goldman's goldman methods). The trouble is at the interface. In the assembled structure the neighbour pushes on the interface, and the local static deflection under that push is spread over all the free modes, mostly the high ones that were thrown away. A truncated set of free modes is therefore far too stiff at exactly the place the components meet.

The fix: residual flexibility

The discarded modes are high in frequency, so under the interface forces fb they respond almost statically. Their static response is kept even though the modes are not:

x = \Phi_k q + G_d E_b f_b G_d = \sum_{\text{discarded}} \frac{\varphi_r \varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}}}{\omega_r^2} = K^{-1} - \Phi_k \Lambda_k^{-1} \Phi_k^{\mathsf{T}}

Gd is the residual flexibility: the flexibility of the component minus the part the kept modes already describe. The second form needs no discarded modes at all, which is how it is done in practice (from K−1, or from a static test). Its columns at the boundary, GdEb, are residual attachment modes: the static deflection under a unit force at one boundary DOF, less what the kept modes carry. They are stiffness-orthogonal to the kept modes: ΦkTKGd = 0.

Making the boundary physical again

Forces are awkward coordinates for joining components. The boundary rows of the equation above give xb = Φbkq + Gbbfb, so fb = Gbb−1(xb − Φbkq). Substituting back:

T = \begin{bmatrix} \Phi_{ik} - R\,\Phi_{bk} & R \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}, \qquad R = G_{ib} G_{bb}^{-1}

This has exactly the shape of the Craig–Bampton T: modal columns that vanish on the boundary, and boundary columns that are 1 at their own DOF and 0 at the others. So the same assembly works: shared xb, entries added. (This form is Craig and Chang's craig-chang.) The stiffness is no longer block diagonal:

\hat K = \begin{bmatrix} \Lambda_k + \Phi_{bk}^{\mathsf{T}} G_{bb}^{-1} \Phi_{bk} & -\Phi_{bk}^{\mathsf{T}} G_{bb}^{-1} \\ -G_{bb}^{-1} \Phi_{bk} & G_{bb}^{-1} \end{bmatrix}

Gbb−1 is the residual stiffness seen from the boundary: a spring from each boundary DOF to the modal motion.

MacNeal and Rubin

  • MacNeal macneal treats the residual flexibility as a massless spring: only the kept modes carry inertia, M̂ = [I 0; 0 0]. The boundary DOFs then have no mass: they follow the modes statically and are condensed out, so the model has only as many modes as modes kept. Dropping mass raises frequencies and adding the residual flexibility lowers them, so there is no bound, and the method is not exact even with every mode there is room for: the boundary DOFs take up places in the model, so some modes are always discarded, and their mass with them.
  • Rubin rubin adds the residual mass: the inertia of the residual attachment modes. Here this is done as a full Rayleigh–Ritz projection, M̂ = TTMT (the Craig–Chang form of Rubin's method). It has all of Craig–Bampton's guarantees: upper bounds that fall as modes are added, and exact with every mode kept.

Damping is not part of either original method. Here both project it like the stiffness, Ĉ = TTCT, so the two differ only in the residual mass.

Rigid-body modes

B is joined to the chain only through the interface, so on its own it floats: its first free mode is a rigid-body mode at 0 Hz, moving every mass by the same amount. It has infinite flexibility (K is singular, K−1 does not exist), so it cannot be part of Gd and must always be kept, which is why B's spin box starts at 1. In practice Gd of a floating component comes from inertia relief: the flexibility of the component held statically determinate, with the rigid-body part projected out. On this page Gd is built directly from the discarded elastic modes, which gives the same matrix.

Back expansion (data recovery)

The coupled model does not contain the interior

In practice a substructure is reduced once, often by someone else (a supplier, another team), and delivered as its reduced matrices M̂, Ĉ, K̂. The system-level analysis couples it with other models on the boundary DOFs and solves for the reduced coordinates r = [q, xb] only. But the questions asked afterwards are usually about the inside of the substructure: how far does this interior point move, how hard is this spring or bolt loaded? Answering them means going back from r to the interior DOFs: back expansion, or data recovery.

Recovery from the coupled solution

Each substructure recovers its own interior from its rows of T, using only its own q and its own boundary DOFs:

\text{Craig–Bampton:}\quad x_i = \Psi x_b + \Phi_k q \text{Rubin, MacNeal:}\quad x_i = R\,x_b + (\Phi_{ik} - R\,\Phi_{bk})\,q

For Craig–Bampton the two parts have a clear meaning: Ψxb is the static shape the boundary drags the interior into, and Φkq is the interior's own vibration relative to it. The matrix [Φk Ψ] is what a supplier delivers with the reduced model as an output transformation matrix (OTM), so the system analyst can recover the interior without the full model. Recovered spring forces come the same way: a spring's force is a fixed combination of displacements, ke(xe − xe−1), so it too is a matrix times r (a displacement-based loads transformation matrix, LTM).

The recovery can be no better than the reduced model, and it inherits the truncation: the discarded modes are not in q, so their motion is missing from xi. Forces are differences of displacements, which weight the higher, shorter-wavelength modes more, so a recovered force is usually less accurate than the displacements it comes from.

Boundary only (static recovery)

If only xb is kept from the system run (or the substructure was Guyan-reduced), the best static guess is xi = −Kii−1Kibxb. For Craig–Bampton that is exactly the Ψxb part: comparing the two shows what the modal coordinates q contribute. It is right for slow loading and misses every resonance of the interior.

Enhanced recovery

Knowing xb(t), the substructure can be solved again on its own, with its boundary motion imposed as a base excitation and with all its interior DOFs:

M_{ii}\ddot x_i + C_{ii}\dot x_i + K_{ii} x_i = -K_{ib} x_b - C_{ib}\dot x_b - M_{ib}\ddot x_b

(Mib = 0 here: the masses are lumped). This puts back the dynamics of the modes that were truncated, driven by the boundary motion the coupled model predicted. Its only remaining error is the error in xb itself, so it is exact whenever xb is, whatever was truncated inside. It costs a second, local analysis per substructure, which is cheap next to the system run.

It is not always better, though. The interior now responds to xb with its own fixed-interface resonances, so near one of them a small error in xb is amplified by the resonance, and the enhanced recovery can be worse than the coupled one. Try a light interior: 8 masses, m1 to m3 set to 0.05 kg, the interface at m4, no modes in A and 1 in B, driven at A's first fixed-interface frequency (10.9 Hz). In A the enhanced recovery of x1 is about 27% off against 6% for the coupled one; in B, where nothing resonates, it is within 1% against 7%.

Properties worth knowing

  • No modes kept = Guyan reduction. Only the constraint modes remain, so the model is exact statically (at 0 Hz) and good for low modes when little mass is "hidden" in the interior. Guyan introduced it as static condensation guyan: keep a set of master DOFs and let the rest follow statically. Finite-element codes still offer it, and it is the starting point that Craig–Bampton improves on.
  • All modes kept = exact. T is then square and invertible: nothing is thrown away, the problem is only rewritten in new coordinates.
  • Upper bounds. Craig–Bampton is a Rayleigh–Ritz method: it restricts the motion to a subspace, which can only make the structure stiffer. Every Craig–Bampton frequency is ≥ the true one, and the error shrinks monotonically as modes are added.
  • Rule of thumb. Keep fixed-interface modes up to about 1.5–2 times the highest frequency of interest. The Matrices tab lists each component's fixed-interface frequencies, kept and discarded.
  • Damping. The component modes come from K and M only; the damping is then projected onto them, Ĉ = TTCT, and assembled like K̂. K̂ is block diagonal because Ψ is a static solution for K. That is not true for C, so Ĉ in general couples the kept modes to each other and to the boundary. Only damping proportional to stiffness inside a component (C = βK) keeps Ĉ block diagonal. In practice the modal block is often replaced by measured or assumed modal damping, Ĉqq = diag(2ζrωr), with joint damping on the boundary. Step 9 of the Matrices tab compares the reduced model's exact damping ratios with the true ones.
  • In time. The Time response tab steps the reduced model beside the full one under the same tip force. All three methods here are exact statically for a load on a boundary DOF: the boundary columns of T (constraint modes, or residual attachment modes) are static solutions. So a slowly varying load is reproduced, and the error lives in the vibration: in the ringing after a step, and above all near a resonance. A resonant peak is only about 2ζfn wide, so a frequency error of a few per cent there is a response error of tens of per cent, in amplitude and in phase.
  • One substructure. With no cut, Craig–Bampton keeps the chain's modes with the tip held plus one constraint mode, the static shape for a tip displacement. With no modes kept that is Guyan reduction to the tip alone: one DOF, one frequency. The free-interface methods are different: the free-interface modes of the only substructure are the chain's own modes, so every kept mode is exact, and the residual flexibility adds the static response of the discarded ones (modal truncation with a static correction).
  • More substructures. Each extra cut adds one boundary DOF that stays in the model whatever is kept, in exchange for smaller components with fewer, lower modes each. For the same model size, fewer interfaces and more modes are usually more accurate; many interfaces pay off when components are analysed or changed separately, or when their own modes are cheap to find.
  • Interface size. Every boundary DOF stays in the model. On this chain an interface is one DOF; on a 3D finite-element model it can be thousands, which is why interface reduction methods exist.

Worked example

The page opens on the Simulation page's four 1 kg masses on 400 N/m springs, uncut, by Craig–Bampton with one mode kept: its only boundary DOF is the tip, x4, and its interior is m1 to m3. Held at the tip, the interior is a three-mass chain fixed at both ends, whose first mode is

\omega_1 = 2\sqrt{k/m}\,\sin\frac{\pi}{8} = 40 \times 0.383 = 15.3\ \text{rad/s} \quad (2.44\ \text{Hz})

with the mass-normalized shape φ = [0.5, 0.707, 0.5]. The constraint mode is the straight line from the ground to the tip, Ψ = [0.25, 0.5, 0.75]. Guyan (no modes) keeps only that: the four springs in series, K̂bb = 400/4 = 100 N/m, and the mass the line carries, M̂bb = 1 + 0.252 + 0.52 + 0.752 = 1.875 kg, so f = √(100/1.875)/2π = 1.162 Hz, against the chain's 1.105 Hz: 5.1 % high. Keeping the mode adds its stiffness ω12 = 234 and the inertial coupling

\hat M_{qb} = \varphi^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi = 0.5 \times 0.25 + 0.707 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.75 = 0.854

and the 2 × 2 model, K̂ = diag(234, 100) and M̂ = [1 0.854; 0.854 1.875], gives 1.108 Hz (0.19 % high) and 3.27 Hz against 3.18 Hz (2.7 % high, MAC 0.96). Both are above the truth, as Rayleigh–Ritz promises; keep 2 modes and they fall to 0.017 % and 0.15 %, and all 3 make it exact.
