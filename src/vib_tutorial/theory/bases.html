Coordinates and coupling Lagrange's equations
  • see two coupled masses trade their energy back and forth, and time it;
  • turn the basis until the coupling vanishes, and find the modes there;
  • keep one arrow, a reduced basis, and see why its frequency is never too low, and nearly right near a mode.

    This page builds on Deriving the equations, where the car's coupling changed with the coordinates it was written in. Here the system is as small as coupling allows, two masses, and the coordinates are arrows you drag while it runs. Changing the basis this way is the move behind modal coordinates, Rayleigh–Ritz models and the reduced models of whole structures.

    Two masses, one point

    Two masses sit between two walls, each tied to its wall by a spring and to the other by a third, the coupling spring k2, with a damper beside each spring:

    Each mass's free-body diagram (or Lagrange's equations) gives Mẍ + Cẋ + Kx = 0 with

    M = \begin{bmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad K = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 & -k_2 \\ -k_2 & k_2 + k_3 \end{bmatrix}

    and C like K, with c for each spring. Its configuration, both displacements at once, is one point x = (x1, x2) in a plane, and the page draws it there as it moves. Pluck one mass and the point traces a curve that fills a box: the sum of two sines at frequencies that aren't in a whole-number ratio, a Lissajous figure lissajous.

    A basis: two arrows

    The axes x1 and x2 are one way to say where the point is. Any two arrows v1 and v2 that don't lie along one line do as well: every point is some amount of each,

    x = q_1 v_1 + q_2 v_2 = V q, \qquad V = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & v_2 \end{bmatrix}

    and the numbers q1, q2 are the point's coordinates in that basis. On the plane, the dashed parallelogram from the point to the two arrows' lines shows them. Put x = Vq into the equations and multiply by Vᵀ strang:

    (V^\mathsf{T} M V)\,\ddot{q} + (V^\mathsf{T} C V)\,\dot{q} + (V^\mathsf{T} K V)\,q = 0

    Why Vᵀ, and not V−1? Because the energies say so: the kinetic energy ½ẋᵀMẋ = ½q̇ᵀ(VᵀMV)q̇ and the potential ½xᵀKx = ½qᵀ(VᵀKV)q, so these are the matrices Lagrange's equations give in q. They stay symmetric, and each stays positive.

    Coupling belongs to the description

    The off-diagonal terms are the coupling: through VᵀKV a coordinate's displacement pushes on the other (static coupling), through VᵀMV its acceleration does (dynamic coupling). Turn the arrows, and the coupling changes. In the masses' own displacements (the arrows along the axes) the coupling is the spring between them, −k2, and the masses' matrix is diagonal. With unequal masses and k1 = k3, the arrows along (1, 1) and (1, −1) make VᵀKV diagonal instead, and VᵀMV couples. Most arrows give both.

    But the motion doesn't change, and so neither do its frequencies:

    \det(V^\mathsf{T} K V - \omega^2 V^\mathsf{T} M V) = (\det V)^2 \det(K - \omega^2 M)

    The roots ωr are the same in every basis. The page works them out from the matrices beside the plane, and they never move however the arrows turn. Only when the arrows lie along one line (det V = 0) does the description fail: then most points can't be reached, as the page says.

    The modes: the basis that uncouples

    There is one pair of directions in which both matrices are diagonal at once. Along them the equations come apart into two single oscillators, and each coordinate moves on its own, a single sine. Those directions are the modes, the solutions of

    K v = \omega^2 M v

    For two modes r ≠ s, multiply Kvr = ωr²Mvr by vsᵀ, swap them, and subtract: since K and M are symmetric, (ωr² − ωs²) vsᵀMvr = 0. So distinct modes are orthogonal through M, vsᵀMvr = 0, and through K too. That is exactly the two off-diagonal terms vanishing thomson. It isn't the same as being at right angles in the plane: with m1 = m2 the modes are perpendicular, but with m2 = 2m1 they are 80.1° apart. Drag an arrow near a mode and it snaps onto it; snap both, and both matrices show zero off the diagonal.

    When the system is symmetric (m1 = m2, k1 = k3), symmetry fixes the modes at ±45° whatever the coupling spring: in phase, the coupling spring isn't stretched, ω1² = k1/m; opposed, it's stretched twice as much on each mass, ω2² = (k1 + 2k2)/m. Unequal masses or walls turn the modes, and then the coupling spring turns them too: a stiffer one pulls mode 1 toward moving together.

    The energy along each arrow

    The arrows split the motion into two pieces, q1v1 and q2v2, moving with velocities q̇1v1 and q̇2v2. Each piece alone has its own energy, E1 and E2. But the energy of a sum isn't the sum of the energies. It's like the length of a sum of two vectors, |a + b|² = |a|² + |b|² + 2a·b: there is a cross term, zero only when a and b are at right angles. Energy is a square too, ½ẋᵀMẋ + ½xᵀKx, so

    E = E_1 + E_2 + \tilde m_{12}\dot q_1 \dot q_2 + \tilde k_{12} q_1 q_2 E_i = \tfrac12 \tilde m_{ii}\dot q_i^2 + \tfrac12 \tilde k_{ii} q_i^2

    with m̃ = VᵀMV and k̃ = VᵀKV. The last two terms are the cross term, and they are made of exactly the off-diagonal terms of the matrices beside the plane: the coupling. The Coupling tab draws the three as they run (E1 as "along v1", E2 as "along v2"), and under them adds them up as bars, each stacked on the last, with the stored energy beside them. The stored energy stays put; the pieces don't. The cross term swings above and below zero, and as it does, energy passes from the motion along one arrow to the motion along the other: pluck one mass and the energy drains from its motion into the other's and back, the beat. In the modes the cross term is zero, the pieces are "at right angles" as the energy measures them (that is what orthogonal through M and K means), and the energy in each mode stays there forever (less what the dampers take). Change the arrows and the tab redraws the past too, in the new coordinates: the same motion, described again.

    One arrow: a reduced basis, and Rayleigh's quotient

    A model of a real structure has thousands of coordinates, and nobody solves it in all of them. Instead one picks a few arrows, shapes the structure is expected to move in, and keeps only those: x = Vq with V taller than it is wide. That is a reduced basis, and the frequencies it gives are only approximate. How far off, and which way? With two masses the only reduced basis is a single arrow, so the page lets you try it: tick One arrow and v2 goes. Now x = q1v1, the point can only slide along v1's line, and the matrices are single numbers. The masses are one oscillator, with mass v1ᵀMv1 and stiffness v1ᵀKv1, so they swing at

    \omega^2 = R(v) = \frac{v^\mathsf{T} K v}{v^\mathsf{T} M v}

    Rayleigh's quotient rayleigh-sound of the arrow. Held to one arrow, the motion is always one pure sine, whatever the arrow: one coordinate can only swing at one frequency. That is the approximation, and the contrast to watch for. Untick One arrow and pluck along the same line: unless it is a mode, the point leaves the line, and the coordinates' traces beat. So one arrow forces a shape on the motion, and Rayleigh's quotient is the frequency that forced shape swings at. Only a mode swings in one shape, as one pure sine, without being held to it.

    You have met the quotient already: with two arrows, each pair of diagonal terms of VᵀKV and VᵀMV is one arrow's vᵀKv and vᵀMv, so R(vi) is what coordinate i would do if it were kept alone, and the page prints it under the matrices. In a coupled basis it isn't one of the system's frequencies, because keeping one arrow leaves the coupling out. Along a mode nothing is left out, and it is.

    So how wrong is one arrow? Try directions on the One arrow tab, each landing its measured frequency at its angle, and three things show:

    1. It never comes in low. No direction swings slower than mode 1, nor faster than mode 2.
    2. The lowest is mode 1 itself, where the answer is exact.
    3. Near mode 1 it hardly matters. The curve is flat at its bottom: an arrow several degrees off mode 1 gives a frequency a fraction of a percent high.

    Why: write v in the modes, v = a1φ1 + a2φ2, with φr the modes' arrows. Their orthogonality leaves

    R = \frac{a_1^2 \mu_1 \omega_1^2 + a_2^2 \mu_2 \omega_2^2}{a_1^2 \mu_1 + a_2^2 \mu_2}, \qquad \mu_r = \varphi_r^\mathsf{T} M \varphi_r

    a weighted average of ω1² and ω2², so it lies between them (1), equals ω1² only when a2 = 0 (2), and an arrow a little off mode 1, a2 small beside a1, errs only by (a2/a1)² (3). Show the theory draws R through your points.

    The conclusion, and the reason it's on this page: a reduced basis holds the system to fewer shapes than it has, which can only stiffen it, so its lowest frequency is never below the true one, and a reasonable guess at the shape gives a frequency very nearly right. More arrows can only bring it down toward the truth. This is the Rayleigh–Ritz method, and it's how large models are solved: in a few good shapes, not in all their coordinates. The modes are the basis where it's exact. Later pages (energy methods, modal coupling, substructuring) build their reduced models this way.

    The arrow can be kept, turned and given back its partner as it runs. Each is a physical event: what holds the masses to the line pushes only across it, so the momentum along it, vᵀMẋ, is kept, and the point's motion across the line is taken away. Ticked, the point is moved to the nearest place on the line; turned, it's carried round at the same distance from the middle. Each costs or adds energy, which the Energy tab books as your hand's work on the masses. Unticked, they run on free from where they were, with nothing lost.

    Changing it as it runs

    The grips on the masses and springs (and the boxes) change them while it runs, and the motion carries on by the physics, as on the Single oscillator: a spring changed at a stretch books ½xᵀΔKx; a heavier mass catches its extra at rest and keeps its momentum; a lighter one drops a piece at its speed. A dashed line on the time plot marks each change. The page steps the motion exactly, by the matrix exponential, a hundred times a period of the faster mode, so a stiff spring can't make it unstable.

    A worked example: the page as it opens

    The page opens on two 1 kg masses, 400 N/m to each wall and 100 N/m between, undamped, at rest, the arrows along the axes. Drag the point to (50, 0) mm on the plane, so only m1 is moved, and let go.

    1. The matrices. K has 500 N/m on its diagonal and −100 N/m off it, M is 1 kg on its diagonal: with the arrows along the axes these are VᵀKV and VᵀMV, coupled through the springs.
    2. The modes. In phase, (1, 1): ω1² = 400, f1 = 3.183 Hz. Opposed, (1, −1): ω2² = 600, f2 = 3.898 Hz. These are the two frequencies under the matrices, whatever the arrows.
    3. The pluck in the modes. (50, 0) mm is 25 mm of each mode, so x1 = 25(cos ω1t + cos ω2t) mm and x2 = 25(cos ω1t − cos ω2t) mm. The two drift in and out of step every 1/(f2 − f1) = 1.398 s: after 0.699 s, half of that, m1 has nearly stopped and m2 swings with the energy. The Coupling tab shows it pass across and back.
    4. One arrow along x₁. v = (1, 0): vᵀKv = 500 N/m and vᵀMv = 1 kg, so R = 500 and 3.559 Hz, 11.8 % above f1. These are the matrices' top-left terms in step 1: q1 kept alone. Turn it to 38.7°, v = (1, 0.8): vᵀKv = 500 − 160 + 320 = 660 N/m and vᵀMv = 1.64 kg, so R = 402.4 and 3.193 Hz. That's 6.3° off mode 1 and only 0.3 % high. At 45° it's mode 1, 3.183 Hz, the least one arrow can give.

    Things to try

    Setups to come back to (Things to try, under the boxes). Once you've handled the masses, some ask you to predict what you'll see first.

  • A two-mass system's configuration is a point in a plane; a basis is any two arrows there, x = Vq.
  • In a basis the matrices are VᵀMV and VᵀKV; their off-diagonal terms, the coupling, depend on the basis, but the frequencies don't.
  • Along the modes both matrices are diagonal: the modes are orthogonal through M and K, not always at right angles.
  • Coupled coordinates trade energy (the beat); uncoupled ones keep theirs.
  • With fewer arrows than masses, a reduced basis, the frequency is Rayleigh's quotient vᵀKv/vᵀMv: never below ω1², exact along mode 1, and nearly right near it. That's why reduced models (Rayleigh–Ritz) work, and why their frequencies err high.
