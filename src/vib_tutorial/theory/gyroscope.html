A rigid body turning

A body turning about a fixed point has angular momentum L = Iω, with I its inertia tensor about the point, and gravity's moment τ about the point changes it: dL/dt = τ (in fixed axes). In the body's principal axes I is diagonal, (I₁, I₂, I₃), but those axes turn, so L's rate in them gains ω × L:

I_1\dot\omega_1 + (I_3 - I_2)\,\omega_2\omega_3 = \tau_1, I_2\dot\omega_2 + (I_1 - I_3)\,\omega_3\omega_1 = \tau_2, I_3\dot\omega_3 + (I_2 - I_1)\,\omega_1\omega_2 = \tau_3,

Euler's equations euler-1765. A symmetric top has I₁ = I₂, so the third reads I₃ω̇₃ = τ₃, and gravity's moment, across the axis, leaves ω₃ (the spin) constant.

The top here is a disc of mass m and radius r on a light stem, its centre a height l above the tip: I₃ = mr²/2 along the axis, and I₁ = m(r²/4 + l²) across it about the tip (parallel axes, as for the Pendulums page's rod).

A disc isn't the only symmetric top. A square plate (Shape) looks the same after a quarter turn, so its moments about two lines across its axis a quarter turn apart are equal, and so (I being a tensor, its moment about a line at angle β is Ix cos²β + Iy sin²β) are its moments about every line across its axis: ms²/12 for a side s, and I₃ = ms²/6 along it (perpendicular axes: I₃ is the two across it together, for any flat plate). That is a disc's mr²/4 and mr²/2 for r = s/√3, so a square moves exactly as that disc does, corners and all; so does any regular polygon. A rectangle doesn't: its two moments across the axis differ, I₁ ≠ I₂, and it is an asymmetric top, without ω₃ constant or the closed forms below.

Without a moment, L stays put and the axis turns round it at |L|/I₁ (in a cone), while the body turns about its own axis at (1/I₃ − 1/I₁)L₃ besides: the free top's wobble.

The asymmetric top

A rectangular plate w × b (Shape) has three different moments about the tip: I₁ = m(b²/12 + l²), I₂ = m(w²/12 + l²) and I₃ = m(w² + b²)/12. With I₁ ≠ I₂, Euler's third equation keeps a term, I₃ω̇₃ = (I₁ − I₂)ω₁ω₂, so the spin isn't kept, and everything below that rests on it goes: the effective potential, the turning points, the nutation period, steady precession. What's left is the energy and L·ẑ. With φ left out (L·ẑ), the top has two degrees of freedom and one quantity kept: too few to solve it. Lagrange's top (I₁ = I₂) and Kovalevskaya's (I₁ = I₂ = 2I₃, the centre of mass in the plane kovalevskaya) are the only heavy tops that can be solved in general.

Without gravity it can still be solved, in Jacobi's elliptic functions jacobi-1850 (Euler–Poinsot poinsot). With the moments sorted Ia < Ib < Ic, the energy E and |L| = M, two of ω's components go as cn(λt, k) and sn(λt, k) and the third as dn(λt, k), with

\lambda^2 = \frac{(I_c - I_b)(M^2 - 2EI_a)}{I_aI_bI_c}, k^2 = \frac{(I_b - I_a)(2EI_c - M^2)}{(I_c - I_b)(M^2 - 2EI_a)}

(a and c traded if M² < 2EIb), coming back every 4K(k)/λ. Spun about its largest or smallest axis, it wobbles round it. Spun about its middle axis, k² is near 1 and the spin is unstable: the top flips over and back, again and again, the tennis-racket effect ashbaugh (or Dzhanibekov's, after the cosmonaut who saw a wing nut do it in orbit). The orientation, not only ω, needs one integral more.

With gravity there is no closed form at all, and the motion can be chaotic: two tops let go a hair apart drift apart exponentially, until they have nothing in common. The Chaos tab shows it two ways. A Poincaré section poincare-celeste marks the spin angle ψ and ω₃ each time the tilt peaks: with L·ẑ and the energy kept, that is the whole state there. Regular motion draws closed curves (a symmetric top, which keeps ω₃, one line); chaos scatters dots over an area. Below it, a second top let go 10⁻⁸ rad further over: regular motion drifts from it in proportion to the time, chaos multiplies the gap over and over.

Euler angles, and why the simulation doesn't use them

Three angles place the axis and the body round it: the precession φ about the vertical, the tilt θ from the vertical, and the spin ψ about the axis (the figure above). Then

\omega_3 = \dot\psi + \dot\varphi\cos\theta,

and the axis's own rates are θ̇ and φ̇ sin θ. Upright (θ = 0), φ and ψ turn about the same axis: one of the three angles is lost, the angle rates' equations divide by sin θ, and they blow up (gimbal lock). A sleeping top sits right there.

A unit quaternion hamilton q = (cos ½χ, n̂ sin ½χ), a turn by χ about n̂, has no such place: turns compose by multiplying quaternions, and nothing divides by zero. The simulation keeps the orientation as one and L in fixed axes.

Lagrange's top

The energy in the angles gives Lagrange's function lagrange

\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}I_1(\dot\theta^2 + \dot\varphi^2\sin^2\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}I_3(\dot\psi + \dot\varphi\cos\theta)^2 - mgl\cos\theta.

Neither φ nor ψ appears in it, so their momenta are constant: pψ = I₃ω₃, the angular momentum about the axis, and pφ = I₁φ̇ sin²θ + I₃ω₃ cos θ, about the vertical (gravity's moment, horizontal, changes neither). So does the energy. Taking φ̇ from pφ, the tilt is left on its own,

\tfrac{1}{2}I_1\dot\theta^2 + U(\theta) = E', U(\theta) = \frac{(p_\varphi - p_\psi\cos\theta)^2}{2I_1\sin^2\theta} + mgl\cos\theta,

E′ the energy less the spin's ½I₃ω₃². It is a one-mass problem in an effective potential, as the Pendulums page's large swing is in its V(θ), and the tilt moves between the two angles where U = E′ (the Potential tab), the turning points.

Steady precession

Held at one tilt (θ̈ = 0), the tilt's equation leaves

I_1\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 - I_3\omega_3\,\dot\varphi + mgl = 0,

so a top can precess steadily at either of two rates. For a fast top, the slow one is about mgl/I₃ω₃: gravity's moment mgl sin θ turns the angular momentum's horizontal part I₃ω₃ sin θ round, at a rate falling as the spin rises. The fast one is about I₃ω₃/I₁ cos θ, the free wobble, gravity hardly in it. Lying flat (cos θ = 0) the slow one is exactly mgl/I₃ω₃ and the fast one is gone: the bicycle wheel held by one end of its axle. The two meet, and then neither is left, where the quadratic's discriminant vanishes, at

I_3\omega_3 = 2\sqrt{I_1\,mgl\cos\theta}:

a slower top can't precess steadily at that tilt (the Precession tab). The precession always turns L toward the moment, so a top spun the other way precesses the other way.

Nutation

Let go at any other rate, the tilt nods between the turning points, nutation. With u = cos θ, the energy is

\dot u^2 = f(u) = (\varepsilon - \gamma u)(1 - u^2) - (a_\varphi - a_\psi u)^2,

with aψ = pψ/I₁, aφ = pφ/I₁, ε = 2E′/I₁ and γ = 2mgl/I₁: a cubic, whose roots u₁ < u₂ in [−1, 1] are the turning points and u₃ > 1 the third. A nod and back takes

T = \frac{4K(k)}{\sqrt{\gamma(u_3 - u_1)}}, \qquad k^2 = \frac{u_2 - u_1}{u_3 - u_1},

K the complete elliptic integral, as in the Pendulums page's large swing. A fast top let go at θ₀ nods about 2I₁mgl sin θ₀/(I₃ω₃)² deep at about I₃ω₃/I₁: the faster it spins, the shallower and quicker. As it nods it precesses at

\dot\varphi = \frac{a_\varphi - a_\psi\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta},

so the end of its axis (the Path tab) traces

  • cusps if φ̇ = 0 at a turning point: let go without a push, it stops at the top of each nod;
  • loops if φ̇ changes sign between them: pushed back, it runs backward at one;
  • waves if it doesn't;
  • a circle if the two turning points meet: steady precession.

Without gravity the top is free: L stays put, and the axis turns round it at |L|/I₁ on a cone, so its tilt from the vertical nods unless L is vertical. Nutation is that wobble, which gravity's precession carries round.

The sleeping top

Upright, aφ = aψ, and for small θ

U(\theta) \approx mgl + \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\frac{I_3^2\omega_3^2}{4I_1} - mgl\Bigr)\theta^2:

a minimum, so upright is stable, if

I_3^2\omega_3^2 > 4I_1\,mgl, \qquad \omega_3 > \frac{2\sqrt{I_1\,mgl}}{I_3}.

A top spinning faster stands still upright, asleep; nudged, it nods a little round upright. Slower, U has a maximum there, and it falls until it comes back up. Friction at the tip slows a real top, which wakes, then wobbles, then falls. (The cup holds this top's tip; on a table, a round tip rolls and slides, which is what makes a top rise to sleep, and a tippe top turn over: see On a table.)

On a table: a round tip

Out of its cup the top stands on a table (Setting). Its underside is a sphere of radius R whose centre is a below the centre of mass G on the axis: a disc on a round tip has a = l − R, and a tippe top, a ball weighted low, a < 0 (α = −a/R).

It touches the table at P, R below the sphere's centre, so G stands at R + a cos θ, and P slips at vP = vG + ω × (P − G). The table pushes up with N, whatever keeps G at that height, and drags with Coulomb's friction coulomb μN against the slip; G moves under the two and gravity, and L about G under their moment. Neither of the cup's momenta is kept now (friction's moment is neither horizontal nor across the axis), and energy leaks out at F·vP.

Where friction can stop the slip, the tip rolls, and loses nothing. A fast top rolling at tilt θ carries G round at ω₃R sin θ while it precesses at about mga/I₃ω₃, which takes a sideways force m²gaR sin θ/I₃. Friction gives μmg at most, so it rolls below sin θ = μI₃/maR, whatever the spin, and slides above.

Jellett's constant: the rising top and the tippe top

One thing survives the friction: Jellett's constant jellett

J = \mathbf{L}\cdot(a\,\mathbf{e}_3 + R\,\hat{z}) = -\mathbf{L}\cdot(P - G).

The contact force's moment about G is (P − G) × F, at right angles to P − G, and ae₃ + Rẑ turns only as e₃ does, at ω × e₃, with L·(ω × e₃) = 0 for a symmetric top. So friction takes energy at a fixed J, and the least energy the top can have at tilt θ with that J (L along I(ae₃ + Rẑ), nothing else moving) is

E_J(\theta) = \frac{J^2}{2D(\theta)} + mg(R + a\cos\theta), D = I_1(a^2 + R^2 + 2aR\cos\theta) + (I_3 - I_1)(a + R\cos\theta)^2,

I₁ now about G. As friction takes energy out, the top sinks onto EJ and toward its least (the Potential tab). Upright EJ(0) = J²/2I₃(a + R)² + mg(R + a); turned over EJ(π) = J²/2I₃(R − a)² + mg(R − a).

  • A disc on a round tip (a > 0) spun fast rises to sleep hugenholtz, at J/I₃(a + R): upright is EJ's least above ω₃² = mga(a + R)²/R(I₃(a + R) − I₁R). It rises only while it slides, so it stalls where its tip starts to roll, and on a rough table hardly rises at all. With R = 0 the J² term no longer depends on the tilt: a sharp tip can't rise.
  • A tippe top (a < 0) turned over has G higher, but its spin J/I₃(R − a) is slower. Spun fast enough, turned over is EJ's least, and upright isn't, if
1 - \alpha < I_1/I_3 < 1 + \alpha:

then it turns over and spins on its top (a real one on its stem, which this ball leaves out), the same way seen from above but the other way about its own axis. Outside that range it stays up, or ends on its side, wherever EJ is least.

The simulation

The simulation steps the whole body: q and L. Each step of h is split: gravity's moment over half a step (L += ½hτ, τ = −mgl e₃ × ẑ: exact, since τ depends only on where the axis points, which doesn't change in it), the free top over the whole step (exact: L fixed, the axis turned round it by |L|h/I₁ and the body about its axis by (1/I₃ − 1/I₁)L₃h), and gravity's other half. This splitting is symplectic, as the Numerical integration page's central difference is: the energy's error stays bounded rather than drifting, and falls as h². Both momenta are kept to round-off, since the moment is at right angles to the vertical and to the axis. The step resolves the free wobble with 200 steps a turn. The Check tab compares the run with the closed forms.

An asymmetric top's free motion splits further: its energy is the symmetric top's with I₁ across both axes, plus (1/I₂ − 1/I₁)L₂²/2, whose flow just turns the body about its own y axis by (1/I₂ − 1/I₁)L₂h (L₂ = L·e₂, which that turn keeps). So each step is gravity's half, that turn's half, the symmetric top's whole step, that turn's other half and gravity's: still exact flows, still symplectic and second order, L·ẑ still kept to round-off. Without the extra turn it is the symmetric top's step exactly.

On a table nothing can be split off exactly and friction takes energy, so the state (the quaternion, ω and vG in space, G's place, and friction's work) is stepped by the classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta step instead: 64 steps a turn of the spin or of the wobble about G, shorter where friction, smoothed below 5 mm/s, would make the step unstable. J and the energy with friction's work hold to about 10⁻⁷ (the Check tab). The tip stays on the table: the run stops if the table would have to pull it down (it would hop), or a disc's rim touches it.

From the top to the rotor

A disc on a spinning shaft is a gyroscope too: as the shaft whirls, the disc's axis tilts, and turning its angular momentum (its polar moment of inertia times the shaft's speed) takes a moment. Its two whirl modes are the top's two precessions: a forward whirl, stiffened by the spin, its tilting frequency rising toward the speed times its polar moment over its diametral one, the fast precession's I₃ω₃/I₁; and a backward one, softened, falling as the slow precession does. The Jeffcott rotor page's Campbell diagram plots them against the speed (see its The gyroscopic effect), later in the course.

Worked example

The page opens on a 0.5 kg disc 40 mm in radius, 30 mm up its stem, spun at 1500 rpm and let go at 30° without a push.

  1. The top. I₃ = 0.5 × 0.04²/2 = 4.0 kg·cm², I₁ = 0.5 × (0.04²/4 + 0.03²) = 6.5 kg·cm², mgl = 0.5 × 9.81 × 0.03 = 0.14715 N·m, and ω₃ = 1500 × 2π/60 = 157.08 rad/s.
  2. It can sleep above 2√(6.5×10⁻⁴ × 0.14715)/4.0×10⁻⁴ = 48.9 rad/s, 467 rpm.
  3. Steady precession. I₃ω₃ = 0.06283 N·m·s and I₁ cos 30° = 5.629×10⁻⁴, so the quadratic's roots are 2.39 and 109 rad/s, against the estimates 0.14715/0.06283 = 2.34 and 0.06283/5.629×10⁻⁴ = 112 rad/s.
  4. Nutation. Let go at rest, aψ = 0.06283/6.5×10⁻⁴ = 96.7 /s, aφ = aψ cos 30° = 83.7 /s and γ = 2 × 0.14715/6.5×10⁻⁴ = 452.8 /s². The page's cubic has roots u₁ = 0.8528, u₂ = 0.8660 (cos 30°, where it was let go) and u₃ = 19.78: it nods between 30° and 31.48°. k² = 0.01321/18.93 = 6.98×10⁻⁴, so K = 1.5711 and T = 4 × 1.5711/√(452.8 × 18.93) = 67.9 ms: 14.7 nods a second.
  5. The fast top's estimates. 2 × 6.5×10⁻⁴ × 0.14715 × 0.5/0.06283² = 0.0242 rad, 1.39° deep (the exact 1.48°), at 0.06283/(2π × 6.5×10⁻⁴) = 15.4 Hz (the exact 14.7).
  6. The path. Let go at rest, φ̇ = 0 at the top of each nod: cusps. On average it precesses at 2.39 rad/s, a turn in 2.63 s.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the top and lets it go (Things to try, top left).

  • Gravity's moment turns a spinning top's angular momentum round instead of tipping it over: it precesses, at about mgl/I₃ω₃, slower the faster it spins.
  • The spin and the angular momentum about the vertical are conserved, so the tilt moves on its own in an effective potential, nodding (nutation) between two turning points.
  • A fast top nods at about I₃ω₃/I₁, shallowly; let go at rest its axis traces cusps, pushed back loops, pushed along waves.
  • Upright, it sleeps if I₃²ω₃² > 4I₁mgl.
  • Euler angles lock upright; a quaternion and L, stepped by an exact split, don't.
  • With I₁ ≠ I₂ the spin isn't kept and the closed forms go: a free asymmetric top still needs Jacobi's elliptic functions and flips about its middle axis, and with gravity it can be chaotic.
  • On a table friction keeps only Jellett's J, so the energy sinks toward the least J allows: a sliding round tip rises to sleep, and a tippe top turns over if 1 − α < I₁/I₃ < 1 + α.
