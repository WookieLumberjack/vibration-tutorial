The equation of motion The rotational form

Two ways to the equations

Every page from here on starts from

M\ddot q + C\dot q + K q = Q

for some coordinates q: the mass, damping and stiffness matrices M, C and K, and the forces Q on each coordinate. For one mass on a spring the equation can be written down by eye; for anything bigger it has to be derived, and there are two ways.

  • Free-body diagrams (Newton and Euler): cut each body free, draw every force on it, and balance the forces against its mass times the acceleration of its centre of mass G, and the moments about G against its inertia times its angular acceleration. Direct, but it takes vectors, the forces that hold the bodies together, and care over which point the moments are taken about.
  • Lagrange's equations: write the kinetic energy T, the potential energy V and the dissipation D in any coordinates q that describe the motion, and differentiate. Scalars only, no forces of constraint, and the matrices come out symmetric whatever the coordinates.

Both give the same motion. The page derives two systems both ways, step by step, with their own numbers (the Free body and Lagrange tabs follow the controls): a car's body in bounce and pitch, linear from the start, and a double pendulum, whose whole equations are nonlinear and whose free-body diagram needs the rods' pulls (pick one under System).

Free-body diagrams

For a rigid body moving in a plane, rising xG at its centre of mass and turning θ:

m\ddot x_G = \sum F, \qquad J_G\ddot\theta = \sum M_G

with the moments taken about G. They may be taken about another point P instead, often to drop a force that passes through it, but then the inertia force mẍG, which acts at G, has a moment about P too. With P a distance e ahead of G (the figure below), and small turns,

\sum M_P = J_G\ddot\theta - m e\,\ddot x_G

Writing ΣMP = JPθ̈ alone, with JP = JG + me² (true only when P is fixed, or is G), is the classic slip, and it gives wrong frequencies (the trial Moments about the front axle). The rows that come out are a force and a moment, so in other coordinates M, C and K need not be symmetric, though they're right.

Lagrange's equations

Write the kinetic energy T(q, q̇), the potential energy V(q) and Rayleigh's dissipation function D(q̇), half the sum of each damper's c times its stretch rate squared rayleigh. Then each coordinate qi has lagrange

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot q_i} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_i} + \frac{\partial D}{\partial \dot q_i} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} = Q_i

The first two terms are the inertia, written from the energy rather than from accelerations; with the Lagrangian ℒ = T − V the conservative part is d/dt(∂ℒ/∂q̇i) − ∂ℒ/∂qi. Qi is the generalized force: the work the applied forces do in a small change δqi, the other coordinates held, over δqi. A force F pushing up on a body at a distance a ahead of G (the car's front axle, below), where it rises δxG + aδθ, does work F(δxG + aδθ), so in (xG, θ) it is Q = (F, aF): a force on the rise and a moment on the turn. The forces that hold the bodies together do no work in any motion the coordinates allow, so they never appear.

For small motion about equilibrium the energies are quadratic,

T = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T} M \dot q, \qquad V = \tfrac12 q^{\mathsf T} K q, \qquad D = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T} C \dot q

and Lagrange's equations are Mq̈ + Cq̇ + Kq = Q: M, K and C are the Hessians of T, V and D, Mij = ∂²T/∂q̇i∂q̇j, so they're symmetric. New coordinates q′, with q = Sq′, make them

M' = S^{\mathsf T} M S, \qquad C' = S^{\mathsf T} C S, \qquad K' = S^{\mathsf T} K S

and the free-body diagram's rows, mixed by Sᵀ, are the same equations. Measuring each spring from where it sits at rest drops gravity: its work cancels the springs' preload. (The Pendulums page's gravity stiffness is where it doesn't: there the weight's own potential curves.)

A car in bounce and pitch

A car's body on its springs, the wheels and tyres left out: a rigid body of mass m and pitch inertia J = mkG² about G, on kf and cf a distance a ahead of G and kr and cr a distance b behind, the wheelbase L = a + b.

Two coordinates describe its motion, xG and θ (nose up) for instance. The body above the front axle rises xG + aθ and above the rear xG − bθ, so

T = \tfrac12 m\dot x_G^2 + \tfrac12 J\dot\theta^2 V = \tfrac12 k_f(x_G + a\theta)^2 + \tfrac12 k_r(x_G - b\theta)^2

and their Hessians are M = diag(m, J) and

K = \begin{bmatrix} k_f + k_r & k_f a - k_r b \\ k_f a - k_r b & k_f a^2 + k_r b^2 \end{bmatrix}

(C the same, with cf and cr). Pure bounce would be at √((kf + kr)/m) and pure pitch at √((kfa² + krb²)/J), but unless kfa = krb the two couple. The natural frequencies ωi are the roots of det(K − ω²M) = 0, a quadratic in ω², and each mode is a turn about a node, the point that stays still: xG + ℓθ = 0, a distance ℓ = −xG/θ ahead of G in the mode's shape. It lies outside the wheelbase for the mostly bouncing mode, inside it for the mostly pitching one.

Driven by the road

Over a bump the road lifts the front wheel yf(t) and the rear yr(t) = yf(t − L/v) at speed v. The front spring then stretches xG + aθ − yf, and differentiating the new V and D leaves the same M, C and K, with the road on the right:

Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ a \end{bmatrix} (k_f y_f + c_f\dot y_f) + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -b \end{bmatrix} (k_r y_r + c_r\dot y_r)

each wheel's spring and damper pushing at its axle, as the base motion did under the isolator's mount. The Bump tab rides a speed hump this way, by the same exact step as the chain.

Coordinates and coupling

Coupling belongs to the coordinates, not to the car. In (xG, θ) only K couples (static coupling). Take instead xP at a point P a distance e ahead of G, so xG = xP − eθ. At the elastic centre

e^* = \frac{k_f a - k_r b}{k_f + k_r}

where a force pushes the body straight up, K is diagonal, and M couples through −me (dynamic coupling). In the ends' coordinates (xf, xr), each spring sees one coordinate and K is diagonal again, while M couples by (mab − J)/L². When J = mab, a dynamic index kG²/(ab) of 1, that vanishes too, and each end bounces on its own spring at √(kf/mf) and √(kr/mr), with mf = mb/L and mr = ma/L: a bump under the front wheel leaves the rear alone. Cars are often laid out near 1, and Olley built his ride rig to vary it milliken. In every set of coordinates the frequencies are the same.

Flat ride

Over a bump the rear wheel arrives L/v after the front. Maurice Olley's rule, from his ride tests at Cadillac in the early 1930s: the front springs' static deflection some 30 % greater than the rear's milliken. Since fn = √(g/δ)/2π (static deflection), the front's ride frequency is then about 12 % lower than the rear's (1/√1.3 = 0.88), so the rear, hit later but quicker, catches the front up and the body bounces rather than pitches. The Bump tab rides a speed hump either way round.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on the car in (xG, θ): a 1200 kg body with kG = 1.3 m, its axles a = 1.2 m ahead of G and b = 1.5 m behind, 40 kN/m and 2500 N·s/m at each.

  1. The energies' Hessians. J = mkG² = 1200 × 1.3² = 2028 kg·m², so M = diag(1200, 2028). K11 = kf + kr = 80,000 N/m, K12 = kfa − krb = 48,000 − 60,000 = −12,000 N, and K22 = kfa² + krb² = 57,600 + 90,000 = 147,600 N·m. C in the same way: 5000, −750 and 9225.
  2. Uncoupled. Pure bounce would be √(80,000/1200) = 8.165 rad/s, 1.30 Hz, and pure pitch √(147,600/2028) = 8.531 rad/s, 1.36 Hz.
  3. The frequencies. det(K − ω²M) = 0 is
2.434\times10^{6}\,\omega^4 - 3.394\times10^{8}\,\omega^2 + 1.166\times10^{10} = 0

(mJ, mK22 + JK11 and K11K22 − K12²), so ω² = 61.45 and 78.00 (rad/s)²: 1.25 Hz and 1.41 Hz, one below both uncoupled frequencies and one above: with M diagonal, static coupling always pushes them apart.

  1. The nodes. In a mode, the first row of (K − ω²M)q = 0 gives xG/θ = −K12/(K11 − ω²m). At 61.45, 12,000/(80,000 − 73,740) = 1.92 m, so the node is 1.92 m behind G, outside the wheelbase: mostly bounce. At 78.00, 12,000/(80,000 − 93,600) = −0.88 m: a node 0.88 m ahead of G, between the axles, mostly pitch.
  2. The other coordinates. The elastic centre is e* = −12,000/80,000 = −0.15 m, 0.15 m behind G, and the dynamic index 1.69/(1.2 × 1.5) = 0.939; the ends alone, mf = 1200 × 1.5/2.7 = 667 kg and mr = 533 kg on 40 kN/m, would bounce at 1.23 and 1.38 Hz.

These are the readouts under the matrices, and the Lagrange tab's last lines. Picking the double pendulum, two 1 kg bobs on 0.5 m rods, ω² = (2 ∓ √2) × 9.807/0.5 = 11.49 and 66.97 (rad/s)²: 0.540 and 1.30 Hz, against 0.705 Hz for either rod alone.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the car or the pendulum (Things to try, top left).

  • Free-body diagrams: mẍG = ΣF and JGθ̈ = ΣMG; moments about another point need G's inertia force too.
  • Lagrange: write T, V and D in any coordinates; M, K and C are their Hessians, so always symmetric, and the forces come in as the work they do, Qi.
  • Coupling depends on the coordinates (static in K, dynamic in M); the frequencies don't.
  • A car's bounce and pitch couple unless kfa = krb; at a dynamic index of 1 its ends bounce apart.
  • Lagrange needs no forces of constraint (a double pendulum's rod pulls); take the whole T and V, or their quadratic parts at rest, never a cruder linearization, and large swings, which the linear model can't follow, can be chaotic.
