The equation of motion The rotational form

Two ways to the equations

Every page from here on starts from M q̈ + C q̇ + K q = f. For one mass on a spring the equation can be written down by eye; for anything bigger it has to be derived, and there are two ways.

  • Free-body diagrams (Newton and Euler): cut each body free, draw every force on it, and write m aG = ΣF and, about G, J θ̈ = ΣMG. Direct, but it takes vectors, forces of constraint and care over which point the moments are taken about.
  • Lagrange's equations: write the kinetic energy T, the potential energy V and the dissipation D in any coordinates q that describe the motion, and differentiate. Scalars only, no forces of constraint, and the matrices come out symmetric whatever the coordinates.

Both give the same motion. The page derives two systems both ways, step by step, with their own numbers (the Free body and Lagrange tabs follow the controls): a car's body in bounce and pitch, linear from the start, and a double pendulum, whose whole equations are nonlinear and whose free-body diagram needs the rods' pulls (pick one under System).

Free-body diagrams

For a rigid body in a plane: m ẍG = ΣF and JG θ̈ = ΣMG, the moments about the centre of mass G. Moments may be taken about another point P instead, often to drop a force that passes through it, but then the inertia force m aG, acting at G, has a moment about P too: ΣMP = JG θ̈ + (rG − rP) × m aG. Writing ΣMP = JP θ̈ alone (true only when P is fixed, or is G) is the classic slip, and it gives wrong frequencies (the trial Moments about the front axle). The rows that come out are a force and a moment, so in other coordinates M, C and K need not be symmetric, though they're right.

Lagrange's equations

With T(q, q̇), V(q) and Rayleigh's dissipation function D(q̇) = ½ Σ ci(stretch rate)², each coordinate qi has

d/dt(∂T/∂q̇i) − ∂T/∂qi + ∂D/∂q̇i + ∂V/∂qi = Qi,

where Qi is the generalized force: the work the applied forces do in a small δqi, over δqi. For small motion about equilibrium T = ½ q̇ᵀMq̇, V = ½ qᵀKq and D = ½ q̇ᵀCq̇, and the equations are M q̈ + C q̇ + K q = Q: M, K and C are the Hessians of T, V and D, so they're symmetric. Changing to new coordinates q = T q′ makes them TᵀMT, TᵀCT and TᵀKT. The free-body diagram's rows, mixed by Tᵀ, are the same equations. Measuring each spring from where it sits at rest drops gravity: its work cancels the springs' preload. (The Pendulums page's gravity stiffness is where it doesn't: there the weight's own potential curves.)

A car in bounce and pitch

A car's body on its springs, the wheels and tyres left out: a rigid body of mass m and pitch inertia J = m r² about G, on kf and cf at a ahead of G and kr and cr at b behind (L = a + b). Two coordinates describe its motion, xG and θ for instance, and

T = ½ m ẋG² + ½ J θ̇²,   V = ½ kf(xG + aθ)² + ½ kr(xG − bθ)²,

so M = diag(m, J) and K = [[kf + kr, kfa − krb], [kfa − krb, kfa² + krb²]]. Pure bounce would be at √((kf + kr)/m) and pure pitch at √((kfa² + krb²)/J), but unless kfa = krb the two couple and each mode is a turn about a node: outside the wheelbase for the mostly bouncing one, inside it for the mostly pitching one.

Coordinates and coupling

Coupling belongs to the coordinates, not to the car. In (xG, θ) only K couples (static coupling). At the elastic centre e* = (kfa − krb)/(kf + kr), where a force pushes the body straight, K is diagonal, and M couples through −m e (dynamic coupling). In the ends' coordinates (xf, xr), each spring sees one coordinate and K is diagonal again, while M couples by (m a b − J)/L². When J = m a b, a dynamic index r²/(ab) of 1, that vanishes too, and each end bounces on its own spring at √(k/mend), mf = m b/L and mr = m a/L: a bump under the front wheel leaves the rear alone. Most cars sit near 1. In every set of coordinates the frequencies are the same.

Flat ride

Over a bump the rear wheel arrives L/v after the front. Maurice Olley's rule from the 1930s: make the front's ride frequency lower than the rear's (about 20 % lower), so that the rear, hit later but quicker, catches the front up and the body bounces rather than pitches. The Bump tab rides a speed hump either way round.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the car or the pendulum (Things to try, top left).

  • Free-body diagrams: m aG = ΣF and J θ̈ = ΣMG; moments about another point need G's inertia force too.
  • Lagrange: write T, V and D in any coordinates; M, K and C are their Hessians, so always symmetric.
  • Coupling depends on the coordinates (static in K, dynamic in M); the frequencies don't.
  • A car's bounce and pitch couple unless kfa = krb; at a dynamic index of 1 its ends bounce apart.
  • Lagrange needs no forces of constraint (a double pendulum's rod pulls); linearize after differentiating, and large swings, which the linear model can't follow, can be chaotic.
