Planning a test

A modal test measures the response at a few points and fits the modes to them. Before the first sensor is glued on, two questions: where should the sensors go so the modes wanted (the targets) can be told apart and fitted well; and what will the structure actually do while it runs, at the frequencies it runs at. The first is sensor placement; the second is its operational deflection shape (ODS).

Both start from a model of the structure, even a rough one: it gives the target modes' shapes at every place a sensor could go (here, every mass: the candidates), and that is all placement needs.

What a placement is worth

At the sensors the response is the target modes' shapes there, Φs (a row per sensor, a column per target), times the modal coordinates η, plus each sensor's noise, of standard deviation σ:

y = \Phi_s\,\eta + \text{noise}, \qquad \hat\eta = (\Phi_s^{T}\Phi_s)^{-1}\,\Phi_s^{T}\,y

The least-squares fit η̂ has covariance σ²(ΦsᵀΦs)⁻¹: ΦsᵀΦs is the Fisher information fisher about η. Its inverse sets the noise on each fitted coordinate, and its determinant the volume of the noise's ellipsoid, inversely: the larger the determinant, the tighter the fit. If it is singular (fewer sensors than targets, or sensors that see two modes alike) the modes can't be separated at all.

The figure is the default chain's (worked below): modes 1 and 2's noise with a sensor on every mass (a circle), on the three masses effective independence keeps (a little larger), and on the three nearest the ground (long and thin: along that diagonal the two modes can't be told apart). The page scores a placement by its determinant as a share of all the candidates', and each mode's noise as a multiple of all the candidates'.

Effective independence

Trying every set of sensors is out of reach (choose 10 of 100 points: 1013 sets). Kammer's effective independence (EfI) kammer starts with every candidate and drops one at a time. Each candidate j's share of the information is

E_j = \bigl[\Phi\,(\Phi^{T}\Phi)^{-1}\,\Phi^{T}\bigr]_{jj}

the diagonal of the projector onto the targets' columns: between 0 and 1, adding up to the number of targets. It is what dropping that candidate costs: its row of Φ, a, adds aaT to the information ΦTΦ, and taking it away scales the determinant by 1 − aT(ΦTΦ)−1a, which is Ej's complement:

\det(\Phi^{T}\Phi - a a^{T}) = \det(\Phi^{T}\Phi)\,(1 - E_j)

So EfI drops the candidate with the smallest Ej, the one the determinant misses least, then works the shares out again for the ones left (losing one raises the others'), until as many are left as there are sensors. With one target, Ej = φj²/‖φ‖²: the sensors go where the mode moves most. It is greedy, not optimal (the best ten sensors aren't always the best eleven less one), but it's fast and usually close.

EfI looks only at shapes, so it may keep a sensor where the response is small and the noise floor matters. Weighting Ej by the driving-point residue,

\mathrm{DPR}_j = \sum_r \frac{\phi_{jr}^2}{\omega_r}

(EfI-DPR), leans toward where the response is large, at some cost in information. (φjr²/ωr is, but for a constant, mode r's residue in the driving-point FRF at j: the lower modes count most.)

Noise and spatial aliasing

The Noise & aliasing tab sets the placement beside EfI's, EfI-DPR's and evenly spaced sensors: the information, each mode's noise, and the auto-MAC of the targets at the sensors. Two modes whose MAC is near 1 there look alike to the test: spatial aliasing, as a signal sampled too slowly in time looks like a slower one. Too few sensors, or sensors bunched where every mode moves the same way, do it.

Where to excite

The force has a placement too. A shaker or hammer at mass k drives mode r in proportion to φkr: at a node of a mode it misses that mode entirely, and no number of sensors brings it back. Pick a point where every target moves well (the smallest |φkr| over the targets as large as can be), which the driving-point residue also favours. A second reference covers a mode the first misses (More than one reference).

Operational deflection shapes

An ODS richardson-ods is how the structure deflects at one frequency Ω under the forces it runs with:

X(\Omega) = H(\Omega)\,F = \sum_r \phi_r\,\eta_r

complex, each mass with its own phase. It is not a mode shape. Its modal coordinates come from the undamped modes, solved together (so they add up to X exactly, whatever the damping):

\eta = \bigl(\mathrm{diag}(\omega_r^2) - \Omega^2 I + i\,\Omega\,\Phi^{T} C\,\Phi\bigr)^{-1}\,\Phi^{T} F

Near a lightly damped mode its term grows as 1/2ζ and the ODS looks like the mode; between modes it's a mix; and it changes when the force moves, which a mode doesn't. That's what an ODS is for: to see how the machine moves where it runs, and so where to stiffen or damp it.

A complex shape has no single picture: mass j moves as Re(XjeiΩt). The page draws it at the instant it is largest, Re(X e−iθ), with

\theta = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{arg} \sum_j X_j^2

since ‖Re(X e−iθ)‖² = ½‖X‖² + ½Re(e−2iθΣXj²), largest when the second term's phase is 0. A quarter cycle later it is the imaginary part, dotted: zero for a shape whose masses all move in or out of phase, as an undamped mode's do.

Measuring an ODS

The forces in a running machine usually aren't measured, so an ODS is measured relative to a reference sensor that stays put while the others rove:

T_j = \frac{X_j}{X_\mathrm{ref}}

in a test the cross-spectrum Gref,j over the reference's auto-spectrum, which keeps each ratio's phase. The shape comes out right whatever the force's size, but if the reference sits near a node of the ODS every ratio is huge, and its noise with it: put the reference where the ODS moves most. (Operational modal analysis goes further, from responses alone to the modes.)

Worked example

The page opens on the Simulation page's chain: four 1 kg masses on 400 N/m springs with 2 N·s/m dampers, modes at 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz. Three targets, three sensors, by effective independence.

  1. The shares. With equal masses the four mass-normalized modes make an orthogonal matrix, so the projector onto the first three is I − φ4φ4ᵀ and Ej = 1 − φj4²: mode 4's shape is −0.4285, 0.6565, −0.5774 and 0.2280, so the shares are 0.816, 0.569, 0.667 and 0.948, adding up to 3. A candidate is worth least where the mode left out moves most.
  2. One drop. Mass 2's 0.569 is the smallest: EfI keeps masses 1, 3 and 4, and the Fisher information is 1 − 0.569 = 0.431 of all four's (43.1 %). Evenly spaced sensors, and EfI-DPR, land on the same three here.
  3. The noise. All four sensors give ΦTΦ = I; dropping mass 2's row a = (0.4285, 0.5774, −0.2280) leaves I − aaT, whose inverse is I + aaT/(1 − 0.569) (multiply them to check). Its diagonal is 1 + 0.4285²/0.431 = 1.43, 1.77 and 1.12, so each mode's noise is √ of that: 1.19, 1.33 and 1.06 times four sensors'. Mode 2 loses most: of the three, it has the largest entry in mass 2's row.
  4. The auto-MAC. At masses 1, 3 and 4 modes 1 and 2 are (0.2280, 0.5774, 0.6565) and (0.5774, 0, −0.5774): their MAC is (0.1316 − 0.3790)²/(0.8164 × 0.6667) = 0.112, the largest off-diagonal. Bunched on masses 1 to 3 instead, the information is 1 − 0.948 = 0.052: the figure's long ellipse.
  5. Where to excite. At mass 3 mode 2's shape is 0: a hammer there would miss it. At mass 4 the targets' shapes are 0.6565, −0.5774 and 0.4285, none small: the page's force is there.
  6. The ODS. The operating point is 1 Hz on mass 4, just below mode 1 (ω1 = 6.946 rad/s, Ω = 6.283 rad/s). The dampers are 0.005 × the springs, so ΦTCΦ = 0.005 diag(ωr²) and mode 1 alone gives, at mass 4, 0.6565²/(48.25 − 39.48 + i × 6.283 × 0.005 × 48.25) = 0.4310/(8.77 + 1.52i), of size 0.0484 m/N against the whole response's 0.0496: mode 1's share is 0.977, and the ODS's MAC with mode 1 is 0.999. Its shape is 0.326, 0.619, 0.852 and 1, close to mode 1's.
  7. The reference. On mass 4, where the ODS is largest, every ratio Tj is at most 1: 0.326, 0.619 and 0.852.

Things to try

Each of these loads a chain into the Simulation page and sets the sensors or the operating point.

  • ΦsᵀΦs at the sensors is the Fisher information: its determinant sets how much the noise spoils the fitted modes.
  • Effective independence drops, one at a time, the sensor with the smallest share of it: the determinant falls by 1 − Ej.
  • Too few sensors, or bunched ones, make modes look alike (spatial aliasing: a large auto-MAC off-diagonal).
  • Excite where every target moves well, not at a node.
  • An ODS is what the structure does at one frequency under its forces: like a mode at a lightly damped peak, a mix between them, and it changes when the force moves.
  • Measure an ODS against a reference where it moves most.
