Planning a test

A modal test measures the response at a few points and fits the modes to them. Before the first sensor is glued on, two questions: where should the sensors go so the modes wanted (the targets) can be told apart and fitted well; and what will the structure actually do while it runs, at the frequencies it runs at. The first is sensor placement; the second is its operational deflection shape.

What a placement is worth

At the sensors the response is y = Φsq + noise: Φs the target modes' rows at the sensors, q their modal coordinates. Least squares gives q̂ = (ΦsᵀΦs)⁻¹Φsᵀy, with covariance σ²(ΦsᵀΦs)⁻¹: ΦsᵀΦs is the Fisher information. The larger its determinant, the smaller the noise's ellipsoid; if it is singular, the modes can't be separated at all. The page scores a placement by its determinant as a share of all the candidates' (every mass with a sensor), and each mode's noise as a multiple of all the candidates'.

Effective independence

Kammer's effective independence (EfI) starts with every candidate and drops one at a time. Each candidate's share of the information is

Ei = [Φ(ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹Φᵀ]ii,

the diagonal of the projector onto the targets' columns: between 0 and 1, adding up to the number of targets. Dropping candidate i multiplies det(ΦᵀΦ) by (1 − Ei), so EfI drops the smallest, then works the shares out again for the ones left, until as many are left as there are sensors. With one target, Ei = φi²/Σφj²: the sensors go where the mode moves most. It is greedy, not optimal (the best set of k isn't always the best k + 1 less one), but it's fast and usually close.

EfI looks only at shapes, so it may keep a sensor where the response is small and the noise floor matters. Weighting Ei by the driving-point residue, DPRi = Σφir²/ωr (EfI-DPR), leans toward where the response is large, at some cost in information.

Noise and spatial aliasing

The Noise & aliasing tab sets the placement beside EfI's, EfI-DPR's and evenly spaced sensors: the information, each mode's noise, and the auto-MAC of the targets at the sensors. Two modes whose MAC is near 1 there look alike to the test: spatial aliasing, as a signal sampled too slowly in time looks like a slower one. Too few sensors, or sensors bunched where every mode moves the same way, do it.

Operational deflection shapes

An ODS is how the structure deflects at one frequency under the forces it runs with, X(ω) = H(ω)F: complex, each point with its own phase. It is not a mode shape. Each mode contributes φrηr, η = (Ω² − ω² + iωΦᵀCΦ)⁻¹ΦᵀF in the undamped modes (solved together, so they add up to X exactly, whatever the damping): near a lightly damped mode its 1/(2ζ) dominates and the ODS looks like the mode; between modes it's a mix; and it changes when the force moves, which a mode doesn't. That's what an ODS is for: to see how the machine moves where it runs, and so where to stiffen or damp it.

Measuring an ODS

The forces in a running machine usually aren't measured, so an ODS is measured relative to a reference sensor that stays put while the others rove: Ti = Xi/Xref, its phase from the cross-spectrum Gref,i. The shape comes out right whatever the force's size, but if the reference sits near a node of the ODS every ratio is huge, and its noise with it: put the reference where the ODS moves most. (Operational modal analysis goes further, from responses alone to the modes.)

  • Effective independence drops, one at a time, the sensor with the smallest share of it.
  • Too few sensors, or bunched ones, make modes look alike (spatial aliasing: a large auto-MAC off-diagonal).
