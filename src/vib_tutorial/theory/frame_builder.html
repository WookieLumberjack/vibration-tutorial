Building a model

Every model so far came with its equations: a chain, a shaft, a box on mounts. A real structure is drawn, not derived: nodes where things join, and elements between them, each of which knows its own stiffness and mass. The finite element method adds their shares up into the global M, C and K, and the rest is the eigenproblem the Simulation page solved. Its matrix form for frames, the direct stiffness method, was set out for aircraft structures in the 1950s turner. This page lets you build one: a plane frame (beams, which bend and stretch) or a truss (bars, which only stretch), with springs, dampers, point masses and supports.

The frame lies in the x–y plane, x across and y up. Each node has three DOFs: u along x, v along y, and θ, a turn anticlockwise. They are stacked into one vector q, node by node: node i's are rows 3i − 2, 3i − 1 and 3i (ui, vi, θi in the tables), and the model is

M\ddot q + C\dot q + Kq = f

with f the loads on those DOFs (a moment on a θ). The sections below build M and K in four steps: each element's matrices in its own axes, turned into the frame's axes, added up, and the supports struck out.

The elements' matrices

Each element is first described in its own axes: u′ along it and v′ across it (the primes mark the element's own axes throughout: its matrices are k′, m′ and c′). A bar of length L only stretches, and the force it takes is EA/L times the stretch u′2 − u′1:

k'_{\text{bar}} = \frac{EA}{L}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{on } (u'_1,\ u'_2)

A beam also bends. Across it, its deflection between the ends is written through the four end DOFs, v′1, θ1, v′2, θ2, with ξ the fraction of the way along:

v'(\xi) = N_1 v'_1 + N_2\,\theta_1 + N_3 v'_2 + N_4\,\theta_2 N_1 = 1 - 3\xi^2 + 2\xi^3, \qquad N_2 = L\,(\xi - 2\xi^2 + \xi^3) N_3 = 3\xi^2 - 2\xi^3, \qquad N_4 = L\,(\xi^3 - \xi^2)

Each Hermite cubic sets one end DOF to 1 and the other three to 0. Why cubics: a beam with no load between its ends has EI v′′′′ = 0, so under any end forces and moments it takes exactly a cubic. These shapes are the beam's exact static shapes, and the stiffness they give, k′ = ∫ EI N″TN″ dx (the bending strain energy, ½∫EI v″2 dx, written through the end DOFs), is exact:

k'_{\text{bend}} = \frac{EI}{L^3}\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 6L & -12 & 6L \\ 6L & 4L^2 & -6L & 2L^2 \\ -12 & -6L & 12 & -6L \\ 6L & 2L^2 & -6L & 4L^2 \end{bmatrix}

Each column is the set of end forces and moments that holds one cubic: the first, 12EI/L3 across and 6EI/L2 of moment at each end, holds end 1 up a unit with neither end turning. A beam's k′ is this on (v′, θ) plus the bar's on u′: six DOFs, stretching and bending uncoupled in its own axes.

The mass is where the approximation comes in. The consistent mass matrix archer takes the same shapes for the motion, m′ = ∫ρA NTN dx (the kinetic energy written through the end DOFs). For a bar it is (ρAL/6)[[2, 1], [1, 2]], along it and across it; for a beam's bending,

m'_{\text{bend}} = \frac{\rho A L}{420}\begin{bmatrix} 156 & 22L & 54 & -13L \\ 22L & 4L^2 & 13L & -3L^2 \\ 54 & 13L & 156 & -22L \\ -13L & -3L^2 & -22L & 4L^2 \end{bmatrix}

A vibrating beam doesn't bend in static shapes, so this is only close. The lumped mass puts half each element's mass ρAL at each end, on u and v only, as the Continuous systems page did for one bar or beam.

The rest are short. A spring k between two nodes is a bar without mass, on the stretch along the line joining them (or to a fixed anchor); a damper c is the same in C. A torsion spring kθ (or damper cθ) acts on a node's turn, to the ground. A point mass adds m to its node's u and v, and its rotary inertia J to its θ.

From the element's axes to the frame's

An element at γ to x sees its ends' displacements in the frame's axes through a rotation: for each end,

u' = u\cos\gamma + v\sin\gamma, \qquad v' = -u\sin\gamma + v\cos\gamma

and θ is θ, a turn being the same in any axes. Stacked for both ends, with d the element's own six DOFs and qe its nodes' six in the frame's axes,

d = T q_e, \qquad T = \begin{bmatrix} R & 0 \\ 0 & R \end{bmatrix}, \quad R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\gamma & \sin\gamma & 0 \\ -\sin\gamma & \cos\gamma & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}

The strain energy is the same whichever axes it's written in:

\tfrac12 d^{\mathsf{T}} k' d = \tfrac12 q_e^{\mathsf{T}} (T^{\mathsf{T}} k' T)\, q_e

so the element's stiffness in the frame's axes is TTk′T, and its mass TTm′T. It is the Substructuring page's change of basis again, for the same reason: a change of coordinates that keeps the energy. A bar's T keeps only u′, so a bar at 45° puts EA/2L on each of u and v and on the u–v coupling. The Element tab shows the picked element's k′ (or m′), its T and TTk′T, with the global DOFs each row lands on.

Assembly

Each element's TTk′T adds into the global K at its nodes' DOFs: an entry Krs is the sum over every element touching both DOF r and DOF s. Where two elements share a node, their blocks overlap and add: the node is held by both. That's the whole of the direct stiffness method. Two DOFs that no element shares stay zero, so K is sparse and, if the nodes are numbered along the structure, banded (the Assembly tab tints the picked element's entries). K is symmetric, since every element's is, and singular until something holds the structure still: a free frame can move as a rigid body with no strain at all. M and C assemble the same way.

Supports and struck-out DOFs

A support holds some of a node's DOFs at zero:

(and the rollers and guides the other way round). A held DOF's column in K multiplies a zero, and its row is the force the support has to supply, its reaction; neither enters the motion, so both are struck out. DOFs that no element touches are struck out too: a node joined only by bars has a θ with no stiffness and no mass (bars have pinned ends), which would be a mode at 0 Hz with nothing in it. What's left is the free DOFs' M, C and K.

Massless DOFs

A free DOF with stiffness but no mass (a lumped beam's turns, or a node with only springs on it) carries no inertia force. Split the free DOFs into those with mass, qm, and those without, qs: the massless rows of Kq = −Mq̈ have nothing on the right,

K_{sm}\,q_m + K_{ss}\,q_s = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_s = -K_{ss}^{-1}K_{sm}\,q_m

So they follow the others statically, and substituting gives a smaller model on qm alone, with stiffness

K_{mm} - K_{ms}K_{ss}^{-1}K_{sm}

This is static condensation, Guyan's reduction guyan, and here it is exact, since what it leaves out has no mass. (The Substructuring page uses the same step on DOFs that do have mass, where it is only an approximation.)

The modes

Then Kφ = ω2Mφ, as on the Simulation page, with each mode drawn on the frame: beams bend along their Hermite cubics between the nodes. The table splits each mode's strain energy, ½φTKφ, among the members' stretching, their bending, and the springs: a frame's low modes bend, a truss's members only ever stretch.

A beam element's cubics are its exact static shapes, not its vibrating ones, so a mesh is a Rayleigh–Ritz model (the Energy methods page's): the frame held to motions the cubics can make. With consistent mass every frequency is an upper bound, and falls as the mesh is refined. Lumped mass breaks the bound, and its frequencies usually come out low. Either way a mode needs a few elements per half wave: the highest modes of any mesh are wrong.

Timoshenko beams

A deep beam also shears, and its sections' turning has inertia (the Beams & plates page's thick beams, Timoshenko's theory timoshenko). Timoshenko's element keeps the same DOFs, with

\varphi_s = \frac{12EI}{\kappa_s G A L^2}

the ratio of a cantilever's shear deflection under a tip load to its bending deflection, over an element. Here κs is the shear coefficient: 5/6 for a rectangle, and Cowper's formula cowper for a tube. Its bending stiffness is

k'_{\text{bend}} = \frac{EI}{(1 + \varphi_s)L^3}\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 6L & \cdots \\ 6L & (4 + \varphi_s)L^2 & \cdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots \end{bmatrix}

exact statically again, and its mass adds ρI terms for the turning (Friedman and Kosmatka's element friedman-kosmatka). At φs = 0 it is Euler–Bernoulli's. A beam's depth h needs to be well under a tenth of a half wave before the difference fades.

Mechanisms

Some structures move without any element straining: a free body's three rigid motions, or a square of bars folding into a parallelogram. Each is a mode at 0 Hz, the null space of K. For a pin-jointed truss, Maxwell's count maxwell-frames compares the bars b and the support reactions r with the joints' 2j DOFs: b + r < 2j means a mechanism. It's necessary but not sufficient: a bar can be redundant in one place while another is missing. Calladine's form calladine makes the count exact:

b + r - 2j = n_s - n_m

with nm the mechanisms and ns the states of self-stress: sets of bar forces that balance with no load at all (a bar that could be tightened with nothing else noticing). The square of bars on two pins has b + r − 2j = 4 + 4 − 8 = 0, yet folds: the bottom bar, between the two pins, is a state of self-stress, so ns = 1 and nm = 1.

Damping

A frame's own damping (its joints, its material) is rarely known element by element, so it's usually put in as Rayleigh damping rayleigh-sound, C = αM + βK (Damping ζ in the Model box). Each undamped mode then has

\zeta_r = \frac12\left(\frac{\alpha}{\omega_r} + \beta\,\omega_r\right)

and α and β are chosen to give ζ at the first two modes:

\alpha = \frac{2\zeta\,\omega_1\omega_2}{\omega_1 + \omega_2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_1 + \omega_2}

Between them it dips a little, and beyond them it grows without end: the high modes die fast. Rayleigh damping is proportional, ΦTCΦ diagonal: the modes stay those of the undamped frame, each damped on its own. Dampers (between two nodes, to the ground, or on a turn) go into C as their TTc′T, just as springs go into K; they are rarely proportional.

Loads and responses

The Load box puts a force (or a moment on a turn) on one DOF: the arrow. The Response tab plots the receptance H(Ω) from it to another DOF (the square), and the response there from rest: to a harmonic force F sin Ωt, an impulse, or a step.

Both models are stepped exactly. The load is given states of its own: a constant for a step; a sine and a cosine turning at Ω for a harmonic force (each one's rate is Ω times the other). These are stacked under the structure's states, so one matrix exponential carries both over each step Δt without approximating either; at resonance too, where the closed-form steady state breaks down. (The Simulation page's FOH update is exact for a force that is linear between samples; this is exact for a sine.) An impulse is the state it leaves: at t = 0⁺ the velocities are M−1 times the impulse.

Two formulations

The conventional model takes the undamped modes, mass-normalized, and keeps them for the damping too: q = Σ φrηr, each mode a single oscillator,

\ddot\eta_r + 2\zeta_r\omega_r\,\dot\eta_r + \omega_r^2\,\eta_r = \varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} f, \qquad 2\zeta_r\omega_r = \varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} C \varphi_r

It drops ΦTCΦ's off-diagonal terms. The state space keeps everything: it carries q and q̇ together, one first-order equation for each,

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} q \\ \dot q \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot q \\ M^{-1}(f - C\dot q - Kq) \end{bmatrix}

twice the size, its eigenvalues λ the damped poles. With proportional damping the two are the same model. With a damper they part, by as much as the damper couples the modes, and most when the modes it couples are close together. A tuned mass damper is the worst case den-hartog, since its whole point is two close modes and a damper between them.

Complex modes

The state space's eigenvectors ψ are complex: each node has its own phase, so with a damper the nodes no longer pass through zero together (Swing: Damped mode), and the shape's points spread off a line in the complex plane (the Formulations tab; MPC 1 for a real mode, as the Complex modes page measured it). A heavy damper also moves the frequencies, which the conventional model's ζr can't. A DOF with a damper on it and no mass (a torsion damper on a lumped beam's node) has a first-order row, Cq̇ + Kq = f: it adds a real root, a relaxation e−t/τ, not a mode; so does an overdamped mode, two of them.

Worked example

The page opens on a steel cantilever, 50 × 50 mm and ℓ = 1 m long, clamped at node 1 and cut into four beams of L = 0.25 m (E = 210 GPa, ρ = 7850 kg/m³). Pick the first beam to follow the numbers on the Element and Assembly tabs.

  1. The section. A = 2500 mm², I = 504/12 = 5.208 × 105 mm⁴, so EA = 525 MN, EI = 109.4 kN·m², and ρA = 19.63 kg/m.
  2. One element. EA/L = 2.10 GN/m along it; across it, 12EI/L3 = 84.0 MN/m, 6EI/L2 = 10.5 MN, 4EI/L = 1.75 MN·m and 2EI/L = 0.875 MN·m: the entries of k′. It lies along x, so γ = 0, T is the identity and TTk′T = k′.
  3. Assembly. Node 2 is shared by beams 1 and 2, so its diagonal entries add: Ku2u2 = 2 × 2.10 = 4.20 GN/m, Kv2v2 = 2 × 84.0 = 168 MN/m and Kθ2θ2 = 2 × 1.75 = 3.50 MN·m. The coupling of v2 to θ2 cancels, −10.5 + 10.5 = 0: beam 1 sees node 2 as its far end, beam 2 as its near one. The mass adds the same way: Mv2v2 = 2 × 156/420 × ρAL = 2 × 0.3714 × 4.906 = 3.645 kg.
  4. Supports. The clamp strikes out node 1's three DOFs: 15 − 3 = 12 free DOFs, so 12 modes.
  5. Statics, exact. Solving Kq = f for 1000 N up at the tip gives 3.048 mm and a turn of 4.571 mrad there: exactly Fℓ3/3EI and Fℓ2/2EI, since the cubics are the beam's static shapes.
  6. The modes. Euler's cantilever has f = (βnℓ)2a/2πℓ2 with a = √(EI/ρA) = 74.65 m²/s and βnℓ = 1.875, 4.694, 7.855: 41.78, 261.8 and 733.1 Hz. The mesh gives 41.78, 262.1 and 738.7 Hz: +0.003 %, +0.12 % and +0.77 %, each above, the higher more. Mode 4, 1301 Hz, is the first stretching mode, against the bar's √(E/ρ)/4ℓ = 5172/4 = 1293 Hz (+0.6 %).

Things to try

Each of these loads a frame (Things to try, top left). Change it from there: it's yours to edit.

  • Each element knows its own k′ and m′ in its own axes; TTk′T turns them into the frame's.
  • A beam's Hermite cubics are its exact static shapes: its stiffness is exact, its consistent mass an approximation.
  • Assembly adds every element's share at its DOFs: K is sparse, banded and symmetric.
  • Supports strike out rows and columns; so does a DOF no element touches (a truss node's turn).
  • A DOF with no mass follows the rest statically: condensing it out is exact.
  • Consistent mass gives upper bounds that fall as the mesh is refined; lumped mass usually comes out low.
  • Timoshenko's beams shear and have turning inertia: deep beams and high modes come out lower.
  • A mode at 0 Hz is a mechanism (or a rigid motion): Maxwell's count can miss one, Calladine's can't.
  • Rayleigh damping αM + βK is proportional: the modes stay real, and the conventional model is exact.
  • A damper couples the modes through ΦTCΦ's off-diagonal terms: the state space keeps them, the conventional model drops them, worst for close modes (a tuned mass damper).
  • The state space's modes are complex: each node has its own phase (MPC < 1).
  • Giving the load states of its own steps a step or a sine exactly, resonance included.
