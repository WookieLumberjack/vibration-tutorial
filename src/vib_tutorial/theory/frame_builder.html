Building a model

Every model so far came with its equations: a chain, a shaft, a box on mounts. A real structure is drawn, not derived: nodes where things join, and elements between them, each of which knows its own stiffness and mass. The finite element method adds their shares up into the global M, C and K, and the rest is the eigenproblem the Simulation page solved. This page lets you build one: a plane frame (beams, which bend) or a truss (bars, which only stretch), with springs, dampers, point masses and supports.

Each node has three DOFs: u across, v up and θ, a turn anticlockwise. Node i's are numbered 3i − 2, 3i − 1 and 3i (ui, vi, θi in the tables).

The elements' matrices

In its own axes (a along it, t across it), an element's matrices are short. A bar of length L only stretches: k' = (EA/L)[[1, −1], [−1, 1]] on its ends' axial displacements. A beam adds bending: on each end's (t, θ),

k'bend = (EI/L³) [[12, 6L, −12, 6L], [6L, 4L², −6L, 2L²], [−12, −6L, 12, −6L], [6L, 2L², −6L, 4L²]],

the forces that hold the Hermite cubics, the beam's exact static shapes under end loads. A spring between two nodes is a bar without mass: k on the stretch along it. A point mass adds m to its node's u and v, and J to its θ.

The mass matrices come from the same shapes (consistent mass: m' = ∫ρA NᵀN dx, e.g. a bar's (ρAL/6)[[2, 1], [1, 2]]), or by putting half each element's mass at each end (lumped), as the Continuous systems page did for one bar or beam.

From the element's axes to the frame's

An element at α to x sees its ends' global displacements through a rotation: for each end, a = u cos α + v sin α, t = −u sin α + v cos α, and θ is θ. Stacked for both ends, d = T qe, and the energy ½dᵀk'd is ½qeᵀ(Tᵀk'T)qe: the element's stiffness in the frame's axes is Tᵀk'T, and its mass Tᵀm'T. A bar's T keeps only a, so a bar at 45° puts EA/2L on each of u and v and on the u–v coupling. The Element tab shows the picked element's k' (or m'), its T and Tᵀk'T, with the global DOFs each row lands on.

Assembly

Each element's Tᵀk'T adds into the global K at its nodes' DOFs: an entry Krs is the sum over every element touching both DOF r and DOF s. That's the direct stiffness method. Two DOFs no element shares stay zero, so K is sparse and, if the nodes are numbered along the structure, banded (the Assembly tab tints the picked element's entries). K is symmetric, and singular until something holds the structure still.

Supports and struck-out DOFs

A support holds some of a node's DOFs at zero: pinned holds u and v, a roller one of them, clamped all three. A held DOF's row is the support's reaction, and its column multiplies a zero, so both are struck out. DOFs that no element touches are struck out too: a node joined only by bars has a θ with no stiffness and no mass (bars have pinned ends), which would be a mode at 0 Hz with nothing in it. What's left is the free DOFs' M, C and K.

Massless DOFs

A free DOF with stiffness but no mass (a lumped beam's turns, or a node with only springs on it) carries no inertia force: its row of K q = −M q̈ is Ksq = 0. So it follows the others statically, qs = −Kss⁻¹Ksmqm, and substituting gives a smaller model, Kmm − KmsKss⁻¹Ksm: static condensation (Guyan's reduction), exact here, since what it leaves out has no mass.

The modes

Then K φ = ω² M φ, as on the Simulation page, with each mode drawn on the frame: beams bend along their Hermite cubics between the nodes. The table splits each mode's strain energy ½φᵀKφ among the members' stretching, their bending, and the springs: a frame's low modes bend, a truss's members only ever stretch.

A beam element's cubics are its exact static shapes, not its vibrating ones, so a mesh is a Rayleigh–Ritz model (the Energy methods page's): with consistent mass every frequency is an upper bound, and falls as the mesh is refined. Lumped mass breaks the bound, and its frequencies usually come out low. Either way a mode needs a few elements per half wave: the highest modes of any mesh are wrong.

Timoshenko beams

A deep beam also shears, and its sections' turning has inertia (the Beams & plates page's thick beams). Timoshenko's element keeps the same DOFs with φ = 12EI/κGAL², the shear's share of a tip load's deflection: its bending stiffness is (EI/(1 + φ)L³)[[12, 6L, …], [6L, (4 + φ)L², …], …], exact statically again, and its mass adds ρI terms for the turning. At φ = 0 it is Euler–Bernoulli's. A beam's depth needs to be well under a tenth of a half wave before the difference fades.

Mechanisms

Some structures move without any element straining: a free body's three rigid motions, or a square of bars folding into a parallelogram. Each is a mode at 0 Hz, the null space of K. For a truss, Maxwell's count compares the bars b and the support reactions r with the joints' 2j DOFs: b + r < 2j means a mechanism. It's necessary but not sufficient: a bar can be redundant in one place while another is missing.

Damping

A frame's own damping (its joints, its material) is rarely known element by element, so it's usually put in as Rayleigh damping, C = αM + βK (Damping ζ in the Model box). Each undamped mode then has ζr = (α/ωr + βωr)/2; α and β are chosen to give ζ at the first two modes, so it dips a little between them and grows beyond them, without end: the high modes die fast. Rayleigh damping is proportional, ΦᵀCΦ diagonal: the modes stay those of the undamped frame, each damped on its own. Dampers (between two nodes, to the ground, or on a turn) go into C as their Tᵀc'T, just as springs go into K; they are rarely proportional.

Loads and responses

The Load box puts a force (or a moment on a turn) on one DOF: the arrow. The Response tab plots the receptance H(ω) from it to another DOF (the square), and the response there from rest: to a harmonic force F sin Ωt, an impulse, or a step. Both models are stepped exactly: the load is given states of its own, a constant for a step, a sine and a cosine turning at Ω for a harmonic force, stacked under the structure's A, so one exponential eAh carries both over each step without approximating either; at resonance too, where the closed-form steady state breaks down. (The Simulation page's FOH update is exact for a force that is linear between samples.) An impulse J is the state it leaves: the velocities M⁻¹J at t = 0⁺.

Two formulations

The conventional model takes the undamped modes, K φ = ω²M φ, and keeps them for the damping too: q = Σ φrηr, each mode a single oscillator, η̈r + crη̇r + ωr²ηr = φrᵀf with cr = φrᵀCφr = 2ζrωr. It drops ΦᵀCΦ's off-diagonal terms. The state space keeps everything: x = (q, q̇), ẋ = A x + B f with A = [0 I; −M⁻¹K −M⁻¹C], twice the size, its eigenvalues the damped poles. With proportional damping the two are the same model. With a damper they part, by as much as the damper couples the modes, and most when the modes it couples are close together: a tuned mass damper is the worst case, since its whole point is two close modes and a damper between them.

Complex modes

The state space's eigenvectors ψ are complex: each node has its own phase, so with a damper the nodes no longer pass through zero together (Swing: Damped mode), and the shape's points spread off a line in the complex plane (the Formulations tab; MPC 1 for a real mode, as the Complex modes page measured it). A heavy damper also moves the frequencies, which the conventional model's ζr can't. A DOF with a damper on it and no mass (a torsion damper on a lumped beam's node) has a first-order row, Cq̇ + Kq = f: it adds a real root, a relaxation e−t/τ, not a mode; so does an overdamped mode, two of them.

Things to try

Each of these loads a frame (Things to try, top left). Change it from there: it's yours to edit.

  • Each element knows its own k' and m' in its own axes; Tᵀk'T turns them into the frame's.
  • Assembly adds every element's share at its DOFs: K is sparse, banded and symmetric.
  • Supports strike out rows and columns; so does a DOF no element touches (a truss node's turn).
  • A DOF with no mass follows the rest statically: condensing it out is exact.
  • Consistent mass gives upper bounds that fall as the mesh is refined; lumped mass usually comes out low.
  • Timoshenko's beams shear and have turning inertia: deep beams and high modes come out lower.
  • A mode at 0 Hz is a mechanism (or a rigid motion): Maxwell's count can miss one.
  • Rayleigh damping αM + βK is proportional: the modes stay real, and the conventional model is exact.
  • A damper couples the modes through ΦᵀCΦ's off-diagonal terms: the state space keeps them, the conventional model drops them, worst for close modes (a tuned mass damper).
  • The state space's modes are complex: each node has its own phase (MPC < 1).
  • Giving the load states of its own steps a step or a sine exactly, resonance included.
