Sampling

A transducer's signal is continuous; the analyzer keeps its value fs times a second. A sine of frequency f is pinned down by its samples only if there are more than two per cycle: up to the Nyquist frequency fs/2.

Aliasing

Above Nyquist the samples can't tell a sine at f from one at |f − kfs|: they lie on both. So a tone at f shows at its alias, folded back into 0 … fs/2, with its whole amplitude: 900 Hz sampled at 1024 Hz shows at 124 Hz. Nothing after the sampler can undo this; the data are simply wrong.

The anti-alias filter

So an analog low-pass filter runs before the sampler. A real filter can't cut off at Nyquist like a wall: it rolls off over a band. Analyzers set it to keep fs/2.56 and below (here an 8-pole Butterworth, 3 dB down there and 48 dB per octave beyond), so a tone that could fold back into the kept lines (above fs − fs/2.56) is cut by 31 dB or more; real analyzers use steeper (elliptic) filters, 80 dB or more. The lines above fs/2.56 are thrown away: 1024 samples give 400 good lines, not 512.

Blocks and lines

The DFT of a block of N samples, T = N/fs long, gives lines

Δf = 1/T = fs/N

apart, from 0 to fs/2. The resolution is set by the block's length in time, nothing else: a finer Δf needs a longer block. More samples in the same time (a higher fs) only add lines at the top.

Leakage

The DFT treats the block as one period of a signal that repeats forever. A tone with a whole number of cycles in the block lands on a line and reads exactly. Any other tone, joined end to end, has a jump at each join, and its energy spreads over every line: leakage. It reads low (by up to 3.9 dB half-way between lines, the scalloping loss), and its skirts, falling only as 1/f, can bury a small tone tens of lines away. A peak in an FRF comes out too low and too wide, which reads as too much damping.

Windows

Multiplying the block by a taper that goes smoothly to zero at both ends removes the jump. Each window is a trade, between how wide its main lobe is (how close two tones can be told apart), how low its sidelobes are (how far a big tone leaks), and its scalloping loss (how wrong an amplitude can read):

Hann is the usual choice for random and continuous signals; flat top for reading amplitudes (calibration); none (rectangular) for signals that are periodic in the block, or that die away inside it (a hammer hit, a burst), where a window would cut away the signal itself. The Windows tab draws them.

Amplitude or density

A window changes how much a tone and how much noise come through, differently. Scaled by its coherent gain (the mean of w), the spectrum reads a tone's amplitude: a peak amplitude (or RMS) spectrum, for tones. Scaled by its noise power (Σw²) and fs, it reads noise's density: the PSD, in units²/Hz, the same whatever the block and the window, and its area is the mean square (Parseval). Noise in an amplitude spectrum depends on Δf: each line holds the noise in a band Δf × ENBW wide, so a longer block lowers it, as 1/√N, while a tone stays put. That is how a tone is dug out of noise. A tone in a PSD grows with N instead: read tones as amplitudes and noise as a density.

Averaging

One block's spectrum of noise is itself random: each line's power scatters by 100 %. Averaging n blocks (as power) cuts the scatter to 1/√n, without lowering the level. Blocks may overlap (50 % with Hann): the window throws away the ends of each block, and the overlap uses them in the next, so a record gives nearly twice the averages. Averaging only makes sense for a steady signal; a transient wants one block that holds all of it.

Time and frequency

An averaged spectrum says what frequencies there are, not when. A spectrogram (short-time Fourier transform) takes one spectrum per block as the record goes by: a run-up's orders, a chirp's sweep. A block T long resolves 1/T in frequency and T in time; their product can't go below about 1. The rotor page's waterfall is one.

Transducers

Most vibration sensors are themselves a single oscillator: a seismic mass m on a spring k and a damper c in a case fixed to the structure, read by the mass's motion z relative to the case. The case moving as y eiωt,

z/y = r² / (1 − r² + 2iζr),   r = f/fn,

the Single oscillator page's equation with the case's acceleration as the force. Two regimes:

  • Accelerometer (r ≪ 1, a stiff spring): z ≈ −ÿ/ωn², the mass follows the case and the spring's stretch is proportional to the acceleration. A piezoelectric crystal is the spring, fn tens of kHz, almost undamped: it reads within 5 % up to 0.22 fn. With ζ ≈ 0.7 the flat band would reach 0.6 fn, with the phase nearly proportional to frequency (a pure delay), but piezo crystals can't be damped that much.
  • Seismometer (r ≫ 1, a soft spring): the mass stays still in space, so z ≈ −y, the displacement. A geophone (fn ≈ 4.5 Hz, ζ ≈ 0.7) reads ground displacement from about 1.7 fn up.

Between them, near r = 1, the sensor resonates: a sensor is only good away from its own fn.

Mass loading

A sensor's mass ms joins the structure where it is fixed. A mode whose modal mass there (scaled to a unit motion at that point) is m drops to f √(m/(m + ms)): a 30 g accelerometer on a panel mode of 0.5 kg modal mass lowers it by 2.9 %. The same sensor roved from point to point loads each mode differently, and the modes seem to shift between measurements. Light sensors, a fixed reference, or a test that adds a dummy mass and sees how much the frequency moves, keep it in check.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the signal, the acquisition and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency present, and filter before sampling: an alias can't be removed afterwards.
  • The lines are 1/T apart: resolution comes from the block's length in time.
  • A tone that isn't periodic in the block leaks; a window trades a wider peak for lower leakage (Hann), or for a true amplitude (flat top).
  • Read tones as amplitudes and noise as a PSD; averaging steadies a spectrum but doesn't lower its noise.
  • A seismic sensor reads acceleration below its own fn and displacement above it, and its mass lowers the modes it measures.
