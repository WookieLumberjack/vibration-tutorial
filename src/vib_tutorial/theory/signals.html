Sampling

A transducer's signal is continuous; the analyzer keeps its value fs times a second. A sine of frequency f is pinned down by its samples only if there are more than two per cycle: up to the Nyquist frequency fs/2. That is the sampling theorem nyquist shannon: a signal with nothing at or above fs/2 is fixed completely by its samples.

Aliasing

Above Nyquist the samples can't tell a sine at f from one at f less a whole multiple of fs: they lie on both. So a tone shows at its alias, folded back into 0 … fs/2,

f_{\text{shown}} = \bigl|\,f - \mathrm{round}(f/f_s)\,f_s\bigr|

with its whole amplitude: 900 Hz sampled at 1024 Hz shows at 1024 − 900 = 124 Hz. Nothing after the sampler can undo this; the data are simply wrong.

The anti-alias filter

So an analog low-pass filter runs before the sampler. A real filter can't cut off at Nyquist like a wall: it rolls off over a band. Analyzers set it to keep fs/2.56 and below. Here it is an 8-pole filter of Butterworth's design butterworth, whose gain is

|H_{\text{aa}}| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2.56\,f/f_s)^{16}}}

3 dB down at fs/2.56 and falling 48 dB per octave beyond. The lowest tone that could fold back into the kept lines is at fs − fs/2.56, and it is cut by 30.9 dB; higher ones by more. Real analyzers use steeper (elliptic) filters, 80 dB or more. The lines above fs/2.56 are thrown away: 1024 samples give 400 good lines, not 512.

Blocks and lines

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT; computed by the fast Fourier transform, the FFT cooley-tukey) of a block of N samples, T = N/fs long, gives lines

\Delta f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{f_s}{N}

apart, from 0 to fs/2. The resolution is set by the block's length in time, nothing else: a finer Δf needs a longer block. More samples in the same time (a higher fs) only add lines at the top.

Leakage

The DFT treats the block as one period of a signal that repeats forever. A tone with a whole number of cycles in the block lands on a line and reads exactly. Any other tone, joined end to end, has a jump at each join, and its energy spreads over every line: leakage. It reads low (by up to 3.9 dB half-way between lines, the scalloping loss), and its skirts, falling only as 1/f, can bury a small tone tens of lines away. A peak in an FRF comes out too low and too wide, which reads as too much damping.

Windows

Multiplying the block by a taper w that goes smoothly to zero at both ends removes the jump. Each window is a trade harris-windows, between how wide its main lobe is (how close two tones can be told apart), how low its sidelobes are (how far a big tone leaks), and its scalloping loss (how wrong an amplitude can read):

Hann is the usual choice for random and continuous signals; flat top for reading amplitudes (calibration); none (rectangular) for signals that are periodic in the block, or that die away inside it (a hammer hit, a burst), where a window would cut away the signal itself. The Windows tab draws them.

Amplitude or density

A window changes how much a tone and how much noise come through, differently. Divided by its sum Σw (its coherent gain, the mean of w, times N), the spectrum reads a tone's amplitude: a peak amplitude (or RMS) spectrum, for tones. Divided by its noise power Σw2 and fs, it reads noise's density: the PSD G, in units²/Hz, the same whatever the block and the window. Its area is the mean square (Parseval's theorem parseval). Noise in an amplitude spectrum depends on Δf: each line holds the noise in a band Δf × ENBW wide (the window's equivalent noise bandwidth, in lines), so a longer block lowers it, as 1/√N, while a tone stays put. That is how a tone is dug out of noise. A tone in a PSD grows with N instead: read tones as amplitudes and noise as a density.

Averaging

One block's spectrum of noise is itself random: each line's power scatters by 100 %. Averaging nd blocks (as power, Welch's method welch) cuts the scatter to 1/√nd, without lowering the level. Blocks may overlap (50 % with Hann): the window throws away the ends of each block, and the overlap uses them in the next, so a record gives nearly twice the averages. Averaging only makes sense for a steady signal; a transient wants one block that holds all of it.

Time and frequency

An averaged spectrum says what frequencies there are, not when. A spectrogram (short-time Fourier transform) takes one spectrum per block as the record goes by: a run-up's orders, a chirp's sweep. A block T long resolves Δf = 1/T in frequency and Δt = T in time, so

\Delta f\,\Delta t \approx 1

and no block does better on both. The rotor page's waterfall is one.

Wavelets

A spectrogram uses one block length for every frequency, so it resolves 10 Hz and 1000 Hz equally well in hertz: too finely in time at high frequencies, too coarsely at low ones for most signals. A wavelet transform grossmann-morlet instead correlates the signal with one short wave shape, a wavelet (a few cycles of a sine under a smooth envelope, Morlet's), stretched to each frequency. At every frequency it holds the same number of cycles, so its band is a fixed fraction of the frequency (a constant Δf/f, as an octave band is) and its length in time is that many periods. High frequencies are placed sharply in time, low ones sharply in frequency. That suits transients and impacts, a gear's tooth fault, or a run-up spread over decades of frequency. Δf Δt ≈ 1 still holds at each frequency; the wavelet only chooses how to spend it. (The Shock spectrum page's wavelets, shaped to match a shock's spectrum, are the same idea used to make a signal rather than to analyse one.)

Digital filters

Once the signal is sampled, filtering is arithmetic on the samples. An FIR (finite impulse response) filter is a weighted moving sum of the last few samples; it can be made to have a phase that is a pure delay, which keeps a waveform's shape. An IIR (infinite impulse response) filter also feeds back its own past outputs: a Butterworth filter (the bilinear transform of the analog one) gets a steep roll-off from a few coefficients, at the price of a phase that varies with frequency and distorts transients. Run forwards and then backwards over a stored record, an IIR filter's phase cancels: zero-phase filtering, for analysis after the fact, not in real time.

Digital filters do three common jobs in vibration work:

  • Band limits: a high-pass to take out a sensor's drift or a DC offset, a low-pass to keep a band, a band-pass around one order.
  • Decimation: to lower the sample rate by a whole factor, say 4, low-pass digitally at the new fs/(2.56 × 4) first, then keep every 4th sample. Skipping the filter aliases exactly as an analog sampler does.
  • Weighting: standard frequency weightings for human vibration and sound (ISO 2631's and the A weighting) are filters too.

From acceleration to velocity and displacement

Accelerometers measure ẍ, but limits are often in velocity (machinery, mm/s) or displacement (clearances). For a tone at angular frequency Ω, integrating is dividing:

\dot x = \frac{\ddot x}{i\Omega}, \qquad x = \frac{\ddot x}{(i\Omega)^2} = -\frac{\ddot x}{\Omega^2}

In a spectrum that is exact, line by line. In the time domain each integration weights low frequencies up, by 1/Ω and then 1/Ω2. An offset or drift that the accelerometer adds near 0 Hz then grows without bound. A constant error of only 0.01 m/s² becomes a velocity of 0.1 m/s after 10 s, and a displacement of ½ × 0.01 × 10² = 0.5 m. So a record is high-passed before each integration (and its mean taken out), with the corner below the lowest frequency that matters: what lies below it is lost, but it was not to be trusted anyway.

Decibels and bands

Vibration spans many decades, so levels are often given in decibels: ten times the log of a ratio of powers, or twenty times the log of a ratio of amplitudes (power goes as amplitude squared). An acceleration level is

L_a = 20\log_{10}\frac{a_{\text{RMS}}}{a_{\text{ref}}}\ \ \text{dB}, \qquad a_{\text{ref}} = 1\ \text{µm/s}^2

the reference the ISO standard prefers iso-1683 (1 nm/s for velocity, 1 pm for displacement); a level means nothing without its reference. A factor of 10 in amplitude is 20 dB, of 2 is 6.02 dB.

Acoustics and many vibration standards also report the spectrum in octave or one-third-octave bands rather than lines iec-61260: each band's level is the mean square in it, the PSD's area between its edges. Bands are equally wide on a log scale. An octave's edges are a factor of 100.3 ≈ 2 apart, a third's 100.1 ≈ 1.26, around a mid-band frequency fm: 1000 Hz times a whole power of 100.3 (of 100.1 for thirds):

f_{\text{lo}} = f_m \times 10^{-0.15}, \quad f_{\text{hi}} = f_m \times 10^{0.15} \quad \text{(octaves)}

with ±0.05 in the exponents for thirds. The bands are named by rounded mid-band frequencies (63, 125, 250 Hz …). A band's width grows with its frequency: a constant Δf/f, like a wavelet's, which suits broad spectra and the ear but blurs a tone's exact frequency.

Transducers

Most vibration sensors are themselves a single oscillator: a seismic mass m on a spring k and a damper c in a case fixed to the structure, read by the mass's motion z relative to the case. The case moving as y eiΩt,

\frac{z}{y} = \frac{r^2}{1 - r^2 + 2i\zeta r}, \qquad r = \frac{f}{f_n}

the Single oscillator page's equation with the case's acceleration as the force. Two regimes:

  • Accelerometer (r ≪ 1, a stiff spring): z ≈ −ÿ/ωn2, the mass follows the case and the spring's stretch is proportional to the acceleration. A piezoelectric crystal is the spring, fn tens of kHz, almost undamped: it reads within 5 % up to 0.22 fn. With ζ ≈ 0.7 the flat band would reach 0.6 fn, with the phase nearly proportional to frequency (a pure delay), but piezo crystals can't be damped that much.
  • Seismometer (r ≫ 1, a soft spring): the mass stays still in space, so z ≈ −y, the displacement. A geophone (fn ≈ 4.5 Hz, ζ ≈ 0.7) reads ground displacement from about 1.7 fn up.

Between them, near r = 1, the sensor resonates: a sensor is only good away from its own fn.

Mass loading

A sensor's mass ms joins the structure where it is fixed. A mode whose modal mass there (scaled to a unit motion at that point) is m drops to

f' = f\sqrt{\frac{m}{m + m_s}}

so a 30 g accelerometer on a panel mode of 0.5 kg modal mass lowers it by 2.9 %. The same sensor roved from point to point loads each mode differently, and the modes seem to shift between measurements. Light sensors, a fixed reference, or a test that adds a dummy mass and sees how much the frequency moves, keep it in check.

Worked example

The page opens on two tones, 1 m/s² at 50 Hz and 0.1 m/s² at 120 Hz, sampled at fs = 1024 Hz through the anti-alias filter, in one block of N = 1024 under a Hann window. Its readouts:

  1. Lines. T = 1024/1024 = 1 s, so Δf = 1 Hz, up to Nyquist at 512 Hz; the filter keeps fs/2.56 = 400 Hz, 400 good lines.
  2. The filter. At 120 Hz, 2.56f/fs = 0.3 and 0.316 = 4.3 × 10−9: no loss. At 400 Hz the gain is 1/√2, −3 dB. A tone at 1024 − 400 = 624 Hz, the lowest that could fold into the kept band, has 1.5616 ≈ 1230 under the root: gain 0.0285, −30.9 dB.
  3. No leakage. 50 and 120 Hz fit whole cycles in the 1 s block, so each lands on a line. Hann's mean is 0.5, and dividing by Σw = 512 makes them read 1.000 and 0.100 (+0.00 dB). Hann spreads each over three lines: 49 and 51 Hz read 0.500, 119 and 121 Hz 0.050.
  4. Parseval. The mean square is ½ × 1² + ½ × 0.1² = 0.505 (m/s²)², and so is the PSD's area. As a PSD (Scale), the 50 Hz tone's power, 0.5, is spread over Hann's noise bandwidth of 1.5 lines, 1.5 Hz: its line reads 0.5/1.5 = 0.333 (m/s²)²/Hz.
  5. Levels. The 50 Hz tone's RMS is 0.707 m/s²: La = 20 log10(0.707 × 106) = 117.0 dB re 1 µm/s²; the 120 Hz tone, a tenth of it, 97.0 dB. In thirds, 50 Hz falls in the 50 Hz band (44.7–56.2 Hz) and 120 Hz in the 125 Hz band (112.2–141.3 Hz).
  6. Integrated. The 50 Hz tone is a velocity of 1/(2π × 50) = 3.18 mm/s and a displacement of 1/(2π × 50)2 = 10.1 µm; the 120 Hz tone 0.133 mm/s and 0.176 µm. Twice integrated, the smaller, higher tone falls from a tenth of the other to a 58th: (120/50)2 = 5.76 times further.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the signal, the acquisition and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency present, and filter before sampling: an alias can't be removed afterwards.
  • The lines are 1/T apart: resolution comes from the block's length in time.
  • A tone that isn't periodic in the block leaks; a window trades a wider peak for lower leakage (Hann), or for a true amplitude (flat top).
  • Read tones as amplitudes and noise as a PSD; averaging steadies a spectrum but doesn't lower its noise.
  • A spectrogram has one resolution at every frequency; a wavelet's (and an octave band's) is a fixed fraction of the frequency.
  • Filter digitally before decimating, and high-pass before integrating: each integration multiplies low-frequency errors.
  • A level in dB means nothing without its reference: 1 µm/s² for acceleration.
  • A seismic sensor reads acceleration below its own fn and displacement above it, and its mass lowers the modes it measures.
