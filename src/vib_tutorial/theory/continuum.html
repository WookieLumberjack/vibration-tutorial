From a chain to a continuum

Cut a string, a bar or a beam of length L into N equal elements of length h = L/N and it becomes a chain of masses and springs, with about N modes where the continuum has infinitely many. A string under tension T, with mass μ per length, becomes a string of beads: masses μh on massless pieces of string, each pulling sideways like a spring of T/h. A bar (Young's modulus E, section A, density ρ) becomes the first page's chain, k = EA/h and m = ρAh. How good are their modes? The clearest way to compare them with the continuum is through the waves each can carry.

The continuum's waves

A short piece dx of string, deflected w(x, t), is pulled by the tension at each end along the string's slope. The sideways force is T times the slope at one end minus at the other, T wxx dx (the subscripts are derivatives), and it accelerates the piece's mass μ dx. A bar's piece, moving u(x, t) along its length, is pushed by the stress Eux on its ends the same way. Both give the wave equation dalembert:

\mu\,w_{tt} = T\,w_{xx}, \qquad \rho A\,u_{tt} = EA\,u_{xx}

A wave ei(κx − ωt) of wavenumber κ = 2π/λ solves it when ω = cκ, with

c = \sqrt{T/\mu} \quad\text{or}\quad c = \sqrt{E/\rho}

Every wavelength travels at the same speed: the medium is non-dispersive, so a pulse keeps its shape. D'Alembert's solution says so directly: any w = r(x − ct) + l(x + ct) solves it, a shape running right and another running left, unchanged.

A beam's bending euler-1744 is resisted by its stiffness EI (I the section's second moment of area), through the fourth derivative:

\rho A\,w_{tt} = -EI\,w_{xxxx}, \qquad \omega = a\kappa^2, \quad a = \sqrt{EI/\rho A}

Short waves outrun long ones (the beam is dispersive), and a pulse spreads into ripples.

A mode is a standing wave that fits the ends. Fixed at both ends, a whole number of half wavelengths fits: κn = nπ/L and fn = nc/2L, every overtone a whole multiple (a guitar string). Fixed at one end and free at the other, an odd number of quarters: fn = (2n − 1)c/4L, 1 : 3 : 5. A beam's ends give a transcendental equation for β = κ, for a cantilever

\cos\beta L\,\cosh\beta L = -1

whose roots are βnL = 1.875, 4.694, 7.855, …, and mode n is at aβn2 rad/s, so its overtones are no multiples at all: 1 : 6.27 : 17.5.

The chain's waves

On an endless chain of masses m = μh joined by springs k = T/h, mass j obeys

m\,\ddot x_j = k\,(x_{j+1} - 2x_j + x_{j-1})

Try the continuum's wave sampled at the masses, xj = ei(κjh − ωt). The neighbours are the same wave a step h along, so the bracket is (2 cos κh − 2)xj = −4 sin2(κh/2) xj, and with k/m = c2/h2 brillouin,

\omega = \frac{2c}{h}\,\sin\frac{\kappa h}{2}

For long waves (κh ≪ 1) the sine is its argument and ω = cκ: the chain is the continuum. Shorter waves go slower, and at κh = π, two elements a wavelength, the curve tops out at 2c/h: the chain's cut-off. Nothing faster can travel on it, and its group velocity, the speed a pulse's energy travels at,

c_g = \frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}\kappa} = c\,\cos\frac{\kappa h}{2}

falls to zero there. A wave beyond κ = π/h looks, at the masses, exactly like a longer one (aliasing): the chain can't tell them apart.

A chain with ends has the same modes as the continuum in shape: the sampled standing waves, at the same κn. Its frequencies are the dispersion curve's at those κn, so the low modes come out nearly right, and the high ones, crowding towards κ = π/h, far too low. Expanding the sine, the lumped string's error is

\frac{\sin(\kappa h/2)}{\kappa h/2} - 1 \approx -\frac{(\kappa h)^2}{24}

to begin with, and −36 % at the cut-off (2/π − 1).

Lumped and consistent masses

Lumping is one way to share an element's mass out: half to each end. The finite element method's consistent mass archer takes it from the same straight-line shape functions as the stiffness, ψ1 = 1 − ξ and ψ2 = ξ, ξ the fraction of the way along it: each entry is ρAh times the integral over ξ of two of them multiplied (∫ψ12dξ = 1/3, ∫ψ1ψ2dξ = 1/6):

M_e = \frac{\rho A h}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad K_e = \frac{EA}{h}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}

The mesh is then a Rayleigh–Ritz approximation ritz (its shapes the straight-line pieces), and every frequency is an upper bound. Each mass now feels its neighbours' accelerations too, and the same sampled wave gives

\omega^2 = \frac{c^2}{h^2}\,\frac{2(1 - \cos\kappa h)}{1 - (1 - \cos\kappa h)/3}

which is high by (κh)2/24 for long waves: the same as lumping's error, the other way. Their average, the mixed mass, cancels it, and its error goes as (κh)4. A mode's κh = nπ/N, so halving the elements' length cuts the lumped or consistent error 4 times (N−2) and the mixed 16 times (N−4): the Errors tab's convergence plot shows the slopes, p = 2 or 4.

A beam's elements are cubics in its deflection (Hermite's), with a deflection and a slope at each node. Its consistent mass is far better than its lumped one (N−4 against N−2), and here averaging only halves the lumped error. With two unknowns a node, the endless mesh has two waves for each κ: the bending wave, and a second branch far above it in which the slopes swing against the deflections, which the continuum doesn't have. Lumped, the slopes carry no mass, and are condensed out exactly.

Waves and reflections

The Waves tab runs a pulse along the medium and the mesh side by side. On a string or bar the continuum's pulse keeps its shape, and comes back from a fixed end upside down (the end must stay still, so the reflection cancels it there) and from a free end upright (doubling there). On the mesh the pulse is made of waves the mesh carries at their own speeds: lumped, its short waves go slow, and the pulse leaves ripples behind it; consistent, they go fast and the ripples lead. Nothing shorter than two elements travels at all. With few elements across the pulse, it falls apart.

What a wave meets reflects some of it, set by the impedance Z, the force a wave needs per unit of velocity. For a wave r(x − ct) on a string the sideways force is −Twx = −Tr′ and the velocity −cr′, so

Z = T/c = \sqrt{T\mu} \quad\text{(string)}, \qquad Z = \rho c A \quad\text{(bar)}

A dashpot cd on the end at x = L must take the string's force, −Twx = cdwt. With an incoming r and an outgoing l = Rr there, the incoming wave pushes with Z times its velocity and the outgoing with −Z times its own, so Z(1 − R) = cd(1 + R):

R = \frac{Z - c_d}{Z + c_d}

cd = Z is a matched, non-reflecting end, which absorbs the wave as an endless medium would carry it off; cd → ∞ is a fixed end (R = −1), cd = 0 a free one (R = 1). A step from Z1 to Z2 is the same, with the second medium as the dashpot. Its deflection and force must match across the joint, and that gives

R = \frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}, \qquad \tau = \frac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2}

upside down into something heavier. The energy is shared R2 : τ2Z2/Z1, which add to 1.

The mesh's impedance is the continuum's only for long waves: a dashpot matched to the continuum reflects the mesh's short ones. That is why an absorbing boundary on a model is never quite silent, and why the mesh's high modes, the short waves, are the ones not to trust.

A beam's bending waves are dispersive on the continuum too, ω = aκ2: a bump released on it spreads into ripples. Their group velocity 2aκ is twice their phase speed aκ, so the short ones run ahead at twice the speed of waves twice as long.

Worked example

The page opens on the guitar's low E string: L = 0.648 m at T = 73 N, with μ = 6.40 g/m, cut into 12 lumped, consistent and mixed elements. Its wave speed and fundamental are

c = \sqrt{73/0.0064} = 106.8\ \text{m/s}, \qquad f_1 = \frac{c}{2L} = 82.41\ \text{Hz}

The elements are h = 0.054 m long, and mode n has κnh = nπ/12. Mode 1, 24 elements a wavelength, has κh = 0.262, so the lumped mesh is (κh)2/24 = 0.29 % low: 82.17 Hz, and the consistent 0.29 % high, 82.64 Hz. The mixed mesh's 82.41 Hz is out by 0.001 %. At mode 6, κh = π/2 and four elements a wavelength, the lumped mode is

f_6 = 6f_1\,\frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{\pi/4} = 494.4 \times 0.900 = 445\ \text{Hz}

10.0 % low; the consistent is 10.3 % high and the mixed 1.4 % low. The 12 elements, held at both ends, have 11 free nodes and so 11 modes. The lumped cut-off is c/πh = 629.5 Hz, and its mode 11 sits just below it at 624.2 Hz, 31 % under the string's 906.5 Hz. The impedance is √(Tμ) = 0.684 N·s/m: the dashpot that would end this string without an echo.

Things to try

Pick a preset at the top of the left column:

  • A mesh is a low-pass filter on the continuum: it carries the long waves correctly, its short ones too slowly (lumped) or too fast (consistent), and nothing shorter than two elements.
  • Its low modes are good and its top modes are artefacts of the mesh: ten elements a wavelength (κh ≈ 0.63) keeps a string's lumped error under 2 %, and the rule of thumb is 6 to 10.
  • A consistent mass makes the mesh Rayleigh–Ritz (upper bounds); mixing lumped and consistent cancels the leading error.
  • Waves reflect at every change of impedance; a matched dashpot absorbs them.
