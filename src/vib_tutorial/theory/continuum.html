Undamped normal modes Estimating a frequency from a guessed shape

From a chain to a continuum

Cut a string, a bar or a beam into N equal elements of length h and it becomes a chain of masses and springs, with N or so modes where the continuum has infinitely many. How good are they? A string becomes a string of beads and a bar becomes the first page's chain (k = EA/h, m = ρAh), and the clearest way to compare them with the continuum is through the waves each can carry.

The continuum's waves

A string under tension T (mass μ per length) and a bar (EA, ρA) obey one equation, the wave equation:

μ wtt = T wxx,   ρA utt = EA uxx.

A wave ei(κx − ωt) solves it when ω = cκ, with c = √(T/μ) or √(E/ρ): every wavelength travels at the same speed (the medium is non-dispersive), so a pulse keeps its shape. A beam's bending, ρA wtt = −EI wxxxx, gives ω = aκ² with a = √(EI/ρA): short waves outrun long ones (dispersive), and a pulse spreads into ripples.

A mode is a standing wave that fits the ends. Fixed at both ends, a whole number of half wavelengths fits: κn = nπ/L and fn = n c / 2L, every overtone a whole multiple (a guitar string). Fixed at one end and free at the other, an odd number of quarters: 1 : 3 : 5. A beam's ends give a transcendental equation, cos βL cosh βL = −1 for a cantilever, so its overtones are no multiples at all (1 : 6.27 : 17.5).

The chain's waves

On an endless chain of masses m = μh joined by springs k = T/h, try the same wave sampled at the nodes, xj = ei(κjh − ωt). One mass's equation gives

ω = (2c/h) sin(κh/2).

For long waves (κh ≪ 1) the sine is its argument and ω = cκ: the chain is the continuum. Shorter waves go slower, and at κh = π, two elements a wavelength, the curve tops out at 2c/h: the chain's cut-off. Nothing faster can travel on it, and its group velocity dω/dκ = c cos(κh/2), the speed a pulse's energy travels at, falls to zero there. A wave beyond κ = π/h looks, at the nodes, exactly like a longer one (aliasing): the chain can't tell them apart.

A chain with ends has the same modes as the continuum in shape: the sampled standing waves, at the same κn. Its frequencies are the dispersion curve's at those κn, so the low modes come out nearly right, and the high ones, crowding towards κ = π/h, far too low: the lumped string's error is −(κh)²/24 to begin with, and −36 % at the cut-off.

Lumped and consistent masses

Lumping is one way to share an element's mass out: half to each end. The finite element method's consistent mass takes it from the same straight-line shape functions as the stiffness, ρAh/6 [2 1; 1 2]: the mesh is then a Rayleigh–Ritz approximation, and every frequency is an upper bound. Its dispersion is

ω² = (c/h)² · 2(1 − cos κh) / (1 − (1 − cos κh)/3),

which is high by (κh)²/24 for long waves: the same as lumping's error, the other way. Their average, the mixed mass, cancels it, and its error goes as (κh)⁴. Halve the elements' length and the lumped or consistent error falls 4 times (N−2), the mixed 16 times (N−4): the Errors tab's convergence plot shows the slopes.

A beam's elements are cubics in its deflection, with a deflection and a slope at each node. Its consistent mass is far better than its lumped one (N−4 against N−2), and here averaging only halves the lumped error. With two unknowns a node the endless mesh has two waves for each κ: the bending wave, and a second branch far above it in which the slopes swing against the deflections, which the continuum doesn't have. Lumped, the slopes carry no mass, and are condensed out exactly.

Waves and reflections

The Waves tab runs a pulse along the medium and the mesh side by side. On a string or bar the continuum's pulse keeps its shape, u = r(x − ct) + l(x + ct), and comes back from a fixed end upside down (the end must stay still, so the reflection cancels it there) and from a free end upright (doubling there). On the mesh the pulse is made of waves the mesh carries at their own speeds: lumped, its short waves go slow, and the pulse leaves ripples behind it; consistent, they go fast and the ripples lead. Nothing shorter than two elements travels at all. Few elements across the pulse and it falls apart.

What a wave meets reflects some of it, by the impedance Z = √(Tμ) (string) or ρcA (bar), the force a wave needs per unit of velocity. A dashpot of cd at an end returns (Z − cd)/(Z + cd) of the wave: cd = Z is a matched, non-reflecting end, which absorbs the wave as an endless medium would carry it off. A step from Z₁ to Z₂ returns (Z₁ − Z₂)/(Z₁ + Z₂), upside down into something heavier, and passes 2Z₁/(Z₁ + Z₂). The energy is shared R² : T²Z₂/Z₁.

The mesh's impedance is the continuum's only for long waves: a dashpot matched to the continuum reflects the mesh's short ones. That is why an absorbing boundary on a model is never quite silent, and why the mesh's high modes, the short waves, are the ones not to trust.

A beam's bending waves are dispersive on the continuum too, ω = aκ²: a bump released on it spreads into ripples, the short ones running ahead at twice the speed of waves twice as long.

  • A mesh is a low-pass filter on the continuum: it carries the long waves correctly, its short ones too slowly (lumped) or too fast (consistent), and nothing shorter than two elements.
  • Its low modes are good and its top modes are artefacts of the mesh: ten elements a wavelength (κh ≈ 0.63) keeps a string's lumped error under 2 %, and the rule of thumb is 6 to 10.
  • A consistent mass makes the mesh Rayleigh–Ritz (upper bounds); mixing lumped and consistent cancels the leading error.
  • Waves reflect at every change of impedance; a matched dashpot absorbs them.
