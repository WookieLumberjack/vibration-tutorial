Radiation efficiency

A vibrating surface pushes the air next to it, and some of that push leaves as sound. How much depends on the motion's pattern as well as its size, so the radiated sound power is written

W = \rho c\, S\, \sigma \langle v^2 \rangle

with ρc the air's characteristic impedance (415 rayl), S the area, ⟨v2⟩ the mean-square velocity averaged over the surface and over time, and σ the radiation efficiency: 1 for a large piston, which pushes a plane wave off at ρc per unit velocity, and far less for a panel whose neighbouring patches move opposite ways, as most of the air they push just sloshes from one to the next. σ is often quoted in decibels, 10 log σ (the radiation index).

Levels and bands

Sound spans too many powers of ten to plot on a linear scale, so it is given as a level in decibels, ten times the logarithm of a power ratio: the sound power level and the sound pressure level

L_W = 10 \log_{10} \frac{W}{10^{-12}\ \text{W}}, \qquad L_p = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\langle p^2 \rangle}{(2\times 10^{-5}\ \text{Pa})^2}

the references being about the quietest sound a person hears at 1 kHz. A pressure, being an amplitude, gains 20 dB per factor of ten; a power 10. Doubling a power adds 3 dB, and two equal, unrelated sources together are 3 dB louder than one. Spectra are given in bands of constant relative width, as an ear hears: an octave doubles the frequency, a third-octave is a third of one (a width of 23 % of its centre, the centres 1000, 1250, 1600, 2000 Hz …), and a band's level is the power in it, the PSD's area over the band (the Random vibration page's). This page's averages are over third-octaves.

Coincidence

A free bending wave on a plate has the wavenumber kb = √ω (ρh/D)1/4: it is dispersive, its speed ω/kb growing as √f, while sound's k = ω/c grows in proportion to f. The two meet at the critical frequency

f_c = \frac{c^2}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{\rho h}{D}}

which for a plate of thickness h is about 12/h kHz·mm in steel, aluminium or glass (D ∝ h3, so fc ∝ 1/h). Above it, the bending wave is supersonic: it drives a plane sound wave at the angle θ where its trace matches,

\sin\theta = \frac{k}{k_b} = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_b}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - f_c/f}}

the second an infinite plate's efficiency: large just above fc (the wave leaves near grazing) and 1 above that. Below fc the wave is subsonic: the air above each crest cancels the air above the trough beside it, and an infinite plate radiates nothing at all. A finite one still radiates from where the cancellation is incomplete: its edges and its corners.

A mode's radiation

A simply supported panel's mode (m, n) is sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b), the sum of four plane bending waves with kx = mπ/a, ky = nπ/b. Set in a rigid baffle (so that the air behind can't reach round the edge) its radiated power is the far field's integral over a hemisphere, which reduces to wallace

\sigma_{mn} = \frac{64 k^2 ab}{\pi^6 m^2 n^2} \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \bigl[A_m(\alpha)\,B_n(\gamma)\bigr]^2 \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi

with α = ka sin θ cos φ, γ = kb sin θ sin φ, and Am = cos(α/2) (m odd) or sin(α/2) (m even) over (α/mπ)2 − 1, Bn likewise. The page works it out numerically. Comparing k with the mode's own wavenumbers sorts the modes:

  • Corner modes (k < kx and k < ky): every half wave cancels its neighbour but for the quarter cells at the corners. Far below a mode's own resonance, σmn ≈ 32k2ab/π5m2n2 for odd m and n, a monopole's k2: the panel pumps like a small piston. An even index cancels that too, leaving a dipole's two more powers of k, and two even indices a quadrupole's four.
  • Edge modes (k between kx and ky): along the longer half waves nothing cancels, so the two edges across the shorter ones radiate like strips.
  • Surface modes (√(kx2 + ky2) < k): the mode is above its own coincidence, and the whole surface radiates, σmn ≈ 1.

At its own resonance a mode is on the bending wave's curve, kmn = kb, so below fc the resonant modes are all corner or edge modes, and above it all surface modes.

Averaged over a band

From a few hundred hertz up a panel has many modes in each third-octave band (Beams & plates' constant (S/2)√(ρh/D) per hertz), and what matters is their average. Maidanik's formula maidanik, as Crocker and Price corrected it crocker-price, gives the mean σ of the resonant modes of a baffled, simply supported panel of perimeter P from the wavelength at coincidence λc = c/fc:

  • below fc, the edge modes (g2, growing with the perimeter) and below fc/2 the corner modes (g1), with ε = √(f/fc):
\sigma = \frac{\lambda_c P}{S}\,g_2(\varepsilon) + \frac{2\lambda_c^2}{S}\,g_1(\varepsilon)
  • near fc, a peak of about √(a/λc) + √(b/λc);
  • above, the infinite plate's 1/√(1 − fc/f).

The page sets it beside the mean of Wallace's σmn over the modes resonant in each band, which it follows to within a dB or two wherever a band holds a few modes. Below fc a panel radiates more the longer its edges for its area: stiffening ribs, which add edges, make a panel louder.

A point-driven panel

Drive the panel with a force F at one point. Each mode is a single oscillator of mass M/4 (the mean of its shape squared), driven by Fφmn(x0, y0), with the loss factor η in its stiffness. The power going in, ½F Re v(x0, y0), peaks at each resonance, but averaged over a band it settles, from where several modes share each band, to an infinite plate's:

P_{\text{in}} = \tfrac12 F^2\, \mathrm{Re}\, Y_\infty, \qquad Y_\infty = \frac{1}{8\sqrt{D\rho h}}

a pure resistance: a wave carried off and never returned. In the band, that power is lost in the panel as fast as it comes in, ωηE = Pin, so the panel's energy E = M⟨v2⟩ is Pin/ωη: the response of a panel with many modes in a band depends on its mass, its stiffness and its damping, not on its shape or where it's driven. This is the starting point of statistical energy analysis. The radiated power is each mode's ρcSσmn⟨vmn2⟩ (the page leaves out the modes' mutual radiation, which is small once a band holds several). Written as a loss, ρcSσ⟨v2⟩ = ωηradE with the radiation loss factor

\eta_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\rho c\,\sigma}{\omega m''}

so the panel's energy leaves by two routes, its damping and its sound, and the sound takes ηrad/(η + ηrad) of it. In air ηrad is small, 10−4 or so below fc for a steel sheet, so sound is a small leak from a panel's energy, a larger one above fc where σ ≈ 1. A heavy fluid (water) changes that, and loads the panel as an added mass too.

Between two rooms

A panel between two rooms is pushed by the sound on one side and radiates on the other. As an infinite plate of mass m″ per area, a plane wave arriving at θ sees the wall impedance Z and gets through with the transmission coefficient τ(θ):

Z = \mathrm{i}\omega m''\Bigl[1 - \Bigl(\frac{f}{f_c}\Bigr)^2 \sin^4\theta\,(1 + \mathrm{i}\eta)\Bigr] \tau(\theta) = \frac{1}{|1 + Z\cos\theta/2\rho c|^2}, \qquad \text{TL} = 10\log_{10}\frac{1}{\bar\tau}

the transmission loss, with τ̄ averaged over the angles the sound arrives from.

  • The mass law. Well below fc, only the mass holds the sound back: at normal incidence TL = 10 log[1 + (ωm″/2ρc)2] ≈ 20 log(fm″) − 42 dB, 6 dB more for each doubling of the frequency or the mass. Averaged over the angles a room's sound arrives from (field incidence, up to 78°) it is about 5 dB less.
  • Coincidence. Above fc, at the angle where sin2θ = fc/f, the sound's trace matches the bending wave: the panel's stiffness cancels its mass, only damping is left, and the sound walks through. The diffuse field's TL dips at fc, and above it rises with the damping, about 10 log(2ηf/πfc) on the mass law cremer-heckl.

Since fc ∝ 1/h while m″ ∝ h, fcm″ depends only on the material: a heavier panel of the same stuff gains on the mass law but brings its dip lower, into the band it was meant to stop. Laminated glass's damping layer fills the dip in; a double wall beats the mass law outright. Its two leaves, m″1 and m″2 a gap d apart, bounce on the air between them as on a spring of stiffness ρc2/d per area, at the mass–air–mass resonance

f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\rho c^2}{d}\Bigl(\frac{1}{m''_1} + \frac{1}{m''_2}\Bigr)}

where the wall lets more through than one leaf would. Above it each leaf isolates the other, as a mount isolates a machine above its natural frequency, and the TL climbs 18 dB an octave instead of 6, until the gap's own standing waves (and the studs that bridge it) cap it: two 10 kg/m² plasterboards 100 mm apart resonate at 85 Hz, below speech. Absorbent in the gap damps the cavity; it is a structure–cavity coupling, the subject of the SEA below.

Two oscillators

A panel with hundreds of modes in a band, joined to another and to a room, is too much to follow mode by mode, and its details (exactly where each resonance falls) are not known well enough to be worth following. Statistical energy analysis (SEA) follows energy instead, and rests on one exact result. Take two oscillators, m1 and m2 with blocked natural frequencies ω1 and ω2 (each with the other held still) and damping rates Δ = c/m = 2ζω, joined by a spring kc, and drive each with its own white noise, independent of the other's.

The power through the spring, averaged over time, is

P_{12} = \beta\,(E_1 - E_2)

E = m⟨v2⟩ each oscillator's energy (twice its kinetic energy, which is its total on average), with

\beta = \frac{(k_c^2/m_1 m_2)(\Delta_1 + \Delta_2)}{(\omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2)^2 + (\Delta_1 + \Delta_2)(\Delta_1\omega_2^2 + \Delta_2\omega_1^2)}

whatever the forces' strengths: power flows from the oscillator with more energy to the one with less, in proportion to the difference, as heat flows between two bodies at different temperatures. This is Lyon and Maidanik's result lyon-maidanik, and it holds for any strength of coupling. The page works the response out exactly (the covariance of the oscillators' motion, from a Lyapunov equation) and sets β beside it. β is largest when the oscillators are tuned, over a band as wide as their damping rates: energy crosses between pairs of modes whose resonances overlap.

Statistical energy analysis

Sum the two-oscillator result over every pair of modes of two subsystems with resonances in a band, each mode of subsystem i holding the same energy Ei/Ni (Ni = niΔf its modes in the band of width Δf, ni its modal density). The flow between the subsystems becomes

P_{ij} = \omega\,(\eta_{ij} E_i - \eta_{ji} E_j)

with the coupling loss factors ηij (the share of subsystem i's energy that leaves for j in a cycle, over 2π, as its own loss factor ηi is the share it dissipates) obeying reciprocity,

n_i\,\eta_{ij} = n_j\,\eta_{ji}

so Pij = ωηijni(Ei/ni − Ej/nj): energy flows down the difference in modal energy, energy per mode, which plays temperature's part. In each band every subsystem balances the power Pi it is given with what it dissipates and what it sends on,

P_i = \omega\eta_i E_i + \sum_j \omega\,(\eta_{ij} E_i - \eta_{ji} E_j)

a small linear system for the energies. The assumptions are the two-oscillator result's, made statistical: several modes in each band, so that their averages mean something; the input spread evenly over the band; and coupling weak enough that each subsystem's modes are its own. The answers are band averages: what a set of nominally identical structures would give on average, not what one of them does at one frequency lyon-dejong.

Plates at a corner

Where two plates meet at a right angle, each plate's in-plane stiffness stops the other's bending at the corner: the line doesn't move, but it turns, both plates turning with it, and their bending moments balance. A bending wave arriving on plate 1 at θ from the normal shares its trace along the corner, ky = k1 sin θ, with every wave it makes: a reflected wave and a transmitted one, and a near field on each side that decays from the corner.

The four conditions give their four amplitudes, and the transmitted wave carries τ(θ) of the energy, the reflected one the rest (τ + |r|2 = 1). Identical plates pass half at normal incidence; a plate much thicker or thinner reflects most. Averaged over a diffuse field's directions (its intensity at the corner goes as cos θ),

\bar\tau_{12} = \int_0^{\pi/2} \tau(\theta)\cos\theta\,d\theta

(a third for identical plates), and the energy crossing a corner of length L each second, out of the energy E1 spread over S1, gives the coupling loss factor

\eta_{12} = \frac{c_{g1} L\,\bar\tau_{12}}{\pi\omega S_1}

cg = 2√(ω√(D/ρh)) being the bending wave's group speed, twice its phase speed cremer-heckl. Reciprocity holds because k1τ̄12 = k2τ̄21. A plate's modal density, (S/2)√(ρh/D) modes per hertz, is Beams & plates'. A plate couples to a room by its radiation, with ηrad = ρcσ/ωm″ from the driven panel.

A room

A room of volume V has 4πf2V/c3 modes per hertz, many more than a panel from a few hundred hertz up, so by reciprocity it gives back far less than it takes. Its sound decays at a rate set by what its surfaces absorb, measured as the reverberation time T60, the time it takes to fall 60 dB once the source stops. Sabine's rule sabine relates it to the absorption A (in m², each surface's area times the share of the sound it absorbs), and it sets the room's loss factor:

T_{60} = \frac{0.161\,V}{A}, \qquad \eta_{\text{room}} = \frac{2.2}{f\,T_{60}}

The room's energy E = ⟨p2⟩V/ρc2 gives the sound pressure level; for a source of power W in it, the balance W = ωηroomE gives ⟨p2⟩ = 4ρcW/A, so Lp = LW + 10 log(4/A): a room twice as absorbent is 3 dB quieter.

Equipartition

For two subsystems with only 1 driven, subsystem 2's balance gives

\frac{E_2}{E_1} = \frac{\eta_{12}}{\eta_2 + \eta_{21}}

If subsystem 2's damping is small next to η21, it can only lose energy by giving it back, and E2/n2 → E1/n1: the modal energies even out, equipartition, as two bodies in contact come to one temperature. If it is large, subsystem 2 drains what it is sent. Damping the receiving structure helps only while its damping is comparable with the coupling; damping the source, or cutting the coupling (a resilient joint, a mismatch in thickness), works whatever the receiver.

Worked example

The page opens on a steel panel 600 × 400 mm, 3 mm thick, η = 0.01. Its bending stiffness is D = 210 × 10⁹ × 0.003³/12(1 − 0.3²) = 519 N·m and m″ = ρh = 23.6 kg/m², so

f_c = \frac{343^2}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{23.55}{519}} = 3.99\ \text{kHz}

(12/3 = 4 kHz by the rule of thumb), and λc = 86 mm. Its first mode, (1, 1), is at 66.6 Hz, where sound's wavelength is 5.2 m. It has (S/2)√(ρh/D) = 0.0256 modes per hertz, so the 1 kHz third-octave (891 to 1122 Hz) should hold 5.9 of them (it holds 5).

Driven by 1 N, it takes in the infinite plate's F2/16√(Dρh) = 565 µW over a band (a sound power level of 87.5 dB, were it all radiated). At 1 kHz that sustains an energy P/ωη = 565 × 10⁻⁶/(2π × 1000 × 0.01) = 9.0 µJ in the panel's 5.65 kg: ⟨v2⟩ = 1.59 × 10⁻⁶ (m/s)², an RMS velocity of 1.26 mm/s. Maidanik's σ there is 0.054, so it radiates 415 × 0.24 × 0.054 × 1.59 × 10⁻⁶ = 8.6 µW, a sound power level of 69.3 dB, 18 dB below what goes in: ηrad = 415 × 0.054/(2π × 1000 × 23.55) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴, a 66th of η.

Between two rooms, the mass law at 500 Hz is 10 log[1 + (2π × 500 × 23.55/830)²] = 39.0 dB at normal incidence (39.4 dB by 20 log(fm″) − 42), 33.8 dB over a diffuse field. In a room of 50 m³ with a reverberation time of 0.5 s, A = 0.161 × 50/0.5 = 16.1 m², ηroom = 0.0044 at 1 kHz, and the room has 15.6 modes per hertz there, 600 times the panel's.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the panel and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Radiated power is ρcSσ⟨v2⟩: the radiation efficiency σ says how much of a surface's motion becomes sound.
  • Sound is given in levels, 10 log of a power ratio (20 of a pressure ratio), and in octave or third-octave bands.
  • Bending waves catch up with sound at the critical frequency fc = (c2/2π)√(ρh/D), ∝ 1/h; above it a plate radiates fully, below it only its edges and corners do.
  • A mode's radiation falls with each sign change across it: odd–odd modes radiate like pistons, even ones like dipoles and quadrupoles.
  • Over a band, a point-driven panel takes in the infinite plate's F2/16√(Dρh) and holds the energy P/ωη, whatever its shape.
  • A panel's transmission loss follows the mass law, 6 dB per doubling of mass or frequency, until the coincidence dip at fc, where damping takes over; a double wall gains 18 dB an octave above its mass–air–mass resonance.
  • Two coupled oscillators driven by independent noise exchange power β(E1 − E2), exactly: from the one with more energy to the one with less.
  • SEA balances each subsystem's power in a band: ω(ηijEi − ηjiEj) flows from i to j, with niηij = njηji.
  • A corner's coupling loss factor is cgLτ̄/πωS, τ̄ from the bending waves it reflects and transmits; a plate couples to a room by its radiation loss factor, and a room's own is set by its reverberation time.
  • When coupling beats a receiver's damping, the energy per mode evens out (equipartition); damping the receiver helps only while it doesn't.
