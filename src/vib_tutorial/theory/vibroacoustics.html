Radiation efficiency

A vibrating surface pushes the air next to it, and some of that push leaves as sound. How much depends on the motion's pattern as well as its size, so the radiated sound power is written

W = ρc S σ ⟨v²⟩,

with ρc the air's characteristic impedance (415 rayl), S the area, ⟨v²⟩ the mean-square velocity averaged over the surface and over time, and σ the radiation efficiency: 1 for a large piston, which pushes a plane wave off at ρc per unit velocity, and far less for a panel whose neighbouring patches move opposite ways, as most of the air they push just sloshes from one to the next. σ is often quoted in decibels, 10 log σ (the radiation index).

Coincidence

A free bending wave on a plate has the wavenumber kb = √ω (ρh/D)¼: it is dispersive, its speed ω/kb growing as √f, while sound's k = ω/c grows in proportion to f. The two meet at the critical frequency

fc = (c²/2π) √(ρh/D),

which for a plate of thickness h is about 12/h kHz·mm in steel, aluminium or glass (D ∝ h³, so fc ∝ 1/h). Above it, the bending wave is supersonic: it drives a plane sound wave at the angle θ where its trace matches, sin θ = k/kb, and an infinite plate's σ is 1/√(1 − fc/f), large just above fc (the wave leaves near grazing) and 1 above that. Below fc the wave is subsonic: the air above each crest cancels the air above the trough beside it, and an infinite plate radiates nothing at all. A finite one still radiates from where the cancellation is incomplete: its edges and its corners.

A mode's radiation

A simply supported panel's mode (m, n) is sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b), the sum of four plane bending waves with kx = mπ/a, ky = nπ/b. Set in a rigid baffle (so that the air behind can't reach round the edge) its radiated power is the far field's integral over a hemisphere, which Wallace (1972) reduced to

σmn = (64k²ab/π⁶m²n²) ∫∫ [Am(α) Bn(β)]² sin θ dθ dφ,

over θ and φ from 0 to π/2, with α = ka sin θ cos φ, β = kb sin θ sin φ, and Am = cos(α/2) (m odd) or sin(α/2) (m even) over (α/mπ)² − 1, Bn likewise. The page works it out numerically. Comparing k with the mode's own wavenumbers sorts the modes:

  • Corner modes (k < kx and k < ky): every half wave cancels its neighbour but for the quarter cells at the corners. Far below a mode's own resonance, σmn ≈ 32k²ab/π⁵m²n² for odd m and n, a monopole's k²: the panel pumps like a small piston. An even index cancels that too, leaving a dipole's two more powers of k, and two even indices a quadrupole's four.
  • Edge modes (k between kx and ky): along the longer half waves nothing cancels, so the two edges across the shorter ones radiate like strips.
  • Surface modes (√(kx² + ky²) < k): the mode is above its own coincidence, and the whole surface radiates, σmn ≈ 1.

At its own resonance a mode is on the bending wave's curve, kmn = kb, so below fc the resonant modes are all corner or edge modes, and above it all surface modes.

Averaged over a band

From a few hundred hertz up a panel has many modes in each third-octave band (Beams & plates' constant S/2 √(ρh/D) per hertz), and what matters is their average. Maidanik's formula (1962, corrected by Crocker and Price) gives the mean σ of the resonant modes of a baffled, simply supported panel of perimeter P from the wavelength at coincidence λc = c/fc:

  • below fc, σ = (λcP/S) g2(α) + 2(λc²/S) g1(α), with α = √(f/fc): the edge modes (g2, growing with the perimeter) and below fc/2 the corner modes (g1);
  • near fc, a peak of about √(a/λc) + √(b/λc);
  • above, the infinite plate's 1/√(1 − fc/f).

The page sets it beside the mean of Wallace's σmn over the modes resonant in each band, which it follows to within a dB or two wherever a band holds a few modes. Below fc a panel radiates more the longer its edges for its area: stiffening ribs, which add edges, make a panel louder.

A point-driven panel

Drive the panel with a force F at one point. Each mode is a single oscillator of mass M/4 (the mean of its shape squared), driven by F φmn(x0, y0), with the loss factor η in its stiffness. The power going in, ½ F Re v(x0, y0), peaks at each resonance, but averaged over a band it settles, from where several modes share each band, to an infinite plate's:

Pin = ½ F² Re Y∞, Y∞ = 1/8√(Dρh),

a pure resistance: a wave carried off and never returned. In the band, that power is lost in the panel as fast as it comes in, ωηE = Pin, so the panel's energy E = M⟨v²⟩ is Pin/ωη: the response of a panel with many modes in a band depends on its mass, its stiffness and its damping, not on its shape or where it's driven. This is the starting point of statistical energy analysis. The radiated power is each mode's ρcS σmn⟨vmn²⟩ (the page leaves out the modes' mutual radiation, which is small once a band holds several). Written as a loss, ρcSσ⟨v²⟩ = ωηradE with the radiation loss factor

ηrad = ρcσ / ωm″,

so the panel's energy leaves by two routes, its damping and its sound, and the sound takes ηrad/(η + ηrad) of it. In air ηrad is small, 10−4 or so below fc for a steel sheet, so sound is a small leak from a panel's energy, a larger one above fc where σ ≈ 1. A heavy fluid (water) changes that, and loads the panel as an added mass too.

Between two rooms

A panel between two rooms is pushed by the sound on one side and radiates on the other. As an infinite plate of mass m″ per area, a plane wave arriving at θ sees the wall impedance Z = iωm″[1 − (f/fc)² sin⁴θ (1 + iη)], and gets through with the transmission coefficient τ(θ) = 1/|1 + Z cos θ / 2ρc|². The transmission loss is TL = 10 log 1/τ̄.

  • The mass law. Well below fc, only the mass holds the sound back: at normal incidence TL = 10 log[1 + (ωm″/2ρc)²] ≈ 20 log(f m″) − 42 dB, 6 dB more for each doubling of the frequency or the mass. Averaged over the angles a room's sound arrives from (field incidence, up to 78°) it is about 5 dB less.
  • Coincidence. Above fc, at the angle where sin²θ = fc/f, the sound's trace matches the bending wave: the panel's stiffness cancels its mass, only damping is left, and the sound walks through. The diffuse field's TL dips at fc, and above it rises with the damping, about 10 log(2ηf/πfc) on the mass law.

Since fc ∝ 1/h while m″ ∝ h, fcm″ depends only on the material: a heavier panel of the same stuff gains on the mass law but brings its dip lower, into the band it was meant to stop. Double walls, and laminated glass's damping layer, are the ways round it.

Two oscillators

A panel with hundreds of modes in a band, joined to another and to a room, is too much to follow mode by mode, and its details (exactly where each resonance falls) are not known well enough to be worth following. Statistical energy analysis (SEA) follows energy instead, and rests on one exact result. Take two oscillators, m₁ and m₂ with blocked natural frequencies ω₁ and ω₂ (each with the other held still) and damping rates Δ = c/m = 2ζω, joined by a spring kc, and drive each with its own white noise, independent of the other's. The power through the spring, averaged over time, is

P₁₂ = β (E₁ − E₂),

E = m⟨v²⟩ each oscillator's energy (twice its kinetic energy, which is its total on average), with

β = (kc²/m₁m₂)(Δ₁ + Δ₂) / [(ω₁² − ω₂²)² + (Δ₁ + Δ₂)(Δ₁ω₂² + Δ₂ω₁²)],

whatever the forces' strengths: power flows from the oscillator with more energy to the one with less, in proportion to the difference, as heat flows between two bodies at different temperatures (Lyon and Maidanik, 1962). It holds for any strength of coupling. The page works the response out exactly (the covariance of the oscillators' motion, from a Lyapunov equation) and sets β beside it. β is largest when the oscillators are tuned, over a band as wide as their damping rates: energy crosses between pairs of modes whose resonances overlap.

Statistical energy analysis

Sum the two-oscillator result over every pair of modes of two subsystems with resonances in a band, each mode of subsystem i holding the same energy Ei/Ni (Ni = niΔf its modes in the band, ni its modal density). The flow between the subsystems becomes

Pij = ω(ηijEi − ηjiEj),

with the coupling loss factors ηij (the share of subsystem i's energy that leaves for j in a cycle, over 2π, as its own loss factor ηi is the share it dissipates) obeying reciprocity,

niηij = njηji,

so Pij = ωηijni(Ei/ni − Ej/nj): energy flows down the difference in modal energy, energy per mode, which plays temperature's part. In each band every subsystem balances the power it is given with what it dissipates and what it sends on,

Pi = ωηiEi + Σj ω(ηijEi − ηjiEj),

a small linear system for the energies. The assumptions are the two-oscillator result's, made statistical: several modes in each band, so that their averages mean something; the input spread evenly over the band; and coupling weak enough that each subsystem's modes are its own. The answers are band averages: what a set of nominally identical structures would give on average, not what one of them does at one frequency.

Plates at a corner

Where two plates meet at a right angle, each plate's in-plane stiffness stops the other's bending at the corner: the line doesn't move, but it turns, both plates turning with it, and their bending moments balance. A bending wave arriving on plate 1 at θ from the normal shares its trace along the corner, ky = k1 sin θ, with every wave it makes: a reflected wave and a transmitted one, and a near field on each side that decays from the corner. The four conditions give their four amplitudes, and the transmitted wave carries τ(θ) of the energy, the reflected one the rest (τ + |r|² = 1). Identical plates pass half at normal incidence; a plate much thicker or thinner reflects most. Averaged over a diffuse field's directions (its intensity at the corner goes as cos θ),

τ̄12 = ∫ τ(θ) cos θ dθ, over 0 to π/2,

(a third for identical plates), and the energy crossing a corner of length L each second, out of the energy E₁ spread over S₁, gives the coupling loss factor

η12 = cg1 L τ̄12 / πωS₁,

cg = 2√(ω√(D/ρh)) being the bending wave's group speed, twice its phase speed. Reciprocity holds because k₁τ̄12 = k₂τ̄21. A plate's modal density, S/2 √(ρh/D) modes per hertz, is Beams & plates'. A plate couples to a room by its radiation, with ηrad = ρcσ/ωm″ from the driven panel; a room has 4πf²V/c³ modes per hertz, many more than a panel from a few hundred hertz up, so by reciprocity it gives back far less, and its own loss factor is set by its reverberation time, 2.2/fT60. Its energy E = ⟨p²⟩V/ρc² gives the sound pressure level.

Equipartition

For two subsystems with only 1 driven, subsystem 2's balance gives

E₂/E₁ = η12 / (η₂ + η21).

If subsystem 2's damping is small next to η21, it can only lose energy by giving it back, and E₂/n₂ → E₁/n₁: the modal energies even out, equipartition, as two bodies in contact come to one temperature. If it is large, subsystem 2 drains what it is sent. Damping the receiving structure helps only while its damping is comparable with the coupling; damping the source, or cutting the coupling (a resilient joint, a mismatch in thickness), works whatever the receiver.

  • A mode's radiation falls with each sign change across it: odd–odd modes radiate like pistons, even ones like dipoles and quadrupoles.
