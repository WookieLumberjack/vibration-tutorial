Operational modal analysis

A modal test measures the force and the response, and their ratio, the FRF, is the structure's own. A bridge or a running machine can't be hammered: it is shaken by traffic, wind or its own working, forces nobody measures. Operational modal analysis (OMA, output-only) identifies the modes from the responses alone, assuming those forces are random and broadband (white), so the responses' spectra take their shape from the structure.

With white forces of spectral density matrix Sff, the responses' cross-spectral matrix is

Gyy(ω) = H(ω) Sff H(ω)H,

and near a lightly damped mode r its term dominates: Gyy ≈ dr φrφrH, a matrix of rank one whose column space is the mode shape.

Frequency domain decomposition

FDD estimates Gyy at each line by Welch's method (the responses cut into blocks, windowed, Fourier transformed, Y YH averaged) and takes its singular value decomposition, Gyy = U S UH. The first singular value s₁ peaks at every mode, and the first singular vector there is the mode's shape. Picking the peaks gives the frequencies, to the line. Two close modes show as s₁ and s₂ both peaking.

Enhanced FDD (EFDD) adds the damping. The lines round a peak whose first singular vector still has a high MAC with the peak's are that mode's single-degree-of-freedom spectrum, its bell. Transformed back to the time domain it is the mode's autocorrelation function, a decaying cosine: its zero crossings give the damped frequency, and the logarithmic decay of its extremes the damping, as from a free decay.

Stochastic subspace identification

SSI-cov works in the time domain. The responses' correlations Ri = E[yk+i ykT], under white input, are the free decay of the sampled system, Ri = C Ai−1 G: an impulse response in disguise. Stacked in a block Toeplitz matrix, they factor into the observability matrix (C, CA, CA², …) times another, so the matrix has rank 2N. Its SVD's first p singular vectors give the observability matrix at model order p; its first block row is C, and A follows from its shift structure. A's eigenvalues are the poles (λ = fs ln μ), and C's view of its eigenvectors the shapes. As with LSCF on the virtual modal test, the order isn't known: the poles at every order go in a stabilization diagram, and the stable columns are the modes. It fits every mode at once, so close and heavily damped modes don't spoil each other as EFDD's bells do.

Harmonics

A machine running on the structure adds a sine: a sharp peak, a pole with almost no damping, just what a very lightly damped mode looks like. Filtered round its frequency, a mode's random response is Gaussian, with a kurtosis E[x⁴]/E[x²]² of 3; a sine's is 1.5. The page projects the responses on each peak's shape, band-pass filters them round it, and crosses out the peaks whose kurtosis is below 2 and whose damping is below 1 %. A mode with a sine in its band reads low too, but keeps its damping. A sine right on a mode hides it from FDD, whose peak there is the sine's. (A harmonic's peak is also one line wide whatever the block length: a longer block makes it taller, a mode's not.)

What OMA can't do

  • The force is unknown, so the shapes can't be scaled: no modal masses, no FRF. (A known mass added and the shift in frequency, or a measured force at one point, can scale them.)
  • It assumes the forces are white over the band. A coloured input shapes the spectra, and a harmonic one (a machine running) makes a sharp peak that looks like a lightly damped mode.
  • It needs long records: the input is random, and only many averages make the spectra smooth. The rule of thumb is a thousand to two thousand periods of the slowest mode.
  • Damping is the hardest: the Hann window's leakage widens the peaks of a short block (too much damping), and a bell cut short by the MAC threshold rings on (too little).
  • With broadband random forces, the responses' spectra take their shape from the structure, so the modes can be found from the responses alone.
  • FDD: the cross-spectral matrix's first singular value peaks at each mode, and its singular vector is the shape; EFDD adds the damping from the bell's decay.
  • SSI-cov fits a state-space model to the responses' correlations, with a stabilization diagram as LSCF has.
  • Output-only shapes can't be scaled, a machine's sine looks like a mode, and the records must be long.
