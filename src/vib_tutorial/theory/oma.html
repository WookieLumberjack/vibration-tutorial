Operational modal analysis

A modal test measures the force and the response, and their ratio, the FRF, is the structure's own. A bridge or a running machine can't be hammered: it is shaken by traffic, wind or its own working, forces nobody measures. Operational modal analysis (OMA, output-only) identifies the modes from the responses alone, assuming those forces are random and broadband (white), so the responses' spectra take their shape from the structure.

The page drives the chain with independent random forces on every mass (or on one) and records the responses y, one channel a mass, as accelerations by default. With white forces of spectral density matrix Sff (the same at every frequency), the responses' cross-spectral matrix is

G_{yy}(\Omega) = H(\Omega)\, S_{ff}\, H(\Omega)^{H}

Each column of H is a sum of modes, so near a lightly damped mode r its term dominates every entry:

G_{yy}(\Omega) \approx d_r(\Omega)\, \phi_r \phi_r^{H}

a matrix of rank one whose column space is the mode shape, with dr a single-degree-of-freedom spectrum: how strongly the mode responds at that line. The methods below find the modes from this structure.

Frequency domain decomposition

FDD brincker-fdd estimates Gyy at each line by Welch's method welch (the responses cut into half-overlapping blocks of Nb samples, Hann windowed, Fourier transformed, Y YH averaged) and takes its singular value decomposition

G_{yy} = U\,\mathrm{diag}(s_1, s_2, \ldots)\,U^{H}

The first singular value s1 peaks at every mode, and the first column of U there is the mode's shape: where one mode dominates, the matrix is (nearly) rank one, and its one direction is φr. Picking the peaks gives the frequencies, to the line. Two close modes show as s1 and s2 both peaking: the SVD splits them into two orthogonal directions, so close modes are found even where their peaks merge.

Enhanced FDD (EFDD) adds the damping brincker-efdd. The lines round a peak whose first singular vector still has a high MAC (above 0.8 here) with the peak's are that mode's single-degree-of-freedom spectrum, its bell. Transformed back to the time domain it is the mode's autocorrelation function, a decaying cosine: its zero crossings give the damped frequency fd, and the logarithmic decrement δ of its extremes the damping, as from a free decay,

\zeta = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \delta^2}}

Why correlations are free decays

Both EFDD's bell and SSI below lean on one fact, the natural excitation technique (NExT) james-next: under white forces, the correlation of the responses, E[y(t + τ) y(t)T] as a function of the lag τ, decays exactly as the structure's free vibration does. A white force has no memory: what it does after t is uncorrelated with the response at t. So, averaged over the forces, the response τ later is just how the motion at t carries on by itself, a free decay from that state, and the correlation is a sum of the modes' free decays, e−ζωnτ cos(ωdτ + …). Every free-decay method, the log decrement and curve fits alike, then works on correlations as on an impulse response.

Stochastic subspace identification

SSI-cov peeters-deroeck works in the time domain, on the sampled system xk+1 = A xk + noise, yk = C xk: A moves the state (every mass's displacement and velocity) one sample on, and C reads the sensors from it. The responses' correlations at lag i samples, by NExT a free decay, are

R_i = E\bigl[y_{k+i}\,y_k^{T}\bigr] = C\,A^{\,i-1}\,G

with G the covariance of the next state with the output. Stacked in a block Toeplitz matrix, each block row one lag further on, they factor into the observability matrix (C, CA, CA2, …) times a partner built the same way from G:

The observability matrix has only 2N columns (two states per mass), so the Toeplitz matrix has rank 2N however many lags it holds. Its SVD's first p singular vectors give the observability matrix Op at model order p. Its first block row is C, and A follows from its shift structure: Op without its last block row, times A, is Op without its first. A's eigenvalues μ give the poles,

\lambda = f_s \ln \mu

and C's view of its eigenvectors the shapes. As with LSCF on the virtual modal test, the order isn't known: the poles at every order go in a stabilization diagram, and the stable columns are the modes. It fits every mode at once, so close and heavily damped modes don't spoil each other as EFDD's bells do.

Harmonics

A machine running on the structure adds a sine: a sharp peak, a pole with almost no damping, just what a very lightly damped mode looks like. Filtered round its frequency, a mode's random response is Gaussian, with a kurtosis

\frac{E[y^4]}{E[y^2]^2} = 3

while a sine's is 1.5: the sine spends its time near its peaks, where a Gaussian's values crowd near zero with a long tail jacobsen-harmonics. The page projects the responses on each peak's shape, band-pass filters them round it, and crosses out the peaks whose kurtosis is below 2 and whose damping is below 1 %. A mode with a sine in its band reads low too, but keeps its damping. A sine right on a mode hides it from FDD, whose peak there is the sine's. (A harmonic's peak is also one line wide whatever the block length: a longer block makes it taller, a mode's not.)

Scaling the shapes

Without the force, OMA's shapes ψ have no scale: no modal masses, no FRF. One way to get it is to add a known mass ΔM and measure again parloo-scaling. If the mass is small enough to leave the shape alone, the mass-normalized φr = αψ keeps its modal stiffness, while its modal mass goes from 1 to 1 + φrTΔMφr. The frequency squared is their ratio, so fn² = f′n²(1 + α²ψTΔMψ), and

\alpha^2 = \frac{f_n^2 - f_n'^2}{f_n'^2\;\psi^{T}\Delta M\,\psi}

with f′n the mode's frequency with the mass on. The mass must go where the mode moves, and shift its frequency by more than the identification's scatter: the worked example's mode 4 shows why.

What OMA can't do

  • The force is unknown, so the shapes can't be scaled by OMA alone (above, for the ways round it).
  • It assumes the forces are white over the band. A coloured input shapes the spectra, and a harmonic one (a machine running) makes a sharp peak that looks like a lightly damped mode.
  • It needs long records: the input is random, and only many averages make the spectra smooth. The rule of thumb is a thousand to two thousand periods of the slowest mode brincker-ventura.
  • Damping is the hardest: the Hann window's leakage widens the peaks of a short block (too much damping), and a bell cut short by the MAC threshold rings on (too little).
  • A mode with a node at every point the forces act isn't excited at all, as one with a node at the force isn't in a modal test.

Worked example

The page opens on the uniform 4-mass chain (the virtual modal test's: modes at 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz, ζ = 0.0174, 0.0500, 0.0766 and 0.0940) with random forces on every mass, accelerations measured with 2 % noise, and FDD + EFDD. The settings it picks, and what they give:

  1. Sample rate and record. fs = 19 Hz, as the virtual modal test's Auto. Two thousand periods of mode 1 are 2000/1.105 = 1809 s, 1809 × 19 = 34,371 samples a channel.
  2. The block. The page wants lines ζfn/2 apart for the slowest mode, 0.0174 × 1.105/2 = 0.0096 Hz, so Nb ≥ 19/0.0096 = 1979: 2048. Then Δf = 19/2048 = 0.00928 Hz, mode 1's half-power width 2 × 0.0174 × 1.105 = 0.0384 Hz spans 4.1 lines, and half-overlapping blocks fit (34,371 − 2048)/1024 + 1 = 32 times.
  3. The scatter. Each line's spectrum is an average of 32 random blocks, so it scatters by about 1/√32 = 18 % (a little more: overlapping blocks aren't independent). That is why the identified damping moves from one New record to the next, and why a hundred periods (Short record), a twentieth of the data, is far rougher.
  4. SSI's size. Block rows over two periods of mode 1, 2 × 19/1.105 = 34.4: 34, so lags 1 to 67, 67/19 = 3.5 s. The Toeplitz matrix is 4 × 34 = 136 square and has rank 2N = 8; orders up to 15 × 4 = 60 leave room for noise poles.
  5. The machines. A machine running puts the sine halfway between modes 1 and 2, (1.105 + 3.183)/2 = 2.144 Hz; near a mode 2 % above mode 2, 3.247 Hz. A sine's kurtosis is E[sin⁴]/E[sin²]² = (3/8)/(1/4) = 1.5.
  6. Scaling by a mass. Add 0.1 kg to m4. Mode 1 falls from 1.1055 to 1.0823 Hz (−2.1 %), and its shape scaled to 1 at m4 gives ψTΔMψ = 0.1, so α² = (1.1055² − 1.0823²)/(1.0823² × 0.1) = 0.433, against the exact 1/(0.347² + 0.653² + 0.879² + 1²) = 0.431: 0.6 % off. Mode 4 moves little at m4 (0.347 of its peak): it shifts only 0.23 %, and its α² comes out 13 % off.

Things to try

Each of these sets up a test of the chain as it is (Things to try, top left).

  • With broadband random forces, the responses' spectra take their shape from the structure, so the modes can be found from the responses alone.
  • FDD: the cross-spectral matrix's first singular value peaks at each mode, and its singular vector is the shape; EFDD adds the damping from the bell's decay.
  • Under white forces the responses' correlations are free decays (NExT), so every free-decay method works on them.
  • SSI-cov fits a state-space model to the responses' correlations, with a stabilization diagram as LSCF has.
  • A sine's kurtosis is 1.5 and a mode's random response's 3: that tells a machine's harmonic from a mode.
  • Output-only shapes can't be scaled without more (a known added mass can do it), and the records must be long: a thousand periods or more.
