The problem

A machine that runs with a little unbalance shakes with the force meeΩ². Bolted straight to the floor, all of it goes into the building; set on mounts, some of it is taken up by the machine's own motion. The same question arises the other way round: an instrument on a floor that shakes. Both are answered by one number, the transmissibility.

Transmissibility

A machine of mass M sits on a mount of stiffness k and damping c. Under a force F cos Ωt it moves X = F/(k − Ω²M + iΩc), and the mount pushes on the floor with kx + cẋ, (k + iΩc)X. Their ratio is

T = \frac{|F_{\mathrm{floor}}|}{|F|} = \frac{|k + i\Omega c|}{|k - \Omega^2 M + i\Omega c|} T = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}

with r = Ω/ωn and ωn = √(k/M). Now turn it round: the floor moves, y = Y cos Ωt, and the mount pushes the machine with k(y − x) + c(ẏ − ẋ). Then Mẍ + cẋ + kx = ky + cẏ, and the motion that reaches the machine, |X|/|Y|, is the same T (the Simulation page, later, drives its chain this way). So one curve serves for both, as the figure sets them side by side.

T is 1 at low speed (the mount passes the force on), peaks at resonance, and crosses 1 again where (1 − r²)² = 1:

r = \sqrt{2}, \quad\text{whatever the damping}

Above √2 fn the mount isolates: the machine's inertia takes most of the force. Far above, T ≈ 1/r² (40 dB a decade). Below √2 fn it amplifies. So isolation needs a mount whose fn is well below the running speed: a soft one. The isolation is 1 − T, often quoted in dB as 20 log T.

Static deflection

A mount's stiffness under a machine of weight Mg is set by how far it sags, δ = Mg/k, and so

f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} = \frac{15.76}{\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{mm}}}}\ \mathrm{Hz}

The mass has gone: isolation is a matter of sag. 1 mm gives 15.8 Hz, 5 mm 7 Hz, 25 mm 3.2 Hz. For 90 % isolation (T = 0.1) undamped mounts need r² = 1 + 1/T = 11, fn = f/3.3; at 1500 rpm that is 7.5 Hz, 4.4 mm. The Design chart tab draws this for every speed. A very slow machine needs a very soft mount, which is why isolating low frequencies is hard.

The damping trade-off

Damping lowers the resonant peak, T ≈ 1/2ζ there, which matters while the machine runs up and down through it. But above √2 fn the damper passes force in proportion to speed, cΩX, and isolation gets worse. Far above resonance

T \approx \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{r^2}

which is still 1/r² while 2ζr ≪ 1, but turns to 2ζ/r, 20 dB a decade, once r ≫ 1/2ζ: at ζ = 0.05 from about 10 fn up, at ζ = 0.3 from 2 fn. Mounts are a compromise: light damping, or damping that only works at resonance.

A rubber mount's loss is closer to the Damping models page's hysteretic damping, k(1 + iη). Then T = √(1 + η²)/√((1 − r²)² + η²): the same peak as ζ = η/2, but it keeps falling as 1/r², since |1 + iη| doesn't grow with frequency as |k + iΩc| does.

The relaxation isolator

Put the damper in series with a spring Nk (an elastically supported damper, harris), as the middle of the figure below. Near resonance the spring is stiff compared with the damper and the damper works; at high frequency the damper hardly moves, so the mount is just k and Nk side by side and T falls as 1/r² again, not 2ζ/r. Near resonance it passes a little more than a plain mount of the same ζ; it pays off well above it. Its dynamic stiffness is that of the Damping models page's Zener element, with k0 = k, k1 = Nk and c1 = c.

Two stages

Mount the machine on an intermediate mass m2 (a concrete block, a sub-frame), and mount that, as on the right of the figure. There are two resonances, but above both each stage isolates on its own, and the transmissibility falls as 1/r⁴, 80 dB a decade. Ships' engines and sensitive instruments are mounted this way.

An inertia block

Bolting the machine to a heavy block and stiffening the mounts to keep the same sag leaves fn, and so T, unchanged. What it changes is the machine's own motion. Its amplitude is

|X| = \frac{m_e e}{M}\,\frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}

which tends to mee/M well above resonance: the machine and its unbalance turn about their common centre of mass, and a heavier M moves less. A block steadies the machine and lowers its centre of gravity; it doesn't isolate better.

Real mounts

The single mass on a massless spring is the place to start, and real installations depart from it in a few known ways (rivin):

  • Rubber is stiffer moving than standing. A rubber mount's dynamic stiffness is above the stiffness its sag gives, so its fn is higher than 15.76/√δmm says; designers use the maker's dynamic figures.
  • The mount has mass too. High enough, waves stand in the mount itself (a coil spring's surge, a rubber block's thickness modes), and T rises in peaks above the 1/r² line.
  • The floor isn't rigid. A light or flexible floor has its own resonances; a mount isolates only if it is soft compared with the floor where it stands too.
  • The machine rocks. It sways, rocks and twists as well as bouncing: six modes, which the mounts couple unless they are placed (or tilted) about its centre of mass just so. That comes later, once the course has modes: see Rigid body on mounts.

Starting up

Running up to speed, the machine passes through the mounts' resonance. The unbalance's force there is smaller than at speed, (fn/f)² of it, and the faster the run up the less time the resonance has to build (the Single oscillator page's time constant): so lightly damped mounts are usually tolerable, and a slow run up or coast down through resonance is the hazard. The Start-up tab steps it exactly. The unbalance has turned through φ = ∫Ω dt, and its force along the mount is

f(t) = m_e e\,\bigl(\Omega^2\cos\varphi + \dot\Omega\sin\varphi\bigr)

as the speed climbs: the second part is the push that speeds the unbalance up.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on a 100 kg machine with an unbalance mee = 0.01 kg·m, running at 1500 rpm on mounts that sag δ = 5 mm, with ζ = 0.05.

  1. The mounts. k = Mg/δ = 100 × 9.807/0.005 = 196,100 N/m, and fn = 15.76/√5 = 7.048 Hz (423 rpm). c = 2ζ√(kM) = 442.9 N·s/m.
  2. The force. 1500 rpm is 25 Hz, Ω = 157.08 rad/s, so the unbalance pulls with meeΩ² = 0.01 × 157.08² = 246.7 N.
  3. The transmissibility. r = 25/7.048 = 3.547, so r² = 12.58, 1 − r² = −11.58 and 2ζr = 0.3547. Then
T = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 0.3547^2}}{\sqrt{11.58^2 + 0.3547^2}} = \frac{1.0610}{11.585} = 0.0916

and the floor gets 246.7 × 0.0916 = 22.6 N: isolation 90.8 %, 20 log 0.0916 = −20.8 dB. The machine moves (mee/M) r²/11.585 = 100 µm × 12.58/11.585 = 109 µm. Running up in 2 s, it passes fn where the unbalance makes only (7.048/25)² = 0.0795 of its running force, 19.6 N; held there, T ≈ 1/2ζ = 10 would pass 196 N, but the run up is through before that can build, and the floor's force peaks at 111 N. These are the readouts under the mounts' controls, the transmissibility and the start-up.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the machine, the mounts and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Transmissibility is the same for force into the floor and motion from it; it falls below 1 only above √2 fn, whatever the damping.
  • A mount's fn is set by its static deflection alone: 15.76/√δmm Hz. Isolation needs a soft mount.
  • Viscous damping lowers the resonant peak but worsens isolation far above it; a relaxation mount, or a rubber's hysteretic loss, gets the first without most of the second.
  • Two stages fall as 1/r⁴; an inertia block steadies the machine but doesn't change the transmissibility.
  • Real mounts are stiffer than their sag says, resonate themselves high up, and isolate only from a floor stiffer than they are.
