Torsional vibration

A drive train turns as a whole, but its discs also twist back and forth against each other on the shafts between them. Each disc i has an angle θi on top of the steady turning, and

M θ″ + C θ′ + K θ = T(t),

with M the discs' inertias J on the diagonal, K each shaft's stiffness k across the pair it joins, and T the pulsating torques. It is the chain of masses and springs again, turned on its side: the train is free at both ends, so its first mode is the whole train turning (0 Hz), and the rest twist it. Each mode's twist says which links carry its strain energy: those are the shafts that see its torque.

Two discs on one shaft have one mode, f = √(k (J₁ + J₂) / J₁J₂) / 2π, with a node (a point that doesn't swing) nearer the heavier disc.

Gears

A gear pair turns the far shaft at zin/zout times the near one's speed. Each gear keeps its own inertia, and the teeth are a spring km along the line of action: its stretch is rinθin − routθout, with r the base radii. Referred to the motor's shaft, θi = siψi with si the speed ratio, every inertia and stiffness is multiplied by s², and the mesh becomes a shaft of km(r s)²: the geared train is a plain chain, with the same frequencies. That is the textbook n² reflection.

The mesh is far stiffer than the couplings, so its own mode, the two gears bouncing on their teeth, is usually kilohertz. Treat the mesh as rigid and the two gears merge into one inertia: the lower modes barely move, but that mode is gone (the Mode shapes tab compares). It matters because the teeth excite it: no gear is perfect, and the transmission error, the far gear's lag behind where perfect teeth would put it, repeats at every tooth, at z × the shaft's speed (the tooth-pass frequency).

The interference diagram

Nothing in the train depends on its speed, so its natural frequencies are flat lines. What changes with speed is the excitation: an engine fires at fixed orders of its speed (a four-stroke engine's at half-orders, a six-cylinder's majors at 3× and 6×), a reciprocating compressor at 1× and 2× its own, a misaligned coupling at 2×, the teeth at the tooth-pass order. Each is a line f = order × speed. Where it crosses a natural frequency is a torsional critical speed: the order there drives the mode at its resonance.

A design keeps every critical out of a band round the running speed: API 617 (9th edition, 2022) asks for 10 % from 1× and 2× of each shaft's speed. A start-up still passes the criticals below, briefly. Not every crossing matters: an order at a disc the mode barely moves (near its node) hardly excites it, and the response tells which do.

The steady response

At each speed each order is a harmonic torque at ω = order × Ω, and the steady response is one complex solve, (K − ω²M + iωC) Θ = T. A shaft's dynamic torque is (k + iωc) times its twist. The transmission error enters as an imposed stretch e on the mesh: a force (km + iωcm) e on the gears, equal and opposite. The orders are at different frequencies, so their peaks line up only now and then: the sum of the amplitudes is the worst the torque can be.

The damping ratios come from φᵀCφ / 2ωφᵀMφ, each mode's share of the dampers: exact when the damping is light and spread over the train.

Starting up

The Start-up tab starts the train from rest with a motor on its first disc and a load on its last that rises as its speed squared. The angles are then the whole turn, and the train's free turning (the 0 Hz mode) carries it up to speed while the shafts twist on top. The motor's torque depends on the motor's angle and speed and the load's on its speed, so each step solves for them at its end by Newton, on the exact FOH update of the linear train.

An induction motor follows its speed–torque curve, here Kloss's, T = 2Tb s sb / (s² + sb²), from the slip s = 1 − Ω/Ωs. Direct on line, it also gives a torque at the line frequency for the first few cycles (the stator's flux starts with a DC part the rotor's currents pull against): a mode near the line frequency is struck by it at once, while the train has barely moved, and the shafts can see many times their running torque.

A synchronous motor starts as an induction motor on its damper cage, and its salient poles add a torque at twice the slip frequency, 2 s f. That is twice the line frequency at rest and nothing at synchronism, so it sweeps down through every mode below 2f on the way up: each is a resonance passed at the speed where 2 s f = fn. Near synchronism the field is switched on and pulls the rotor into step. A fast start passes the resonances before they build up; a slow one, against a heavy load, lets them grow: that is why such drives get a torsional analysis of the start, and couplings sized for its peaks.

The tab compares each link's peak torque with the torque it carries in steady running: the power drawn by the load and the dampers beyond it, divided by the link's speed.
