Torsional vibration

A drive train turns as a whole, but its discs also twist back and forth against each other on the shafts between them. Each disc i has an inertia Ji and an angle θi on top of the steady turning, each shaft a stiffness ki and damping ci across the pair it joins, and

M\ddot{\theta} + C\dot{\theta} + K\theta = T(t)

with M the inertias on the diagonal and T the pulsating torques. It is the chain of masses and springs again, turned on its side: a disc for a mass, a shaft for a spring. The train is free at both ends, so its first mode is the whole train turning (0 Hz: K has no stiffness against it), and the rest twist it. Each mode's twist says which links carry its strain energy: those are the shafts that see its torque.

A shaft's stiffness comes from its material and shape. A solid round shaft of shear modulus G, diameter d and length L has

k = \frac{G}{L}\,\frac{\pi d^4}{32}

so it goes as d⁴: twice as thick is sixteen times as stiff. A coupling's stiffness is its maker's figure.

Two discs on one shaft

Two discs on one shaft have one flexible mode. Nothing acts on the pair from outside, so their momenta cancel, J1θ̇1 = −J2θ̇2: they swing opposite, the lighter further. The shaft's twist is θ1 − θ2, and either disc's equation gives

\omega^2 = k\,\frac{J_1 + J_2}{J_1 J_2}, \qquad \frac{\theta_2}{\theta_1} = -\frac{J_1}{J_2}

The shaft's own inertia neglected, its angle varies linearly between the discs, so it has a node (a point that doesn't swing) a fraction J2/(J1 + J2) of the way from disc 1: nearer the heavier disc.

Longer trains have no such formula. Holzer's method holzer, the classic hand calculation, guesses ω, swings the first disc by 1, and walks along the train: each disc adds Jiω²θi to the torque the next shaft carries, which twists it by that torque over ki. At a natural frequency the torque left over past the last disc is zero. The page solves the eigenproblem Kφ = ω²Mφ instead, which is the same condition all at once.

Gears

A gear pair with zin teeth on the motor's side and zout on the far side turns the far shaft at zin/zout times the near one's speed. Each gear keeps its own inertia, and the teeth are a spring km along the line of action, the common tangent of the two base circles (radii rin, rout) that the contact moves along:

With each gear's angle measured in its own direction of turning, the spring's stretch is rinθin − routθout − ε, where ε is the transmission error below, and the mesh stiffens the pair by

k_m \begin{bmatrix} r_{in}^2 & -r_{in} r_{out} \\ -r_{in} r_{out} & r_{out}^2 \end{bmatrix}

Referred to the motor's shaft. Write each angle as θi = niψi, with ni the product of the ratios on the way out to disc i. A disc's kinetic energy ½Jiθ̇i² is then ½(Jini²)ψ̇i² and a shaft's strain energy goes the same way, so every inertia and stiffness is multiplied by n². The mesh becomes a shaft of km(rinnin)² (since routnout = rinnin). It is only a change of coordinates, so the geared train is a plain chain with exactly the same frequencies. That is the textbook n² reflection.

The mesh is far stiffer than the couplings, so its own mode, the two gears bouncing on their teeth, is usually kilohertz. Treat the mesh as rigid and the two gears merge into one inertia, Jinnin² + Joutnout²: the lower modes barely move, but that mode is gone (the Mode shapes tab compares). It matters because the teeth excite it. No gear is perfect, and the transmission error ε, the far gear's lag behind where perfect teeth would put it, repeats at every tooth: at z times the shaft's speed (the tooth-pass frequency).

The interference diagram

Nothing in the train depends on its speed, so its natural frequencies are flat lines. What changes with speed Ω is the excitation. Each source is a line ω = noΩ, its order no times the speed:

  • A four-stroke engine fires each cylinder once every two turns, so its torque has every half-order. With N cylinders firing evenly, the bursts come N/2 times a turn, and the major orders, where all the cylinders' torques add, are the multiples of N/2: 3× and 6× for six cylinders.
  • A reciprocating compressor gives 1× and 2× its own speed.
  • A misaligned coupling gives 2×.
  • The teeth give the tooth-pass order.

Where an order's line crosses a natural frequency fr is a torsional critical speed, Ω = 2πfr/no: the order there drives the mode at its resonance.

A design keeps every critical out of a band round the running speed. API 617 api-617 asks for the train's torsional natural frequencies to be at least 10 % away from every excitation in the operating speed range. A start-up still passes the criticals below, briefly. Not every crossing matters: an order applied at a disc the mode barely moves (near its node) hardly excites it, and the response tells which do.

The steady response

At each speed each order is a harmonic torque at ω = noΩ, and the steady response is one complex solve,

\left(K - \omega^2 M + \mathrm{i}\,\omega C\right)\Theta = T

A shaft's dynamic torque is (ki + iωci) times its twist. The transmission error enters as an imposed stretch on the mesh: a force (km + iωcm)ε on the gears, equal and opposite. The orders are at different frequencies, so their peaks line up only now and then: the sum of the amplitudes is the worst the torque can be.

Each mode's damping ratio is its share of the dampers,

\zeta_r = \frac{\phi_r^{T} C\,\phi_r}{2\,\omega_r\,\phi_r^{T} M\,\phi_r}

exact when the damping is light and spread over the train. At a critical, a single mode's response is 1/2ζr times what the same torque would do slowly. So the peak torque in a shaft is about 1/2ζr times the share of the excitation that shaft carries quasi-statically.

Starting up

The Start-up tab starts the train from rest with a motor on its first disc and a load on its last that rises as its speed squared. The angles are then the whole turn, and the train's free turning (the 0 Hz mode) carries it up to speed while the shafts twist on top. The motor's torque depends on the motor's angle and speed, and the load's on its speed, so each step solves for them at its end by Newton, on the exact FOH update of the linear train.

An induction motor with p pole pairs on a supply of fe turns just below its synchronous speed Ωs = 2πfe/p. Its torque follows a speed–torque curve, here Kloss's, in the slip s = 1 − Ω/Ωs:

T = \frac{2\,T_b\,s\,s_b}{s^2 + s_b^2}

It peaks at the breakdown torque Tb at the slip sb, gives 2Tbsb/(1 + sb²) at rest, and nothing at synchronism. The train runs where the curve meets the load, on the steep side below sb:

Direct on line, the motor also gives a torque at the line frequency for the first few cycles, Tte−t/τ sin 2πfet: the stator's flux starts with a DC part the rotor's currents pull against. A mode near the line frequency is struck by it at once, while the train has barely moved, and the shafts can see many times their running torque.

A synchronous motor starts as an induction motor on its damper cage. Its salient poles add a torque Tp sin 2δ, with δ the rotor's electrical lag behind the stator's field. At a slip s that lag grows at 2πsfe, so the torque pulses at 2sfe: twice the line frequency at rest and nothing at synchronism. It sweeps down through every mode below 2fe on the way up, each a resonance passed at the slip where 2sfe = fr. Near synchronism the field is switched on, adding Tf sin δ, which pulls the rotor into step. A fast start passes the resonances before they build up; a slow one, against a heavy load, lets them grow. That is why such drives get a torsional analysis of the start, and couplings sized for its peaks.

The tab compares each link's peak torque with the torque it carries in steady running: the power drawn by the load and the dampers beyond it, divided by the link's speed.

Worked example

The page opens on the motor and fan: a 2 kg·m² motor and a 10 kg·m² fan on a coupling of 180 kN·m/rad (damping 20 N·m·s/rad, and 2 N·m·s/rad of the fan's to ground), running at 1480 rpm. A misaligned coupling puts 50 N·m at 2× on the fan.

  1. The mode. ω² = 180 000 × 12/20 = 108 000, so ω = 328.6 rad/s and f = 52.3 Hz. The fan swings −2/10 = −0.2 of the motor's swing, and the node is 10/12 = 83 % of the way along the coupling, by the fan.
  2. Holzer's check. At ω² = 108 000, swing the motor by 1: the coupling carries 2 × 108 000 = 216 000 N·m per radian and twists by 216 000/180 000 = 1.2, leaving the fan at −0.2. The fan adds 10 × 108 000 × (−0.2) = −216 000: nothing is left over.
  3. The damping. ζ = 0.0183, so 1/2ζ = 27.3.
  4. The critical. The 2× line meets 52.3 Hz at 60 × 52.3/2 = 1569 rpm, 6.0 % above the running speed: inside the 10 % band.
  5. The torque there. Slowly, a torque on the fan accelerates both discs, and the coupling carries the motor's share, 50 × 2/12 = 8.33 N·m. At the critical that is multiplied by 27.3: 227 N·m, against the response tab's peak of 228.
  6. At the running speed. 2 × 1480/60 = 49.3 Hz is a frequency ratio of 0.943. One mode gives 8.33/√((1 − 0.943²)² + (2 × 0.0183 × 0.943)²) = 71.8 N·m, against the page's 72.1.
  7. A softer coupling. To put the critical 10 % below the running speed, f ≤ 0.9 × 49.3 = 44.4 Hz: k ≤ (2π × 44.4)² × 20/12 = 130 kN·m/rad.
  8. The motor. Two pole pairs on 50 Hz: Ωs is 1500 rpm. With Tb = 1600 N·m at sb = 0.2 it starts with 2 × 1600 × 0.2/1.04 = 615 N·m. It runs at 1444 rpm (a 3.75 % slip), where the fan and its damper take 580 N·m.

The gears. The geared compressor's bull gear (3 kg·m², 120 teeth, base radius 0.3 m) drives a pinion (0.05 kg·m², 30 teeth, 0.075 m) through a 2 GN/m mesh. Referred to the pinion's shaft, the bull gear weighs 3/4² = 0.1875 kg·m², and the mesh is a shaft of 2×10⁹ × 0.075² = 1.125×10⁷ N·m/rad. Two discs on it: ω² = 1.125×10⁷ × 0.2375/(0.05 × 0.1875) = 2.85×10⁸, so f = 2687 Hz, against the page's 2688 Hz (the couplings barely hold the gears). The tooth-pass, 120× the motor's speed, meets it at 60 × 2688/120 = 1344 rpm.

Things to try

Each preset (top left) sets up a train, and its start-up where it has one:

  • A drive train in torsion is the chain again, with inertias and shaft stiffnesses, free at both ends so its first mode is rigid turning.
  • Gears refer to one shaft by the square of the speed ratio; a stiff mesh has its own mode, excited at tooth-pass.
  • Criticals are where an excitation order's line crosses a natural frequency; keep them out of a band round the running speed.
  • At a critical, a shaft's torque is about 1/2ζ times its quasi-static share of the excitation.
  • A start-up sweeps the excitations through the modes below running speed: a slow start lets them build up.
