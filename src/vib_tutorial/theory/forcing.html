Free vibration The poles Forced response
  • see a blow start the free vibration, and work out how far it swings;
  • see a step overshoot its static deflection, and by how much;
  • find how a pulse's length against the period sets its peak;
  • see a slow force resonate the oscillator through one of its harmonics;
  • build a repeating force from its harmonics, and see each one through H.
    •

    The Single oscillator page drove the mass with a sine that went on for ever. Real forces start and stop, or repeat without being sines: a hammer blow, a load put down, a door slammed; an engine's firing, a press's strokes, footsteps on a floor. This page runs the same oscillator, live, and you apply them as it runs: a one-off force from Apply (a hit, a push held and let go, a ramp, a rectangular or half-sine pulse), a periodic one from Wave. Each acts from the moment it is applied, on whatever the mass is already doing, and the motion is the sum of each force's own response: hit it twice and the second hit's ring adds to the first's. That is superposition, the property of a linear system that the whole page rests on. The one-off forces, each from rest:

    An impulse and the impulse response

    A hammer blow lasts far less than a period. All that it leaves behind is its impulse I = ∫f dt: it changes the mass's momentum by I before the spring has time to stretch, so the mass starts from where it was with velocity I/m and then vibrates freely. Put x0 = 0 and v0 = I/m into the free vibration: for a unit impulse (1 N·s) on a mass at rest it is the impulse response

    h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin\omega_d t}{m\omega_d} \qquad (t \ge 0)

    and 0 before the blow: nothing moves until it is struck (causality). A blow I gives x = I h(t), whose first swing is a little under I/mωn.

    Convolution: Duhamel's integral

    Any force is a string of small impulses: in the moment dτ at the time τ it gives the impulse f(τ) dτ. Each rings as its own impulse response, which at the later time t has been ringing for t − τ. The system is linear, so the responses add:

    x(t) = \int_0^t f(\tau)\,h(t - \tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

    This is Duhamel's integral (duhamel), the convolution of the force with h. The Convolution tab draws it at one instant from the run's own record: h is flipped (time runs backwards from t) and slid along, multiplied by the force, and the shaded area is x(t). Recent force counts most; force from long ago has rung down. Pause and drag the time line back, and the area traces out the response.

    So h says everything about the system: knowing it, every response follows. The integral starts from rest; from x0 and v0, add the free vibration from there. (The page steps the mass exactly as it runs instead, and the tab works the integral out beside it, from the last time the mass was let go or changed: they agree to the integral's accuracy.)

    h and H: one function, two views

    Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency. Transform Duhamel's integral and it becomes X(Ω) = H(Ω)F(Ω), with X(Ω) and F(Ω) the Fourier transforms of the response and the force: the frequency response is the Fourier transform of the impulse response,

    H(\Omega) = \int_0^\infty h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t

    A long, lightly damped ring in time is a tall, narrow peak in frequency; a quick decay is a broad, low one: transform the ring a blow leaves and you have the Single oscillator page's receptance. This is why a hammer test works: one blow excites every frequency at once, and the ring that follows holds the whole H.

    The step response

    A force F switched on and left on is a string of impulses F dτ without end, so its response is F times the integral of h:

    x(t) = \frac{F}{k}\left[1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\zeta\omega_n}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right)\right]

    (and the other way round, h is the slope of the unit step's response). It rises toward the static F/k but overshoots it, by the fraction Mp of it, at the peak time tp, half a damped period:

    M_p = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}, \qquad t_p = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d}

    and then rings down about it. Undamped it swings to twice the static deflection: suddenly applied, a load does double the damage it does when placed gently. It settles within 2 % once its envelope e−ζωnt has fallen to 0.02, after ts ≈ ln 50/ζωn, which is rounded to 4/ζωn, four time constants (ogata). Critically damped, it rises without overshoot; overdamped, slowly.

    The Laplace transform

    Duhamel's integral starts from rest, and working it out for a given force means integrating. The Laplace transform takes the start in too, and turns the equation of motion into algebra: each derivative becomes a multiplication by s, and the response becomes a fraction to be read back from a table. It is the standard tool for transients, and the language of control (the Active control page, later, uses its transfer functions). It turns a function of time x(t), from t = 0 on, into a function X(s) of a complex frequency s:

    X(s) = \int_0^\infty x(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t

    Think of s as the exponent of a motion est, as with the poles: on the imaginary axis, s = iΩ, it is a steady oscillation at Ω; with a negative real part, one that dies away. The transform weighs x(t) against e−st for every such s. With a large enough real part e−st dies away faster than any response on these pages grows, so the integral is finite.

    The transforms needed here all follow from that one integral. A step, 1 from t = 0 on, gives ∫e−st dt = 1/s. An exponential es1t gives ∫e(s1 − s)t dt = 1/(s − s1), and with s1 = −ζωn ± iωd (and eiωdt = cos ωdt + i sin ωdt) the two together give the decaying cosine and sine. A unit impulse, all of it at t = 0 where e−st = 1, gives 1:

    x(t), from t = 0X(s)
    a unit impulse at t = 01
    a unit step1/s
    es1t1/(s − s1)
    e−ζωnt cos ωdt(s + ζωn)/((s + ζωn)² + ωd²)
    e−ζωnt sin ωdtωd/((s + ζωn)² + ωd²)

    Four rules do the rest:

    • It is linear: the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms, and a constant factor stays a factor.
    • A derivative is a multiplication by s, less the start. Integrating ẋ e−st by parts gives s X(s) − x0, and doing it again s² X(s) − s x0 − v0 for ẍ.
    • A delay is a factor: x(t − T), started at T instead of 0, has the transform e−sTX(s).
    • A convolution is a product: Duhamel's integral has the transform H(s)F(s), as in time and frequency above.

    The equation transformed: the transfer function

    Transform each term of m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = f(t) with the derivative rule, starting from x(0) = x0 and ẋ(0) = v0, and collect the terms in X(s):

    (m s^2 + c s + k)\,X(s) = F(s) + m(s x_0 + v_0) + c x_0

    The differential equation has become an algebraic one, solved by dividing:

    X(s) = \underbrace{H(s)\,F(s)}_{\text{forced}} + \underbrace{\frac{m(s x_0 + v_0) + c x_0}{m s^2 + c s + k}}_{\text{free}}

    with the transfer function H(s) = 1/(m s² + c s + k). The first part is Duhamel's integral, the second the free vibration from the start, both at once. A unit impulse from rest (F(s) = 1) leaves X = H(s), so H(s) is the transform of h(t); to check, m s² + c s + k is m((s + ζωn)² + ωd²), and the table's last row turns H(s) back into the h(t) above.

    The transfer function says everything about the oscillator in two ways:

    • Its poles, the roots of m s² + c s + k, are where it is infinite: the s-plane points of the Single oscillator page. Each pole s1 puts an es1t in the response, so the poles set the free response's decay and frequency.
    • On the imaginary axis, s = iΩ, it is the frequency response H(Ω): the Laplace transform there is the Fourier transform, for anything that has died away.

    Back to time: partial fractions

    Under a step F from rest, F(s) = F/s, so

    X(s) = \frac{F}{s\,(m s^2 + c s + k)}

    which is in no table. Split it into fractions that are, the partial fractions:

    X(s) = \frac{F}{k}\left(\frac{1}{s} - \frac{s + 2\zeta\omega_n}{(s + \zeta\omega_n)^2 + \omega_d^2}\right)

    (put the two over the common denominator to check: the top becomes ωn² = k/m). Write s + 2ζωn as (s + ζωn) + (ζωn/ωd)ωd and read each piece off the table: 1/s is the static step, the rest the decaying cosine and ζωn/ωd times the decaying sine. That is the step response above. Its final value needs no inverting at all: a response that settles ends at lims→0 s X(s), here F/k.

    The delay rule builds the pulses from steps. A rectangle of length T is a step F at 0 less a step F at T:

    so F(s) = F(1 − e−sT)/s, and its response is the step response less the same step response started T later. Push and hold, then let go: that's the rectangle, by hand.

    Pulses: impulse, step, or in between

    What a pulse of length T does depends on T against the natural period Tn = 2π/ωn, through their ratio T/Tn:

    • T ≪ Tn: it is an impulse. Only its area counts, and the peak grows with it: a rectangle reaches 2 sin(πT/Tn) times static, about 2πT/Tn.
    • T ≫ Tn: the mass follows it statically: a ramp's peak tends to 1 (its overshoot is |sin(πT/Tn)|/(πT/Tn), and none at all for a whole number of periods); a rectangle, which is switched on and off suddenly, still throws it to 2.
    • In between: the worst case. A half-sine is worst at T ≈ 0.8Tn, 1.77 times static undamped.

    The peak can come while the pulse lasts (the primary response) or in the free vibration it leaves (the residual response). Undamped, the residual swing has a simple meaning. Once the pulse is over, Duhamel's integral runs over the whole pulse, and with h = sin ωnt/mωn it is a free vibration of amplitude

    \frac{1}{m\omega_n}\left|\int_0^T f(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_n\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right| = \frac{|F(\omega_n)|}{m\omega_n}

    the force's Fourier transform at the natural frequency. A short pulse's transform is flat at its area I there, so it swings by I/mωn, as a blow does. A rectangle's is 2F|sin(ΩT/2)|/Ω, which at ωn is (F/k) 2|sin(πT/Tn)|: zero for a rectangle of a whole number of periods, which leaves the mass at rest. A ramp's overshoot is the residual of its slope, a rectangle F/T high, and so (F/k)|sin(πT/Tn)|/(πT/Tn).

    The Pulse spectrum tab builds the peak ratio against T/Tn: each pulse applied with the mass at rest lands its point a period after it ends, and the theory's curves come on request. It is a shock response spectrum. The Shock spectrum page, later, turns it round: one pulse, a bank of oscillators of every frequency.

    A periodic force is a sum of harmonics

    Many forces repeat: an engine's firing, a pump's pulses, a press's strokes, footsteps on a floor. A force that repeats every period T0, however it is shaped, can be built from sines and cosines: one at the fundamental frequency f0 = 1/T0, the rest at whole multiples of it, the harmonics. With ω0 = 2πf0 and harmonic number j, this is its Fourier series (fourier):

    f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_j\cos j\omega_0 t + b_j\sin j\omega_0 t\bigr)

    Only multiples of f0 can appear, because only they repeat every T0 too. So a periodic force has a line spectrum: its mean a0, and a line at each harmonic. The picture below builds a ±F square wave from its first three harmonics. Each one alone is a smooth sine; added, the 3rd and 5th flatten the 1st's top and steepen its sides, and the sum is already close to square. More harmonics square it further.

    Finding the coefficients

    Each coefficient is found by an average, because the harmonics are orthogonal: over a period, the product of two different harmonics (a sine and a cosine, or two at different j) averages to zero, and a harmonic times itself (sin² jω0t, say) averages to ½. Multiply the series by sin jω0t and average it over a period: every term drops out except bj's, which leaves bj/2. So

    a_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad a_j = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f\cos j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t b_j = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t

    In words: a0 is the mean, and bj is twice the mean of the force times the harmonic's sine. The figure does it for the square wave. Times sin ω0t, the product is never negative: the two agree in sign everywhere, the mean is 2F/π, and b1 = 4F/π. Times sin 2ω0t, each half period holds as much below the axis as above, and b2 = 0.

    Worked out for every j, with f = F over the first half period and −F over the second (and ω0T0 = 2π),

    b_j = \frac{2F}{T_0}\left(\int_0^{T_0/2}\!\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t - \int_{T_0/2}^{T_0}\!\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t\right) b_j = \frac{2F}{\pi j}\,(1 - \cos j\pi) = \begin{cases} 4F/\pi j & j\ \text{odd} \\ 0 & j\ \text{even}\end{cases}

    and every aj is zero: the series below. Tables of such series are in any engineering mathematics text (kreyszig).

    Symmetry saves work. A force that is odd about t = 0 (f(−t) = −f(t), as this square wave is) has only sines; an even one (f(−t) = f(t)) only cosines and the mean. A force with half-wave symmetry, f(t + T0/2) = −f(t) (each half period the last one upside down), has odd harmonics only. Moving the force along in time changes how each harmonic is split between its cosine and its sine, but not its height.

    Height and phase: the complex amplitudes

    Each harmonic's cosine and sine together are one sinusoid, with a height and a phase. As on the Single oscillator page, a complex number holds both: with Cj = aj − i bj (and C0 = a0),

    f(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{j\ge 0} C_j\,\mathrm{e}^{ij\omega_0 t}, \qquad C_j = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f\,\mathrm{e}^{-ij\omega_0 t}\,\mathrm{d}t

    (the integral for j ≥ 1). |Cj| = √(aj² + bj²) is the harmonic's height, which the Harmonics tab plots, and its angle the harmonic's phase. (The Signals & spectra page, later, finds these lines in a sampled record with the FFT, which splits each into two half-height lines at ±jf0.)

    The heights also share out the force's power. The mean of f² over a period is

    \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0} f^2\,\mathrm{d}t = a_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\ge1}|C_j|^2

    (Parseval's theorem, parseval): the cross terms average to zero, and each sinusoid's mean square is half its height squared. A ±F square wave's mean square is F²; its fundamental, 4F/π high, carries 8/π² = 81.1 % of it, and the first three harmonics 93.3 %.

    Each harmonic through H

    The oscillator is linear, so each harmonic drives it as a harmonic force on its own (the Single oscillator page's forced response, at Ω = jω0), and the steady response is the sum of their responses:

    x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{j\ge 0} X_j\,\mathrm{e}^{ij\omega_0 t}, \qquad X_j = H(j\omega_0)\,C_j H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\Omega c}

    Each harmonic is multiplied by its own |H| and lags by its own φ, with r = jω0/ωn:

    |X_j| = \frac{|C_j|/k}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{j\omega_0}{\omega_n}
    • The mean only sags the spring: H(0) = 1/k.
    • A harmonic that lands near fn is amplified by about Q; the rest are not. So a force at f0 can resonate the oscillator at any multiple jf0 = fn it contains: a machine running at a third of a mode's frequency can still drive it, through its third harmonic.
    • The harmonics well above fn are cut by |H| ≈ 1/mΩ², so by 1/j² more than the force's: the response is smoother than the force.
    • Each harmonic is shifted by a different lag, so the response's shape is not the force's, and its peak is not the sum of the harmonics' heights: they don't peak at the same time.

    On a machine, the harmonics of the running speed are called orders (once per revolution, twice, …); the Machinery diagnostics page, later, tracks them through a run-up.

    Shapes and their harmonics

    The page's waves, each as its series (the formulas for the |Cj| the Harmonics tab plots):

    • Square wave (±F): (4F/π) Σ sin jω0t / j over odd j. It has half-wave symmetry, so odd harmonics only.
    • Sawtooth: (2F/π) Σ (−1)j+1 sin jω0t / j, every harmonic.
    • Triangle: (8F/π²) Σ ± sin jω0t / j², odd only, the signs alternating.
    • Rectified sine F|sin πt/T0|: 2F/π − (4F/π) Σ cos jω0t / (4j² − 1).
    • Rectangular pulses (F for a fraction d of each period): mean Fd, and |Cj| = (2F/πj)|sin πjd|, zero at every multiple of 1/d.
    • Impulse train (a blow of impulse I every T0): I/T0 + (2I/T0) Σ cos jω0t: every harmonic the same height.

    How fast the harmonics fall depends on how smooth the wave is: as 1/j with a jump, 1/j² with a kink (a jump in slope), and not at all for an impulse. The smoother the force, the fewer harmonics it takes.

    Cutting the series short: Gibbs

    Keep the first N harmonics. Where the force is smooth, the series closes in on it as N grows. At a jump it goes through the midpoint (0 for the square wave), and on either side it overshoots: by 8.95 % of the jump, however many terms it keeps, the overshoot only getting narrower. This is the Gibbs phenomenon, described by Wilbraham (wilbraham) and again by Gibbs (gibbs). For the ±F square wave the jump is 2F, so the overshoot settles at 0.18F. The response doesn't jump (a mass can't), so its own series has no Gibbs overshoot and converges much faster: as 1/N for a square wave's, as 1/N² for a triangle's.

    The steady state without the series

    The steady state can also be worked out directly. Write the oscillator's state, its position and velocity, as z = [x, ẋ]. Starting a period from z0, the free motion carries it to ΦTz0, and the force adds zp, where one period of the force would leave it from rest (both stepped exactly, the force by a straight line between closely spaced samples). In the steady state each period ends where it started:

    z_0 = \Phi_T z_0 + z_p

    two linear equations for the two numbers in z0. That is exact, and it is what the series converges to. Without damping, a harmonic exactly on fn leaves the equations with no solution: no steady state, the swing grows for ever.

    Starting from rest

    Switched on from rest, the response is the steady state plus a free vibration that makes up the start, as for a single sine; it dies away in a few time constants, 1/ζωn each. Switch a wave on and watch it: the Harmonics tab lands its lines only once the start has died away.

    How this page computes it

    The mass is stepped as it runs by the exact update the chain's simulator uses, which is exact for a force that is linear between steps: a ramp, and a step or a rectangle whose edges fall on a step. A hit is a jump in the mass's momentum, I/m, at its own instant (one that falls inside a step is carried on to the step's end). Each sample of the force is its mean over the step around it, so a jump anywhere in a step keeps its impulse and its place; there are 400 steps across the shortest pulse or the wave's period. Each force's response is its closed form above to within 0.05 %. The convolution integral is worked out separately from the run's record by the trapezium rule, to show that it gives the same.

    A worked example: a step

    The page opens on the Single oscillator's defaults, m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m, at rest. Push with 1 N and hold it (the trial A step), and look at the convolution 0.3 s after the push.

    1. The oscillator. ωn = √400 = 20 rad/s, so fn = 3.18 Hz and Tn = 2π/20 = 0.314 s; ζ = 2/(2√400) = 0.05, ζωn = 1 s−1 and ωd = 19.97 rad/s. The readout under the oscillator's boxes says the same.
    2. The transform. F(s) = 1/s, so X(s) = 1/(s(s² + 2s + 400)), which splits into (1/400)(1/s − (s + 2)/((s + 1)² + 19.97²)). From the table, x(t) is 2.5 mm times 1 − e−t(cos 19.97t + 0.0501 sin 19.97t), with t in seconds.
    3. The overshoot. ζπ/√(1 − ζ²) = 0.1573, so Mp = e−0.1573 = 0.854: 85.4 %, up to 2.5 × 1.854 = 4.64 mm, at tp = π/19.97 = 0.157 s.
    4. Settling. The rule gives 4/ζωn = 4 s; the response itself last leaves the 2 % band at 3.8 s.
    5. At 0.3 s after the push: ωdt = 5.9925 rad, whose cosine is 0.95805 and sine −0.28661, and e−0.3 = 0.74082. So x is 2.5 mm times 1 − 0.74082 × (0.95805 − 0.0501 × 0.28661) = 0.3009, 0.752 mm: the 752 µm the Convolution tab gives there, from its shaded area and from the run itself.
    6. h and H. The ring's time constant 1/ζωn = 1 s makes a peak 2ζfn = 0.318 Hz wide and 1/cωn = 1/(2 × 20) = 25 mm/N high: the receptance a blow's ring transforms to.

    A worked example: the page's square wave

    Switch on the wave as the page opens it, a ±1 N square wave at f0 = 1 Hz, on the Single oscillator's defaults, m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m, with the Harmonics tab showing 5 harmonics.

    1. The oscillator. fn = 3.18 Hz and ζ = 0.05, so fn/f0 = 3.18: the 3rd harmonic, at 3 Hz, sits just below resonance.
    2. The force's harmonics. Odd only: |C1| = 4/π = 1.273 N, |C3| = 4/3π = 0.4244 N, |C5| = 4/5π = 0.255 N.
    3. Each through H. r = 2πj/20 = 0.314, 0.942 and 1.571. At 1 Hz, 1 − r² = 0.9013 and 2ζr = 0.0314, so |H| = 2.5 mm/N / 0.902 = 2.77 mm/N; at 3 Hz it is the Single oscillator example's 17.10 mm/N; at 5 Hz, above resonance, 1 − r² = −1.4674 and 2ζr = 0.1571, so |H| = 2.5 mm/N / 1.476 = 1.69 mm/N. Then
    |X_1| = 1.273 \times 2.77 = 3.53\ \mathrm{mm} |X_3| = 0.4244 \times 17.10 = 7.26\ \mathrm{mm} |X_5| = 0.255 \times 1.69 = 0.431\ \mathrm{mm}

    lagging their harmonics of the force by 2.0°, 40.1° and 173.9°. The force's fundamental is three times its 3rd harmonic, but the response's 3rd harmonic is about twice its fundamental: it carries 80.6 % of the swing's power (7.26² over 3.53² + 7.26² + 0.431²), the 1st 19.1 % and the 5th 0.3 %, as the Harmonics tab lists them once the start has died away. The response rings at three times the drive. By Parseval its RMS is

    \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(3.53^2 + 7.26^2 + 0.431^2\right)}\ \mathrm{mm} = 5.72\ \mathrm{mm}

    the exact steady state's RMS too. Its peak is not the heights' sum, 11.2 mm: with their different lags the harmonics never peak together, and the steady state swings between −10 mm and 10 mm, as the time plot shows. The series with 5 harmonics is within 2.02 % of that swing.

    Things to try

    Each of these sets up the oscillator and the force, and sets it going (Things to try, under the force's controls).

  • Forces on a linear oscillator add: applied as it runs, each one's response adds to whatever the mass was already doing (superposition).
  • An impulse I starts the mass at I/m; the response to a unit impulse, h(t), is a free vibration.
  • Any response is the convolution of the force with h (Duhamel's integral): every slice of force rings as h from its own moment on.
  • h(t) and H(Ω) are a Fourier pair; the Laplace transform of h is the transfer function H(s) = 1/(m s² + c s + k), infinite at the poles.
  • The Laplace transform turns the equation into algebra, the start included; partial fractions and a table bring the answer back to time.
  • A step overshoots its static value by e−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²) (twice the static deflection, undamped) and settles in about 4/ζωn.
  • A pulse much shorter than the period acts by its area, one much longer statically; the worst lengths lie near half a period to a period. Undamped, the swing it leaves is its spectrum at ωn.
  • A periodic force is a line spectrum: its mean and harmonics at whole multiples of f0, each coefficient twice the mean of the force times that harmonic.
  • Odd symmetry gives sines only, even cosines only, half-wave symmetry odd harmonics only.
  • Each harmonic goes through H(jω0) on its own; a mode at any jf0 resonates.
  • The harmonics fall as 1/j after a jump, 1/j² after a kink; a jump's series overshoots it by 9 % (Gibbs).
  • The steady state can be found exactly from one period: the state that repeats, z0 = ΦTz0 + zp.
