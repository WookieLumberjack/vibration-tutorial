What a machine's vibration says

Every part of a running machine leaves its own frequencies in the casing's vibration. The shafts leave their speeds and harmonics (unbalance at 1X, misalignment at 2X). The gears leave their tooth-mesh frequency (the number of teeth times the shaft's speed), and the rolling bearings their defect frequencies. Condition monitoring reads the spectrum against that list. A fault changes a line, adds new ones, or adds a pattern: sidebands, impacts, families of harmonics. This page synthesizes a gearbox's casing acceleration x(t) and looks at it four ways. The sections after them cover what a monitoring programme does with the readings: averaging, the common shaft faults, severity limits and trends.

The machine: a shaft turning at fr = 29.95 Hz (1797 rpm), a pinion of z = 27 teeth, a 6205 ball bearing, and an accelerometer on the casing with a resonance at 3 kHz. The record is 4 s long, sampled at 20,480 Hz, and its spectra take blocks of 8192 samples, so their lines are Δf = 2.5 Hz apart.

Run-ups and order tracking

While the speed changes, a frequency analysis smears: a fixed-length block sees the 1X sweep across several lines. Each force, though, stays at a fixed order, a multiple of the speed. So order tracking analyses in angle instead of time. The record is resampled at equal steps of the shaft's angle (from a tachometer's pulses), and each block of whole revolutions is transformed. An order is then one line, whatever the speed did. The page resamples at 32 points a revolution, which reaches order 16, and transforms blocks of 8 revolutions, so its order lines are 1/8 apart.

Against frequency, the orders are rays from the origin, and a structural mode is a horizontal band that they light up as they cross it. Against order, the orders stand still and the mode's band slants. Each order's amplitude along the run-up is its order track: the Bode plot of the Jeffcott rotor page, measured.

A run-up that is too fast misreads a resonance. The response needs about Q = 1/2ζ cycles to build up, so a sweep that crosses the half-power band in fewer peaks lower and later, and rings after (see the Jeffcott rotor page's run-up, Lewis's result lewis-1932). The run-up's casing mode is at 40 Hz with ζ = 0.03: Q = 16.7 and a half-power band of 2ζf = 2.4 Hz. Running from 10 to 70 Hz in 20 s, the 1X crosses that band in 0.8 s, 32 of the mode's cycles. In 4 s it crosses in 0.16 s, only 6.4 cycles.

Gear-mesh sidebands

The mesh's force repeats at every tooth, at fm = zfr, with harmonics. A fault on one gear modulates it once a turn of that gear, which puts sidebands at fm ± kfr. Take an eccentric pinion. Once a turn, at the shaft's angle θ, its mesh grows and shrinks by a share s, and its phase swings by β:

x = (1 + s\cos\theta)\cos(z\theta + \beta\sin\theta)

To first order in s and β, the products of cosines split into sums:

x \approx \cos z\theta + \tfrac12(s + \beta)\cos(z + 1)\theta +\ \tfrac12(s - \beta)\cos(z - 1)\theta

The amplitude modulation alone gives equal sidebands, s/2 each side. The phase modulation adds β/2 to the upper one and takes it from the lower. So the pair is unequal whenever both are present, and its two lines measure s and β separately. The page's eccentric pinion has s = 0.3 and β = 0.15 rad: sidebands of 0.225 above and 0.075 below the unit mesh line. Its spectrum reads 0.226 and 0.076 beside 0.995.

How many pairs tells what kind of fault it is. A smooth modulation (an eccentric or bent gear) gives one or two pairs. A short event once a turn (a cracked or broken tooth) gives a long, flat family, since a narrow pulse's spectrum is broad. The spacing says which shaft the faulty gear is on.

Bearings and the envelope

A rolling bearing with a spall gives a strike each time a ball meets it. The bearing has n balls of diameter d on a pitch circle of diameter D, touching the races along a line at the contact angle α, with the inner race turning at fr and the outer one still.

A ball bearing end on: n balls of diameter d on a pitch circle of diameter D, the inner race turning at fr and the outer one still. Right, a ball cut through, touching its races along a line at the contact angle α.

Where the strikes come follows from rolling without slip. Write rb = (d/D)cos α. Then the ball's contact with the inner race is at a radius (D/2)(1 − rb), moving at 2πfr times that, and its contact with the still outer race doesn't move. A ball's centre moves at the average of its two contact points, so the cage turns at the fundamental train frequency:

\mathrm{FTF} = \frac{f_r}{2}(1 - r_b)

A point on the outer race is passed by all n balls each turn of the cage. A point on the inner race is passed at n times the inner race's speed relative to the cage, fr − FTF. A ball spins, relative to the cage, at that relative speed times the inner contact's radius over the ball's:

\mathrm{BPFO} = n\,\mathrm{FTF}, \qquad \mathrm{BPFI} = \frac{n f_r}{2}(1 + r_b) \mathrm{BSF} = \frac{D f_r}{2d}\bigl(1 - r_b^2\bigr)

A defect on a ball meets both races each spin, so it strikes at 2 BSF. On the 6205 (n = 9, d = 7.94 mm, D = 39.04 mm, α = 0): rb = 0.2034, FTF = 11.93 Hz, BPFO = 107.4 Hz (3.585 × the shaft's speed), BPFI = 162.2 Hz (5.415 ×) and 2 BSF = 141.2 Hz. That the two race frequencies add up to n times the shaft's speed is a quick check.

Each strike is a short impulse that rings the structure's resonances, in the kilohertz. The strikes' rate carries little energy at its own frequency, so the plain spectrum barely shows it under the shafts and the mesh. Envelope analysis band-passes the record round a resonance the strikes ring, clear of the gears. It takes the envelope, the magnitude of the analytic signal gabor-1946 (by the Hilbert transform), and transforms that, and the strikes' rate and its harmonics stand out randall-antoni. A resonance at fc = 3000 Hz with ζ = 0.05 rings over its half-power band, 2ζfc = 300 Hz wide, and its strikes spread about 3ζfc either side. Hence the page's band, 2550 to 3450 Hz.

The kurtosis of the band climbs with impacts and helps pick the band. It is 3 for Gaussian noise and 1.5 for a sine. Doing that band by band is the spectral kurtosis dwyer-1983 antoni-2006. Inner-race and ball defects pass through the load zone, so their strikes swell and fade, which gives sidebands at ± 1X (inner race) or ± FTF (ball). The balls slip a little, so the strikes' spacing scatters by a percent or two. The envelope's lines stay sharp at low harmonics, while the raw spectrum's high ones smear.

The cepstrum

A family of lines spaced Δf is a periodic ripple in the log spectrum. The cepstrum bogert-1963, the inverse Fourier transform of the log of the power spectrum S(f),

c(\tau) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigl[\ln S(f)\bigr]

turns that ripple into one peak at the quefrency τ = 1/Δf (and its rahmonics). A gear's sidebands, however many and however unequal, become one peak at 1/fr, and the mesh's own harmonics another at 1/fm. The log also separates a source from the path it passes through, which multiply in the spectrum and add in the cepstrum.

Time-synchronous averaging

A gearbox has several shafts, each with its own gears, and their lines crowd the spectrum. Time-synchronous averaging mcfadden-1987 picks out one shaft. The record is resampled at equal steps of that shaft's angle, as for order tracking, cut into whole revolutions, and the revolutions are averaged. What repeats every turn of that shaft (its orders, its gears' mesh and their sidebands) survives. Everything else (the other shafts, the bearings, which are not locked to the shaft, and the noise) averages away, its amplitude falling as 1/√N for N revolutions. The 4 s record holds 119 whole revolutions, which would cut the noise by √119 = 10.9. Subtracting the mesh's harmonics from the average then leaves a residual, in which a cracked tooth shows as one local event per revolution. This page doesn't average: its spectra are of the raw record.

Misalignment and looseness

Not every fault has a frequency of its own. The shaft's own orders carry the commonest ones, and they are told apart by how their orders, directions and phases go together randall-2011.

  • Unbalance: 1X, radial, in about the same amount horizontally and vertically on a round rotor, and about 90° apart in phase between the two: the rotor's forward whirl.
  • Misalignment across a coupling: a large 2X, and often 1X, both radially and axially. The two ends of the coupling move about 180° out of phase. Angular misalignment shows most in the axial direction.
  • Mechanical looseness (a cracked foot, a loose bearing in its housing): many harmonics of 1X, often with half-orders (½X, 1½X), because the part rattles, clipping the motion each turn. A clipped waveform's spectrum is rich in harmonics, as a cracked tooth's is in sidebands.

The page's run-up has an unbalance and a misalignment driving one casing mode: the 1X peaks as it crosses the mode at 40 Hz (2400 rpm), and the 2X at half that speed.

Severity and trends

How much vibration is too much is set by standards. ISO 20816 iso-20816-3 judges machines by the RMS velocity on their bearing housings, over 10 to 1000 Hz. Velocity weights low and high frequencies alike in damage, where displacement favours the low and acceleration the high. Readings fall into four zones: A for new machines, B acceptable for long running, C for limited running, and D, damaging. The zones' boundaries depend on the machine's size and on its support. For a 15 to 300 kW machine on a rigid support they are 1.4, 2.8 and 4.5 mm/s.

The page's healthy gearbox has, in velocity, 1X at 0.3 m/s²/(2π × 29.95 Hz) = 1.594 mm/s peak, 1.127 mm/s RMS. Its 2X adds 0.188 mm/s, the three mesh harmonics 0.139, 0.028 and 0.007 mm/s, and the noise 0.099 mm/s over 10 to 1000 Hz. Together, as the root of the sum of squares: 1.16 mm/s RMS, zone A. The mesh, the largest line in acceleration, hardly counts in velocity.

A monitoring programme trends what it measures: the overall level, and the bands that mean something (1X, 2X, the mesh, the envelope at BPFO). A level in zone B is not the whole story: a steady machine whose reading jumps has changed. The standard counts a change of a quarter of the B/C boundary as significant, up or down, which is 0.7 mm/s for the machine above. Alarms are set from the machine's own baseline, and a trip near the top of zone C.

Worked example: the healthy gearbox

The page opens on the healthy gearbox. Every number here is read off its tabs or worked from its settings.

  1. The lines. The shaft at 29.95 Hz meshes 27 teeth at fm = 808.65 Hz, with harmonics at 1617.3 and 2425.95 Hz. The spectrum reads 0.301 and 0.102 m/s² at 1X and 2X, and 1.001, 0.399 and 0.149 m/s² at the mesh's three harmonics, against the model's 0.3, 0.1, 1.0, 0.4 and 0.15: the flat-top window reads a tone's amplitude wherever it falls between the 2.5 Hz lines.
  2. The bearing. The 6205's defect frequencies, from its geometry: FTF = 11.93, BPFO = 107.4, BPFI = 162.2 and 2 BSF = 141.2 Hz. None shows: the bearing is healthy.
  3. The kurtosis. The record's is 2.40, below a Gaussian's 3, since a sum of steady sines is flatter-topped than noise. An impacting bearing would raise it (see the outer race defect).
  4. Severity. 1.16 mm/s RMS in velocity over 10 to 1000 Hz, almost all of it the 1X: zone A for a medium machine on a rigid support.
  5. Averaging. Time-synchronous averaging over its 119 revolutions would leave the noise's 0.2 m/s² RMS at 0.018 m/s².

Things to try

Each of these sets up the machine (Things to try, top left).

  • Each part of a machine has its own frequencies; a diagnosis reads the spectrum against them.
  • Under a changing speed, track orders: resample at equal angles, and each order stays on one line.
  • Sidebands at the mesh ± the shaft's speed point to a gear; a long flat family to a local fault. Unequal pairs mean amplitude and phase modulation together.
  • A bearing's defect frequencies follow from its geometry; its strikes hide in the plain spectrum, so band-pass round a resonance and look at the envelope's.
  • The cepstrum turns a family of equally spaced lines into one peak at 1/spacing.
  • Time-synchronous averaging keeps one shaft's orders and averages the rest away.
  • Severity is judged in RMS velocity against zones; a change from a machine's own baseline matters as much as the zone.
