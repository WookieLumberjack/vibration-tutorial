Two systems, joined

A machine on a floor, a pipe on a support, an instrument on a panel: each part has its own natural frequencies, measured or computed on its own. What happens to them when the parts are joined? If the parts' frequencies are far apart, not much: each keeps its mode, shifted a little. If two of them are close, and the masses involved are not too different, the two modes split: neither frequency survives, and two new modes appear either side, each a mix of both parts. A vibration absorber does this on purpose. Most of the time it is not the intent, and it is worth being able to see it coming. A useful screening rule, derived below: with μ the ratio of the two modal masses, the modes stay apart, each little changed, when their frequencies differ by much more than √μ (as a fraction), and they split when they are closer than that.

This page takes the chain on the Simulation page and splits it into two subsystems:

  • A: masses 1 to j, on the ground through k1, c1 as before, its tip mj free.
  • B: the rest, with the spring and damper that joined it to A, kj+1 and cj+1, tied to the ground instead. B's "ground" is where A's tip will be.

Each subsystem is solved on its own. Then one mode of each is replaced by a single-DOF oscillator, and the two oscillators are joined the way A and B are, B's on A's (dampers ca, cb beside the springs, not drawn):

That 2-DOF system is solved and its two modes are compared with the chain's.

The oscillator for one mode

An oscillator keeps its mode's natural frequency ω and modal damping ratio ζ, so once its mass m is chosen,

k = m\omega^2, \qquad c = m\,\varphi^{\mathsf{T}} C \varphi = 2\zeta\omega m

(φ mass-normalized, φTMφ = 1).

The mass is the question: a mode has no one mass. It has as many as there are ways to push on it, and the right one is the mass the joint feels (below).

Which mass? The mass the joint feels

Both oscillator masses are "the mass of one mode", but measured at different places, so the formula and the value differ. A and B touch the joint in different ways:

  • A's mode moves the interface, so its oscillator mass comes from how far the interface moves in the mode: 1/φtip2.
  • B's mode holds the interface still and pushes on it, so its oscillator mass comes from how hard it pushes back when the interface moves: Γ2.

A: its modal mass at the interface

A is grounded on the left, and B is joined to its free tip. In A's mode the tip moves, so the natural coordinate for A's oscillator is the tip displacement ua. Take the mass-normalized mode φ (φTMφ = 1) and scale it so the tip moves exactly 1: x = (φ/φtip)ua. Then the kinetic and strain energies (Kφ = ω2Mφ) are

\tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{\varphi}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}}\Bigr)^{\!\mathsf{T}} M \Bigl(\frac{\varphi}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}}\Bigr)\dot u_a^2 = \frac{\dot u_a^2}{2\varphi_{\text{tip}}^2} \tfrac12\,\omega^2 \Bigl(\frac{\varphi}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}}\Bigr)^{\!\mathsf{T}} M \Bigl(\frac{\varphi}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}}\Bigr) u_a^2 = \frac{\omega^2 u_a^2}{2\varphi_{\text{tip}}^2}

An oscillator with the same energies at the same tip displacement has

m_a = \frac{1}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}^2}, \qquad k_a = \omega^2 m_a

the modal mass at the interface. The driving-point FRF says the same thing: near resonance the tip receptance is

H_{\text{tip,tip}} \approx \frac{\varphi_{\text{tip}}^2}{\omega_r^2 - \omega^2} = \frac{1}{m_a(\omega_r^2 - \omega^2)}

so to a force at its tip A responds like an oscillator of mass 1/φtip2. The tip moves more than the rest of A, so per unit of tip motion the mode looks light: ma is less than A's total mass. This is the same modal mass the vibration absorber presets use for their primary.

Why one entry of φ, squared, is a mass. A mode shape by itself has no size: the eigenproblem fixes only its shape. Scaled to 1 at the tip, ψ = φ/φtip is dimensionless (metres per metre of tip motion), and its generalized mass ψTMψ = Σmiψi2 is in kg. Mass-normalizing divides the shape by the square root of that mass, φ = ψ/√(ψTMψ), so every entry of φ has units of 1/√kg, and the tip entry is φtip = 1/√(ψTMψ). Squaring and inverting it only undoes the normalization:

\frac{1}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}^2} = \psi^{\mathsf{T}} M \psi = \sum_i m_i \Bigl(\frac{\varphi_i}{\varphi_{\text{tip}}}\Bigr)^2

The one entry stands for the whole sum, because the normalization put the whole mode's mass into it.

Why only one entry. Compare B's participation Γ = φTMr, the mode projected on its load, which is spread over every mass. B pushes on A only at A's tip, so A's load is a point load, Fetip, with etip = [0, …, 0, 1]T. Projecting A's mode on it, φTetip = φtip, keeps one entry only. The Matrices (step by step) tab puts the two projections side by side.

B: its effective mass

B's own modes are computed with its base held (its first spring and damper tied to ground). In them the interface does not move: φbase = 0, so a "1/φbase2" would be infinite. B cannot couple to the joint through its mode's displacement there. It couples through the force its mode puts on the base when the base moves.

When the base moves by u, the masses of B move by ru, plus the mode's motion relative to the base, φq. r is the influence vector (or direction vector): how far each DOF moves when the base moves by 1 in the direction of excitation, found from the static response KBr = kj+1e1 (the base pulls on B's first mass through its first spring only). Here every mass moves by 1, so r = 1 = [1, 1, …, 1]T. In a 3-D model each node has x, y and z DOFs, and r for a shake along x has 1 at the x DOFs and 0 at the others (plus lever arms for rotations). The chain has only x DOFs, so there is only one direction, and r is all ones. Then

\ddot q + \omega^2 q = -\Gamma \ddot u, \qquad \Gamma = \varphi^{\mathsf{T}} M \mathbf{r} = \sum_i m_i \varphi_i

with the participation factor Γ (M is B's own mass matrix, the diagonal of its masses), and the force on the base is

F = m_B \ddot u + \Gamma \ddot q

With y = q/Γ the base sees exactly two things:

  • a mass Γ2 on a spring of frequency ω, moving relative to it: the oscillator, mb = Γ2 (the effective mass along x), kb = ω2mb;
  • the remaining mB − Γ2 moving rigidly with the base: the residual mass mres. It belongs to B's other modes, which are stiff well below their frequencies. B's base is A's tip, so it rides on A's oscillator: Add B's residual mass to A puts it there.

Units: φ is in 1/√kg and Mr in kg, so Γ is in √kg and Γ2 in kg. Like the tip modal mass, it does not depend on how the mode is scaled: for any scaling ψ, Γ2 = (ψTMr)2/ψTMψ. The effective masses of all of B's modes add up to B's total mass, rTMr = mB. The lowest mode of a chain usually carries most of it; the table lists each mode's share.

Why not A's effective mass?

ΓA2 answers the base question for A: how hard shaking A's ground would drive its mode. But A's ground does not move here, and B pushes on A's tip. For a uniform chain Γ2 is well above 1/φtip2 (1.89 against 1.38 kg in the worked example), because the tip moves more than the average mass. Switch Mass of A's oscillator to the effective mass to see it: the errors grow (in The wrong mass for A under Things to try, from about 0.2% to about 3%), and the model is no longer Rayleigh–Ritz, so its errors can have either sign.

Why this works: it is a Rayleigh–Ritz model

With both choices above, the 2-DOF model is exactly the chain restricted to two shapes:

  • A in its mode φA, scaled to ua at the tip, with all of B carried rigidly along;
  • B in its mode φB relative to its base: ΓφB(ub − ua).

The chain's displacements are x = T[ua; ub], the two shapes the columns of T, and writing the kinetic and strain energies in ua and ub (TTMT, TTKT) gives the 2-DOF masses and springs above ritz. For B this is what the Substructuring page, next, calls Craig–Bampton craig-bampton: the interface's own motion carrying the part rigidly, plus the part's modes with its interface held fixed (here one). Its reduced mass matrix, in q and u,

\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Gamma \\ \Gamma & m_B \end{bmatrix}

is the effective mass plus the residual mass in other coordinates. For A it is one free-interface mode with no residual flexibility.

Being Rayleigh–Ritz, the 2-DOF frequencies are upper bounds: its mode 1 is never below the chain's mode 1, nor its mode 2 below the chain's mode 2. The model is stiffer than the chain because it allows fewer shapes. (Couple higher modes, and a 2-DOF mode can pair by shape with a higher chain mode and fall below it: the bound is by mode number.) Leave out the residual mass, or use A's effective mass, and that guarantee is gone.

The 2-DOF system and mode splitting

With μ = mb/ma, ωa2 = ka/ma and ωb2 = kb/mb, the undamped frequencies solve

\omega^4 - \omega^2\bigl[\omega_a^2 + (1 + \mu)\,\omega_b^2\bigr] + \omega_a^2\omega_b^2 = 0

(With the residual mass, ma includes it and ωa is a little lower than A's own.) Two limits:

  • Far apart (|fB/fA − 1| ≫ √μ): the roots are close to ωa and ωb. The lower mode is mostly one subsystem, the upper mostly the other. Coupling pushes them apart a little: the lower goes down, the upper up.
  • Tuned (ωa = ωb): the roots are given below. Their product is ωa4 and their sum (2 + μ)ωa2, so (ω2 − ω1)2 = μωa2: the two modes are exactly √μ ωa apart, for any μ, one either side, as in Den Hartog's absorber den-hartog. In the lower mode ma and mb move in phase, in the upper in opposite phase. A mass ratio of only 1% already splits them by 10%.
\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_a^2} = 1 + \frac{\mu}{2} \pm \sqrt{\mu + \frac{\mu^2}{4}} \qquad (\omega_a = \omega_b)

In between, the Veering & splitting tab shows the two frequencies as B is made stiffer. They approach each other but never cross: they veer apart, and the mode shapes swap over through the veering. The heavier B (the larger μ), the wider the veering: the two curves stay apart over a band of fB/fA roughly √μ wide. The chain's own frequencies do the same, and so does every other pair of modes of A and B.

Damping

Each oscillator keeps its mode's modal damping ratio, and the 2-DOF damping ratios are exact for the 2-DOF model. Where the modes split, each coupled mode is a mix of both, and its damping is roughly the energy-weighted mix of the two. A lightly damped part joined to a well-damped one at the same frequency shares its damping: that is how a tuned mass damper works. At exact tuning the two coupled modes each get about the average of the two damping ratios: in the Vibration absorber preset the machine alone has ζ = 0.025 and the absorber none, and both coupled modes come out at 0.012. Far apart, each keeps its own.

What is left out

  • The other modes of A and B. Each coupled chain mode near the two chosen ones is well matched if the other modes are far off in frequency. A MAC allemang-brown well below 1 means they take part too. Couple A's mode 2 with B's mode 1, say, to follow a different pair.
  • A's residual flexibility. A's other modes add static flexibility at its tip, which the 2-DOF model leaves out (MacNeal's method macneal and Rubin's rubin, on the Substructuring page next, put it back). That is why the model is too stiff.
  • Non-proportional damping: the modal damping ratios are the diagonal of ΦTCΦ, as in the Simulation page's classical method.

The drive-point FRF

The Drive-point FRF tab compares the receptance at A's tip (or at the chain's free end) of the chain, of the 2-DOF model and of each part on its own. The model follows the chain round its two modes and parts from it above them, where the modes it leaves out take over. The joint also suggests another way to join two parts, without their modes: by their FRFs. At a rigid joint the force that moves A's tip by u at a frequency is A's own dynamic stiffness 1/HA plus B's at its base, ZB (the force B's base needs to move by u with B's far end free), so

H_{\text{joined}} = \frac{1}{1/H_A + Z_B}

at every frequency: dynamic stiffnesses add at a joint, as springs in parallel do. This is frequency-based substructuring de-klerk, and with a measured HA it predicts the assembly from a test of one part and a model of the other. Its weak point is the division: near the joined system's resonances 1/HA + ZB is small, and a little noise in the measured part moves the answer a lot. The page doesn't do this; the Substructuring page, next, joins parts by their modes too.

Worked example

The page opens on the Simulation page's four 1 kg masses on 400 N/m springs, split after m2, mode 1 of each. A (m1, m2) and B (m3, m4 on their base) are the same two-mass chain, so both have mode 1 at 1.967 Hz, with the mass-normalized shape φ = [0.526, 0.851]. A's tip is the 0.851:

m_a = \frac{1}{0.851^2} = 1.38\ \text{kg} k_a = 1.38\,(2\pi \times 1.967)^2 = 211\ \text{N/m}

and B's participation is Γ = 0.526 + 0.851 = 1.376, so

m_b = \Gamma^2 = 1.89\ \text{kg} k_b = 1.89\,(2\pi \times 1.967)^2 = 289\ \text{N/m}

with a residual mass of 2 − 1.894 = 0.106 kg. Without it, μ = 1.894/1.382 = 1.37 and the two oscillators are tuned, so

\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_a^2} = 1.685 \pm 1.356 = 0.329,\ 3.04

and the two modes are at 1.967 × √0.329 = 1.13 Hz and 1.967 × √3.04 = 3.43 Hz, 2.30 Hz apart: √μ × 1.967 Hz, as the tuned case says. With the residual mass on A (the page's default), ma = 1.49 kg and μ = 1.27, and the model gives 1.117 and 3.339 Hz against the chain's 1.105 and 3.183 Hz: 1.1 % and 4.9 % high, both above (Rayleigh–Ritz), with MACs of 0.999 and 0.958. The halves' modes are pushed far apart by a μ far larger than any absorber's, and the upper one is too high because both halves' second modes, at 5.15 Hz, are left out. Leaving out the residual mass makes it worse: 2.0 % and 7.8 %.

Things to try

Each of these loads a chain into the Simulation page and sets the split and the modes (Things to try, top left).

  • Two parts' modes joined stay apart when their frequencies differ by much more than √μ, and split into two either side when they're closer; tuned, exactly √μ ω apart.
  • A part joined at a point that moves in its mode (A's tip) acts with its modal mass there, 1/φtip²; a part whose modes hold the joint still (B) acts with its effective mass Γ², plus a residual mass that rides along.
  • With those two masses the 2-DOF model is a Rayleigh–Ritz model of the chain, so its frequencies are upper bounds, mode by mode.
  • A MAC well below 1 means modes left out take part.
  • Joined by their FRFs, two parts' dynamic stiffnesses add at the joint: frequency-based substructuring.
