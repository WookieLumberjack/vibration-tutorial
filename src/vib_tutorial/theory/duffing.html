The Duffing oscillator

A mass on a spring whose force is not proportional to its stretch duffing:

m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx + k_3x^3 = F\cos\varphi, \qquad \dot{\varphi} = \Omega

The drive's phase φ advances at its frequency Ω: φ = Ωt while the frequency is held, and a sweep changes Ω as it goes. The cubic is the first term a symmetric spring adds to a straight line: a pendulum's sin θ ≈ θ − θ³/6 has k3 < 0 (the Pendulums page), and a string or thin plate that has to stretch as it bends has k3 > 0.

With k3 > 0 the spring hardens: the further it stretches, the stiffer it gets, so a big swing is a fast one. With k3 < 0 it softens. Its force stops rising where its slope k + 3k3x² is nil, at xe/√3, and falls back to zero at the escape amplitude

x_e = \sqrt{k/(-k_3)},

the top of the potential's hill, kxe²/4 above the bottom (the dot in the figure). Past it the spring pushes outward, and a mass carried over the hilltop runs away.

Harmonic balance

Guess that the mass moves at the drive's frequency, x = a cos(φ − θ), with an amplitude a and a lag θ to be found, and keep only the first harmonic of each force. Since cos³u = ¾ cos u + ¼ cos 3u, the cubic's force k3a³cos³(φ − θ) has a first harmonic ¾k3a³cos(φ − θ): to the first harmonic the spring acts as one of stiffness

\kappa(a) = k + \tfrac{3}{4}k_3a^2,

stiffer the bigger the swing. Put x in the equation and match the parts in cos(φ − θ) and in sin(φ − θ) on each side (the force is F cos θ of the one and F sin θ of the other):

\bigl(\kappa(a) - m\Omega^2\bigr)a = F\cos\theta, \qquad c\Omega a = F\sin\theta.

Squaring and adding takes out θ:

a^2\Bigl[\bigl(\kappa(a) - m\Omega^2\bigr)^2 + (c\Omega)^2\Bigr] = F^2.

That is a cubic in a², so at one frequency there can be one steady amplitude or three. The curve leans along the backbone, mΩ² = κ(a): the frequency at which the mass would swing freely at amplitude a,

\Omega = \omega_n\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{3}{4}(k_3/k)\,a^2}.

A hardening spring's leans to the right, a softening one's to the left. Where the curve crosses the backbone the spring and the inertia cancel, θ = 90°, and the damping alone holds the drive: the peak is where the backbone meets a = F/cΩ.

The jump

Where there are three amplitudes, the middle one is unstable (a saddle): nudged, the mass leaves it for one of the other two, which are both stable. (On it the left-hand side above falls as a grows: a bigger swing would need less force than the drive gives, so a nudge outward keeps growing, and one inward keeps shrinking.) So the response depends on where it came from. Sweeping a hardening spring up, the mass rides the upper branch past the linear resonance until the branch ends at a fold, and drops to the lower one. Sweeping down, it follows the lower branch to its own fold, at a lower frequency, and jumps up. Between the folds the two sweeps disagree: hysteresis. A softening spring does the same, mirrored.

Near a fold the steady state is barely stable: a disturbance there dies away ever more slowly (critical slowing down), so a sweep overruns the fold before the mass leaves. The slower the sweep, the nearer the fold it jumps. A jump up takes a few of the damping's time constants, 2m/c, to grow.

Hold the frequency inside the band and start the mass on each branch (Start on a branch): from the lower or upper it stays; from the middle one it falls off to one or the other, depending on the slightest difference. From rest it settles on the lower branch.

How good is one harmonic?

The cubic makes 3Ω too, which the balance leaves out. The measured dots are each drive cycle's first harmonic, so they compare like with like, and they sit on the curve to within about 1 % even where the cubic doubles the spring's stiffness. A strongly driven, lightly damped spring has more: superharmonic resonances, where 3Ω (or 2Ω, with a static offset) meets the natural frequency, so with Ω near a third of it; subharmonic ones, with Ω near three times it, where the mass swings at Ω/3; and chaos.

Chaos

Ueda's oscillator ueda, ẍ + 0.05ẋ + x³ = 7.5 cos t, has no linear spring, light damping and a big drive. Its motion never repeats. The Poincaré section poincare-celeste, the state (x, ẋ) once per drive cycle, shows it: one dot for a steady response at the drive's frequency, three for a subharmonic at a third of it, and for chaos a cloud that fills a folded, layered shape (a strange attractor) without ever coming back to the same point.

Parametric excitation: the Mathieu equation

Instead of pushing the mass, make its spring pulse, with no force at all:

m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + k(1 + \mu\cos\Omega t)\,x = 0.

With c = 0 this is Mathieu's equation, which he met in the modes of an elliptical membrane mathieu. A child on a swing pumps it, standing and squatting twice a swing. Rest is still a solution, but near Ω = 2ωn it is unstable: the smallest disturbance grows into a swing at Ω/2, half the pump's frequency. The timing is what does it: the swing that grows is the one on which the spring is stiff while the mass swings back toward the middle and soft while it swings out, so it gives back more energy than it took.

On a linear spring nothing stops it, and the swing grows as eσt for ever.

Floquet theory floquet says how fast. Over one pump period, 2π/Ω, the linear equation maps the state (x, ẋ) by a matrix (the monodromy matrix). Its largest eigenvalue λ, the Floquet multiplier, is what each period multiplies the growing swing by, so

\sigma = \frac{\Omega}{2\pi}\ln|\lambda|.

The Ince–Strutt chart ince van-der-pol-strutt maps σ over the pump's frequency and depth: rest is unstable inside tongues that reach down to Ω = 2ωn/n (n = 1: the swing at Ω/2; n = 2: at Ω; ...), narrower the higher n. Without damping their tips touch the axis, so any pump destabilizes a spring tuned to them; damping lifts them, so the pump must beat it. For a light pump and light damping, at Ω = 2ωn the swing grows at

\sigma \approx \bigl(\tfrac{\mu}{4} - \zeta\bigr)\,\omega_n,

the pump's gain less the damping's decay, so rest turns unstable once μ > 4ζ.

Harmonic balance with x = a cos(Ωt/2 − θ) gives the first tongue. The pump's term μk cos Ωt · a cos(Ωt/2 − θ) has a part at the swing's own frequency, ½μka cos(Ωt/2 + θ), and matching it as before, with a cubic spring:

\Bigl(\kappa(a) - \frac{m\Omega^2}{4}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{c\Omega}{2}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{\mu k}{2}\Bigr)^2.

As a → 0 (κ = k) that is the tongue's edge: without damping, Ω = 2ωn√(1 ∓ μ/2), and at the centre the damping's term needs μ = 4ζ, as above. A hardening spring's swing detunes itself as it grows, out of the tongue, and settles where κ(a) meets the equation's larger root. Sweep down from rest and rest stays stable down to the tongue's upper edge, where it turns unstable and the swing jumps up to the branch; sweep up and the swing grows near the lower edge (a little past it: at the edge itself the growth is nil) and rides the branch far past the upper edge, where rest is stable too. Hysteresis, as in the forced jump.

Worked example

The page opens on the hardening spring: m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m, c = 1 N·s/m, k3 = 10⁵ N/m³, driven by F = 2 N.

  1. Small swings. ωn = √400 = 20 rad/s, so fn = 3.18 Hz, and ζ = 1/(2 × 20) = 0.025 (the page's 2.5 %). The damping's time constant 2m/c is 2 s.
  2. The backbone. At a = 50 mm, κ = 400 + 0.75 × 10⁵ × 0.05² = 587.5 N/m, so a free swing that big runs at √587.5/2π = 3.86 Hz.
  3. The peak. Try a = 71.5 mm: κ = 400 + 75 000 × 0.0715² = 783.4 N/m, so the backbone is at Ω = √783.4 = 27.99 rad/s (4.45 Hz), where F/cΩ = 2/27.99 = 71.5 mm: that is the peak. The cubic adds 383.4/400 = 96 % to the spring's stiffness there (the page's 95.8 %). Without it the peak would be F/cωn = 100 mm at 3.18 Hz.
  4. The folds. The page finds them at 4.45 Hz, where the upper branch (71.4 mm) ends and drops to 5.23 mm, and at 3.64 Hz, where the lower one (24.0 mm) ends and jumps to 46.2 mm. In light damping the upper fold sits almost at the peak.
  5. Inside the band. At 4 Hz (Ω = 25.13 rad/s, mΩ² = 631.7 N/m) the cubic has three roots: 8.80, 52.0 and 58.3 mm, the middle one unstable. Check the upper: κ = 400 + 75 000 × 0.0583² = 654.9 N/m, and 0.0583 × √(23.2² + 25.13²) = 1.99 N, the drive's 2 N to the rounding.

The pump. The parametric presets pump the same spring by μ = 0.3 at 2fn = 6.37 Hz (Ω = 40 rad/s), with no force. 4ζ = 0.1, so 0.3 is well over the threshold, and the swing grows at (0.3/4 − 0.025) × 20 = 1.0 /s; the chart's Floquet multiplier gives 0.0498ωn = 0.996 /s. Undamped, the tongue would run from 6.37 × √0.85 = 5.87 to 6.37 × √1.15 = 6.83 Hz; damped, the page's 5.89 to 6.80 Hz. On the hardening spring mΩ²/4 = 400 N/m and the larger root is √(60² − 20²) = 56.6 N/m above it, so κ = 456.6 N/m and a = √(56.6/75 000) = 27.5 mm, the preset's settled swing.

How this page computes it

The linear part, mẍ + cẋ + kx, is stepped exactly, as the chain's simulator steps it (a first-order hold on the input). The cubic's and the pump's forces go in as an input that changes linearly over each step, its end value found by Newton from where the step ends. The step resolves the drive and the spring's stiffness at the largest amplitude in reach. The measured amplitudes are the displacement's first and half harmonics over each drive cycle and the one before (over a pair, a swing at Ω/2 doesn't leak into the first harmonic). The chart's multipliers come from a Runge–Kutta step of every cell at once.

  • A nonlinear spring's resonance leans along its backbone, the frequency of a free swing at each amplitude: right for a hardening spring, left for a softening one.
  • Where the curve folds over there are three steady states, the middle one unstable: sweeps jump at the folds, and the two directions disagree.
  • Harmonic balance (one harmonic) predicts the curve well, but misses super- and subharmonic resonances and chaos.
  • A spring pumped near twice its natural frequency is unstable at rest once the pump beats the damping (μ > 4ζ); its swing grows at half the pump's frequency until the nonlinearity detunes it.
