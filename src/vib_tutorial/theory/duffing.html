Start with one mass Friction

The Duffing oscillator

A mass on a spring whose force is not proportional to its stretch:

m x″ + c x′ + k x + k3 x³ = F cos φ,   φ′ = Ω

With k3 > 0 the spring hardens: the further it stretches, the stiffer it gets, so a big swing is a fast one. With k3 < 0 it softens: its force peaks at √(k / −3k3) and falls back to zero at the escape amplitude √(k / −k3), the potential's hilltop. Past that the spring pushes outward, and a mass carried over the potential's hilltop runs away.

Harmonic balance

Guess that the mass moves at the drive's frequency, x = a cos(φ − θ), and keep only the first harmonic of each force. Since cos³ = ¾ cos + ¼ cos 3φ, the spring acts on the first harmonic as one of stiffness

κ(a) = k + ¾ k3 a²,

stiffer the bigger the swing. Matching the cosine and sine parts:

(κ(a) − mΩ²) a = F cos θ,   cΩ a = F sin θ,

and squaring and adding, a²[(κ(a) − mΩ²)² + (cΩ)²] = F². That is a cubic in a², so at one frequency there can be one steady amplitude or three. The curve leans along the backbone, mΩ² = κ(a): the frequency at which the mass would swing freely at amplitude a. A hardening spring's leans to the right, a softening one's to the left.

The jump

Where there are three amplitudes, the middle one is unstable (a saddle): nudged, the mass leaves it for one of the other two, which are both stable. So the response depends on where it came from. Sweeping a hardening spring up, the mass rides the upper branch past the linear resonance until the branch ends at a fold, and drops to the lower one. Sweeping down, it follows the lower branch to its own fold, at a lower frequency, and jumps up. Between the folds the two sweeps disagree: hysteresis. A softening spring does the same, mirrored.

Near a fold the steady state is barely stable: a disturbance there dies away ever more slowly (critical slowing down), so a sweep overruns the fold before the mass leaves. The slower the sweep, the nearer the fold it jumps. A jump up takes a few of the damping's time constants, 2m/c, to grow.

Hold the frequency inside the band and start the mass on each branch (Start on a branch): from the lower or upper it stays; from the middle one it falls off to one or the other, depending on the slightest difference. From rest it settles on the lower branch.

How good is one harmonic?

The cubic makes 3Ω too, which the balance leaves out. The measured dots are each drive cycle's first harmonic, so they compare like with like, and they sit on the curve to within about 1 % even where the cubic doubles the spring's stiffness. A strongly driven, lightly damped spring has more: superharmonic resonances, where 3Ω (or 2Ω, with a static offset) meets the natural frequency, so near a third of it; subharmonic ones, at three times it, where the mass swings at Ω/3; and chaos.

Chaos

Ueda's oscillator, x″ + 0.05 x′ + x³ = 7.5 cos t, has no linear spring, light damping and a big drive. Its motion never repeats. The Poincaré section, the state once per drive cycle, shows it: one dot for a steady response at the drive's frequency, three for a subharmonic at a third of it, and for chaos a cloud that fills a folded, layered shape (a strange attractor) without ever coming back to the same point.

Parametric excitation: the Mathieu equation

Instead of pushing the mass, make its spring pulse: k(1 + μ cos Ωt), with no force at all. A child on a swing does this, standing and squatting twice a swing. Rest is still a solution, but near Ω = 2ωn it is unstable: the smallest disturbance grows into a swing at Ω/2, half the pump's frequency, because each pulse comes when the stretch adds energy. On a linear spring nothing stops it, and the swing grows as eσt for ever.

Floquet theory says how fast. Over one pump period the linear equation maps the state by a matrix (the monodromy matrix), and its largest eigenvalue λ gives σ = Ω ln|λ| / 2π. The Ince–Strutt chart maps σ over the pump's frequency and depth: rest is unstable inside tongues that reach down to Ω = 2ωn/n (n = 1: the swing at Ω/2; n = 2: at Ω; ...), narrower the higher n. Without damping their tips touch the axis, so any pump destabilizes a spring tuned to them; damping lifts them, so the pump must beat it: at Ω = 2ωn the growth is σ ≈ (μ/4 − ζ)ωn.

Harmonic balance with x = a cos(Ωt/2 − θ) gives the first tongue and, with a cubic spring, its steady swing:

(κ(a) − mΩ²/4)² + (cΩ/2)² = (μk/2)².

A hardening spring's swing detunes itself as it grows, out of the tongue, and settles there. Sweep down from rest and rest stays stable down to the tongue's upper edge, where it turns unstable and the swing jumps up to the branch; sweep up and the swing grows near the lower edge (a little past it: at the edge itself the growth is nil) and rides the branch far past the upper edge, where rest is stable too. Hysteresis, as in the forced jump.

How this page computes it

The linear part, m x″ + c x′ + k x, is stepped exactly, as the chain's simulator steps it (a first-order hold on the input). The cubic's and the pump's forces go in as an input that changes linearly over each step, its end value found by Newton from where the step ends. The step resolves the drive and the spring's stiffness at the largest amplitude in reach. The measured amplitudes are the displacement's first and half harmonics over each drive cycle and the one before (over a pair, a swing at Ω/2 doesn't leak into the first harmonic). The chart's multipliers come from a Runge–Kutta step of every cell at once.
