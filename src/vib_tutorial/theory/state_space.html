The modes: the basis that uncouples Coupling belongs to the description
  • see the modes of M and K leak when the damping isn't proportional;
  • start the masses in one of A's own modes and watch it spiral in alone, at its own rate;
  • see why those modes' shapes are complex, and what that means for the masses.
    •

    This page builds on Coordinates & bases, with the same two masses. There, the modes made M and K diagonal, but not always the dampers' C: a change of basis in x uncouples only some systems. Here the coordinates are doubled to x and ẋ together, and in that bigger space there is always a basis that uncouples everything, dampers and all. Its modes are the ones the Simulation page's modal analysis and the Complex modes page work with later.

    The state: four numbers

    To know how the two masses will move, it isn't enough to know where they are; you need how fast they're going too. Those four numbers together are the state, z = (x1, x2, ẋ1, ẋ2). Writing Mẍ + Cẋ + Kx = 0 as "the velocity is the velocity, and the acceleration is what the forces make it" gives four first-order equations instead of two second-order ones:

    \dot z = A z, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ -M^{-1}K & -M^{-1}C \end{bmatrix}

    with I the identity. At the page's start, a 1 kg and a 2 kg mass, 400 N/m to each wall, 100 N/m between, and a 1 N·s/m damper beside each spring, the matrices panel shows A as the first of its steps. The motion is a point moving in a space of four dimensions, which no one plane can show. So the page shows it two numbers at a time, on two planes, and the question is which two numbers go together.

    A's modes come in pairs

    Try a motion that keeps its shape, z = y eλt. Then Ay = λy: y is an eigenvector of A, and λ its eigenvalue. A is 4×4, so there are four. Since A is real, any complex ones come in conjugate pairs,

    \lambda_r,\ \bar\lambda_r = \sigma_r \pm i\omega_r, \qquad y_r = \begin{bmatrix} \psi_r \\ \lambda_r \psi_r \end{bmatrix}

    one pair a mode. The upper half, ψr, is the masses' displacements; the lower half is their velocities, λ times as much, since ẋ = λx for such a motion. Each pair is a mode that swings at ωr and dies away at the rate σr (negative), just as the single oscillator's poles were σ ± iωd. Its damping ratio is ζ = −σ/|λ|. None of this needs the damping to be proportional, or small.

    A real basis: one plane a mode

    The complex yr can be traded for two real vectors, its real and imaginary parts, as the columns of a real basis T. Because A(Re y + i Im y) = (σ + iω)(Re y + i Im y), matching the real and imaginary parts gives

    A \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{Re}\,y & \mathrm{Im}\,y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{Re}\,y & \mathrm{Im}\,y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sigma & \omega \\ -\omega & \sigma \end{bmatrix}

    So in the coordinates w with z = Tw, the two belonging to mode r obey that 2×2 block and nothing else. Each pair turns at ω and shrinks as eσt: on its own plane, its point spirals in, clockwise as a phase plane turns, whatever the other mode is doing. That is what the page's two planes show in State-space modes.

    Started in one mode alone, the other mode's point stays at the center forever. The size of a pair, √(w1² + w2²), falls exactly as eσt, a straight line on the Sizes tab's log axis. Its halving time is ln 2/|σ|.

    Changing the state's basis

    This is the last page's move, made on the state. There, two arrows were the columns of V and x = Vq; here four state vectors are the columns of T and z = Tw. T plays V's part and w plays q's, with their own letters because the page also uses the last page's V and q (they build the second basis). Put z = Tw into ż = Az:

    T\dot w = A T w \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot w = T^{-1} A T\, w

    The matrix in the new basis is T−1AT strang. Why the inverse here, when the last page multiplied by Vᵀ? There, MVq̈ + CVq̇ + KVq = 0 holds multiplied by any invertible matrix, and Vᵀ was the choice that keeps the matrices symmetric, the energies' own. Here there's no choice: the equation has to say what ẇ is, so ẇ must stand alone on the left, and only T−1 does that. (Had the last page wanted q̈ alone it would have needed (VᵀMV)−1 too.) Either way, the eigenvalues are the same in every basis: det(T−1AT − λI) = det(A − λI). The page lists each mode's λ, frequency and damping ratio under the matrices, and they never move when the basis changes.

    The page keeps each basis's columns in pairs, one pair to a plane, so T−1AT splits into four 2×2 blocks. The two on the diagonal are each pair's own motion; the two off it are the coupling between the pairs, drawn in red where they aren't zero.

    Three bases compared

    1. Each mass: x and ẋ. Each plane is one mass's displacement and velocity, the velocity scaled by ω̂i = √(Kii/Mii), the mass's frequency with the other held, so its swing is round. Both off-diagonal blocks are red: the coupling spring and the dampers both pass motion between the masses.
    2. The modes of M and K: q and q̇. Each plane is one of the last page's modes, its q and q̇/ω. The springs no longer couple: each diagonal block is a harmonic oscillator, ω above and below its diagonal, less its own damping. What's left off the diagonal is one term in each velocity row, the dampers' −c̃12 scaled by the modes' masses and frequencies, where c̃12 = φ2ᵀCφ1: the part of the dampers' forces in mode 1 that pushes on mode 2's shape. The inertia and spring forces never do that, φ2ᵀMφ1 = φ2ᵀKφ1 = 0, and the dampers' don't either when the damping is proportional, as when C = αM + βK: then φ2ᵀCφ1 = αφ2ᵀMφ1 + βφ2ᵀKφ1 = 0. (The general test is CM−1K = KM−1C caughey.) The symmetric pair's dampers are proportional: with M = I, C = −3M + 0.01K. With a 2 kg m2 no α and β fit, and c̃12 is −0.51 N·s/m: the heavier mass turned the modes, the springs' forces turned with them (the modes come from M and K), but the dampers stayed where they were.
    3. State-space modes. A's own pairs: block diagonal whatever C is, the blocks built from σ and ω. This is the basis that uncouples any damped linear system foss.

    When the second basis leaks, the dampers pass motion from one mode of M and K to the other. Start the masses in mode 2 of M and K, with mode 1's pair at zero, and mode 1's pair climbs from nothing: the Sizes tab shows it beside the state-space modes, which only decay. With light damping the leak is small, which is why the modes of M and K serve so well in practice.

    Complex shapes

    When the damping is proportional, ψr is the undamped mode's own shape, real: the masses move exactly in step, or exactly opposed, and pass through zero together. When it isn't, ψr is complex: each mass's part has its own phase, so the masses don't reach their peaks together. The page scales each ψ to unit length and turns it to be as real as it can be, so the imaginary part is only what can't be turned away. Alone, the mode moves the masses as x(t) = eσt(Re ψ cos ωt − Im ψ sin ωt): on the plane of x1 and x2 it traces an ellipse spanned by Re ψ and Im ψ, not a line.

    The page draws both parts as arrows on that plane, with the ellipse, and the Shapes tab draws each mass's part of ψ as an arrow in the complex plane, a phasor, beside the undamped mode's real shape. The angle between the two phasors is the phase between the masses. It grows with the dampers, and with how far they are from being proportional.

    An overdamped mode

    Strong enough dampers stop a mode swinging. Its pair of eigenvalues then meet on the real axis and part along it: two real decay rates, λa and λb, and two real eigenvectors. They still make a pair of columns, and the block is just diag(λa, λb): the point slides into the center of its plane without turning. With a 2 kg m2, mode 2 is overdamped from about 18.8 N·s/m on each damper. At critical damping exactly, the two eigenvectors merge into one, and no basis diagonalizes A; the page's dampers pass through that point without stopping on it.

    What it costs

    The state has twice as many coordinates as the masses, and its modes are complex, so the price of uncoupling any damping is a doubled, complex problem. For the light damping of most structures the modes of M and K leak only a little, and engineers use them with a damping ratio for each mode. How much that misses, and how complex a measured mode can be, is Complex modes' subject later.

    Changing it as it runs

    The masses, springs and dampers change as it runs, by the grips on the drawing or the boxes, as on the last page, and the motion carries on by the physics. The basis can be switched as it runs too: the motion doesn't change, only its description, and the planes redraw the recent past in the new coordinates. Drag a point on a coordinate plane to set that pair, the other kept, and let go; drag on the plane of x1 and x2, or a mass, to pluck the masses from rest.

    A worked example: the page as it opens

    A 1 kg and a 2 kg mass, 400 N/m to each wall and 100 N/m between, a 1 N·s/m damper beside each spring.

    1. The matrices. M = diag(1, 2) kg; K has 500 N/m on its diagonal and −100 N/m off it; C has 2 N·s/m on its diagonal and −1 N·s/m off it. So A's lower rows are −M−1K = [−500, 100; 50, −250] and −M−1C = [−2, 1; 0.5, −1].
    2. Each mass. ω̂1 = √500 = 22.4 rad/s and ω̂2 = √(500/2) = 15.8 rad/s. Both off-diagonal blocks of T−1AT are red.
    3. The modes of M and K. 2.421 Hz and 3.624 Hz. In them VᵀCV keeps −0.51 N·s/m off its diagonal, so T−1AT keeps two red terms, 0.41 and 0.333 /s: the dampers couple these coordinates.
    4. A's modes. λ1 = −0.358 ± 15.21i /s, 2.421 Hz and ζ = 2.35 %; λ2 = −1.14 ± 22.74i /s, 3.619 Hz and ζ = 5.02 %, a little below the undamped 3.624 Hz, as the single oscillator's damped frequency was. T−1AT is two blocks, nothing red.
    5. The shapes. In mode 1, m2 lags m1 by 4.51°; in mode 2 by 173.29°, 6.71° short of opposed. Light damping, nearly real shapes: the ellipses on the plane are thin.
    6. The leak. Started 30 mm out in mode 2 of M and K, mode 1's pair climbs to 1.48 mm in 0.41 s. In the state-space modes, mode 2's pair halves every ln 2/1.142 = 0.607 s and mode 1's every 1.94 s, each on its own.

    Things to try

    Setups to come back to (Things to try, under the boxes). Once you've handled the masses, some ask you to predict what you'll see first.

  • The state z = (x, ẋ) turns Mẍ + Cẋ + Kx = 0 into ż = Az, twice as many first-order equations.
  • A's eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs σ ± iω, one pair a mode, and its eigenvectors' real and imaginary parts make a real basis in which T−1AT is block diagonal, whatever the damping.
  • In that basis each mode's pair spirals in on its own plane, as eσt; in the modes of M and K the dampers leak between the modes unless they're proportional.
  • A basis of the state changes A to T−1AT, not TᵀAT: A isn't symmetric.
  • With non-proportional damping the modes' shapes are complex: the masses don't move in step, and a mode alone traces an ellipse.
