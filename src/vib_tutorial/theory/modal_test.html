A virtual modal test

In a lab the FRF is not computed from M, C and K: it is measured. A known force excites the structure, sensors record the force and the responses, and the FRF is estimated from those signals. This page does the same with the simulated chain, so each step of the measurement, and each error it can bring in, can be compared with the exact answer.

What is measured, and where

  • Displacement or acceleration. The Response sensor setting picks what is recorded at each mass. Displacement gives the receptance H = X/F directly, as on the other pages. A lab usually measures with accelerometers and a force transducer, so its FRF is the accelerance A = −ω²H (see Three forms of the same FRF on the FRF matrix page); pick Acceleration to measure that way. The acceleration is that of the simulated chain, a = M−1(f − Cv − Kx), not a converted displacement, so it passes through the same filter, sampling and noise as a real accelerometer signal. The plots then show accelerances: towards 0 Hz they fall as ω² (or, for a free chain, level off at 1/total mass), and above the modes the driving point levels off at 1/m.
  • What the choice changes. The poles, shapes and damping are the same in either form; only the weighting of low and high frequencies changes. Displacement is dominated by the lowest mode, so noise sized to the channel's peak swamps the high modes. Acceleration weights each mode by ω², which evens the modes out, but makes a mode above Nyquist alias more strongly when the anti-alias filter is off. The extraction methods here fit receptance, so a measured accelerance is divided by −ω² first: the poles do not move, but the noise below the first mode, where the acceleration is tiny, is amplified enormously. An analyzer that fits accelerance directly swaps the residuals' roles: the modes below the band give a constant and the modes above a term growing as ω².
  • One column, or one row. Here one force acts and every mass is measured: one column of H, as with a shaker and an accelerometer moved from point to point. An impact test usually does the opposite: one accelerometer stays put and the hammer roves, measuring one row of H. By reciprocity, Hjk = Hkj (FRF matrix page), a row holds the same mode shapes as a column, so either gives every mode, as long as the fixed point is not at a node of one of them.
  • The sensor is part of the structure. A real accelerometer adds its mass at the point it measures, lowering the frequencies of a light structure (Mass loading). Here the sensors weigh nothing, so this error is absent; in practice it is checked by moving or doubling the sensor mass.

Sampling

Each channel is sampled at fs. Only frequencies up to the Nyquist frequency fs/2 can be represented; anything above aliases, appearing at |f − k fs| as if it were real response. So an anti-alias filter removes it before sampling. The filter rolls off below Nyquist, so only the lower part of the band (here 80 %, so fs = 2.5 fmax) is usable. Analyzers usually keep a little less, fs = 2.56 fmax (78 %, the Signals & spectra page's choice), so that a power-of-two block gives a round number of lines. The same filter on the force and every response cancels in their ratio.

Blocks, the DFT and leakage

The analyzer takes blocks of Nb samples, lasting T = Nb/fs, and takes their discrete Fourier transform (FFT). That gives spectral lines Δf = 1/T apart. The DFT treats the block as one period of a signal that repeats forever. If the signal is not periodic in the block, the repeat has a jump at the join, and energy at one frequency spreads into its neighbours. That is leakage: resonance peaks come out too low and too wide, which looks like too much damping.

  • Periodic in the block (periodic random, chirp, a hit or burst that dies away inside the block): no leakage, no window needed.
  • Not periodic (continuous random): taper each block with a window (Hann), which trades leakage for a slightly wider peak. The wider peak still biases the identified damping upward, as leakage does, though much less.
  • Resolution: a peak's half-power bandwidth is 2ζfn. A lightly damped mode needs a long block to get lines across it.

Averaging and estimators

With F and X the spectra of a block (X of the response, displacement or acceleration; the estimators are the same for either), averaging over blocks gives the auto-spectra Gff = ⟨|F|²⟩, Gxx = ⟨|X|²⟩ and the cross-spectrum Gxf = ⟨X F*⟩. Then

  H1 = Gxf/Gff,    H2 = Gxx/Gfx

Noise uncorrelated with the force averages out of Gxf but adds to the auto-spectrum of its own channel. So noise on the response leaves H1 unbiased and pushes H2 up, and noise on the force pushes H1 down and leaves H2 unbiased. In practice the response is noisiest near antiresonances, where it is small, so H1 is used there; the force is noisiest at resonances, where the structure barely resists it, so H2 is used there.

The coherence γ² = |Gxf|²/(GffGxx) = H1/H2 is between 0 and 1. It is 1 when the response is entirely explained by the measured force, linearly. Noise, leakage and aliasing all lower it. With one average it is always 1, which is why at least a few averages are needed. In practice a coherence above about 0.9 near the resonances is taken as a good measurement.

Excitation

  • Impact hammer: quick and portable. The tip sets the pulse length τp, and the force spectrum is flat only up to about 1/τp. Each hit is checked before it is averaged: a double hit (the hammer bouncing back onto the structure) puts notches in the force spectrum, and an overload clips the force or the response; both are rejected. A force window keeps the hit and zeroes the noise after it. An exponential window forces a slowly decaying response to die away inside the block. It multiplies the impulse response by e−t/τw, which moves every pole left by 1/τw. So every mode looks more damped, by Δζ = 1/(τwω), which has to be subtracted when damping is identified.
  • Random (shaker): excites the whole band at once, and averaging removes noise, but it leaks unless windowed.
  • Burst random: random, then silence. If the response dies away inside the block, there is no leakage.
  • Periodic random / chirp: the same signal every block, after waiting for steady state, so both signals are exactly periodic. No leakage.
  • Stepped sine: one frequency at a time, a sine fitted to each signal. Slowest, most accurate, and with the best signal-to-noise ratio: the fit only keeps what is at the drive frequency, so noise falls as 1/√(samples fitted), hundreds or thousands of them at low frequencies. Near the band edge there are only 2.5 samples per cycle, which is enough for a fit at a known frequency.

Nonlinearity: friction

Everything above assumes the structure is linear: double the force and every response doubles, so the FRF is a property of the structure alone. Real joints rub, and friction breaks this. Give the masses Coulomb friction (Ff under System parameters on the Simulation page) and the test simulates the stick-slip motion step by step. The FRF it estimates is then the best linear fit to a nonlinear chain, and it depends on how hard and how the chain is driven:

  • Force level. Friction takes out Ff times the distance slid, which grows only with the amplitude, while the energy a mode stores grows with its square. So friction damps small motions heavily and large ones lightly: as the force level rises, the peaks grow taller and sharper towards the exact (frictionless) FRF. At a low level the masses spend much of the time stuck, the peaks flatten out and can vanish, and the response is barely proportional to the force. The frequencies of the peaks hardly move.
  • The linearity check. In a lab the FRF is measured at two or three force levels and overlaid. If the curves differ, the structure is nonlinear and every modal parameter belongs to one level only. Use Hold for comparison to do the same here.
  • Coherence. The part of the response that is not proportional to the force (stick-slip distortion, harmonics) looks like noise to the estimator, so the coherence drops below 1 even with no noise. Continuous random excitation averages the nonlinearity into a best linear estimate; a stepped sine or a hammer each give a different one.
  • Outside the band. The distortion has frequencies the force does not, such as harmonics of a sine, so above the excited band the response is not zero while the force is, and the estimate there is meaningless.

The modal extraction still fits a linear model, so with friction the identified damping ratios and frequencies are those of the linear fit at this level, not properties of the chain.

  • A measured FRF is an estimate: sampling, blocks, windows, noise and averaging each leave their mark, and the coherence says how much to trust each line.
  • H1 for noise on the response (antiresonances), H2 for noise on the force (resonances).
  • Leakage and windows both widen peaks, so both read as extra damping; an exponential window's share can be taken off exactly.
  • Filter before sampling, or a mode above Nyquist shows up as a false one below it.
  • Curve fitting turns the FRFs into poles and shapes; the stabilization diagram separates the physical poles from the fit's.
  • If the FRF changes with the force level, the structure is nonlinear and its modal parameters belong to that level.
