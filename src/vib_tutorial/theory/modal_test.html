A virtual modal test

In a lab the FRF is not computed from M, C and K: it is measured. A known force excites the structure, sensors record the force and the responses, and the FRF is estimated from those signals. This page does the same with the simulated chain, so each step of the measurement, and each error it can bring in, can be compared with the exact answer.

The page's test is the one drawn: the force at one mass (a hammer, or a shaker through a thin rod, the stinger, and a force gauge), and a sensor on every mass. The steps below follow the signals from the sensors to the modes: what is measured, how it is sampled and cut into blocks, how the blocks become an FRF, how the structure is excited, and how the modes are fitted to the result.

What is measured, and where

  • Displacement or acceleration. The Response sensor setting picks what is recorded at each mass. Displacement gives the receptance H = X/F directly, as on the other pages. A lab usually measures with accelerometers and a force gauge, so its FRF is the accelerance A = −Ω²H (see Three forms of the same FRF on the FRF matrix page); pick Acceleration to measure that way. The acceleration is that of the simulated chain, from its equation of motion, not a converted displacement, so it passes through the same filter, sampling and noise as a real accelerometer signal. The plots then show accelerances: towards 0 Hz they fall as Ω² (or, for a free chain, level off at 1/total mass), and above the modes the driving point levels off at 1/mj.
  • What the choice changes. The poles, shapes and damping are the same in either form; only the weighting of low and high frequencies changes. Displacement is dominated by the lowest mode, so noise sized to the channel's peak swamps the high modes. Acceleration weights each mode by Ω², which evens the modes out, but makes a mode above Nyquist alias more strongly when the anti-alias filter is off. The extraction methods here fit receptance, so a measured accelerance is divided by −Ω² first: the poles do not move, but the noise below the first mode, where the acceleration is tiny, is amplified enormously. An analyzer that fits accelerance directly swaps the residuals' roles: the modes below the band give a constant and the modes above a term growing as Ω².
  • One column, or one row. Here one force acts and every mass is measured: one column of H, Hjk for every j, as with a shaker and an accelerometer moved from point to point. An impact test usually does the opposite: one accelerometer stays put and the hammer roves, measuring one row of H. By reciprocity, Hjk = Hkj (FRF matrix page), a row holds the same mode shapes as a column, so either gives every mode, as long as the fixed point is not at a node of one of them. The fixed point (the force's, or the sensor's) is the test's reference.
  • The sensor is part of the structure. A real accelerometer adds its mass ms at the point it measures, lowering the frequencies of a light structure (Mass loading). Here the sensors weigh nothing, so this error is absent; in practice it is checked by moving or doubling the sensor mass.

Sampling

Each channel is sampled at fs. Only frequencies up to the Nyquist frequency fs/2 can be represented; anything above aliases, appearing at

f_{\text{shown}} = \bigl|\,f - \mathrm{round}(f/f_s)\,f_s\bigr|

as if it were real response (Aliasing). So an anti-alias filter removes it before sampling. The filter rolls off below Nyquist, so only the lower part of the band (here 80 %, so fs = 2.5 fmax) is usable. Analyzers usually keep a little less, fs = 2.56 fmax (78 %, the Signals & spectra page's choice), so that a power-of-two block gives a round number of lines. The same filter on the force and every response cancels in their ratio. Auto picks the sample rate that puts the highest mode a quarter inside the usable band, fmax = 1.25 fd of the top mode, rounded up.

Blocks, the DFT and leakage

The analyzer takes blocks of Nb samples, lasting

T = \frac{N_b}{f_s}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{1}{T}

and takes their discrete Fourier transform (FFT), whose spectral lines are Δf apart. The DFT treats the block as one period of a signal that repeats forever. If the signal is not periodic in the block, the repeat has a jump at the join, and energy at one frequency spreads into its neighbours. That is leakage: resonance peaks come out too low and too wide, which looks like too much damping.

  • Periodic in the block (periodic random, chirp, a hit or burst that dies away inside the block): no leakage, no window needed. A free decay has e−ζωnT of itself left at the block's end (the Left at T column of the Setup tab); under about 1 % it has died away.
  • Not periodic (continuous random): taper each block with a window w (Hann), which trades leakage for a slightly wider peak. The wider peak still biases the identified damping upward, as leakage does, though much less.

Resolution. A peak's half-power bandwidth is 2ζfn, and the lines across it are

\frac{2\zeta f_n}{\Delta f} = 2\zeta f_n T

should be at least about 2, or the peak falls between lines and reads too low. A lightly damped mode needs a long block.

Averaging and estimators

With F and X the spectra of a block (X of the response, displacement or acceleration; the estimators are the same for either), averaging over blocks gives the auto-spectra and the cross-spectrum

G_{ff} = \langle |F|^2 \rangle, \quad G_{xx} = \langle |X|^2 \rangle, \quad G_{xf} = \langle X F^* \rangle

(Gfx is Gxf's conjugate). Then

H_1 = \frac{G_{xf}}{G_{ff}}, \qquad H_2 = \frac{G_{xx}}{G_{fx}}

Noise uncorrelated with the force averages out of Gxf but adds to the auto-spectrum of its own channel. So noise on the response leaves H1 unbiased and pushes H2 up, and noise on the force pushes H1 down and leaves H2 unbiased bendat-piersol. In practice the response is noisiest near antiresonances, where it is small, so H1 is used there; the force is noisiest at resonances, where the structure barely resists it (Shaker and structure), so H2 is used there.

The coherence

\gamma^2 = \frac{|G_{xf}|^2}{G_{ff}\,G_{xx}} = \frac{H_1}{H_2}

is between 0 and 1. It is 1 when the response is entirely explained by the measured force, linearly. Noise, leakage and aliasing all lower it. With one average it is always 1, which is why at least a few averages are needed. In practice a coherence above about 0.9 near the resonances is taken as a good measurement.

Excitation

Impact hammer: quick and portable. The tip sets the pulse length τp, and the force spectrum is flat only up to about 1/τp (a half-sine pulse's first zero is at 1.5/τp). Each hit is checked before it is averaged: a double hit (the hammer bouncing back onto the structure) puts notches in the force spectrum, and an overload clips the force or the response; both are rejected. A force window keeps the hit and zeroes the noise after it. An exponential window

w(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_w}

forces a slowly decaying response to die away inside the block. It multiplies every mode of the impulse response, e−ζωnt, so it moves every pole left by 1/τw, and every mode looks more damped, by

\Delta\zeta = \frac{1}{\tau_w\,\omega_n}

which has to be subtracted when damping is identified trethewey-cafeo. A window that falls to 5 % at the block's end has τw = T/ln 20.

A shaker can drive other signals:

  • Random (shaker): excites the whole band at once, and averaging removes noise, but it leaks unless windowed.
  • Burst random: random, then silence. If the response dies away inside the block, there is no leakage.
  • Periodic random / chirp: the same signal every block, after waiting for steady state, so both signals are exactly periodic. No leakage.
  • Stepped sine: one frequency at a time, a sine fitted to each signal. Slowest, most accurate, and with the best signal-to-noise ratio: the fit only keeps what is at the drive frequency, so noise falls as 1/√(samples fitted), hundreds or thousands of them at low frequencies. Near the band edge there are only 2.5 samples per cycle, which is enough for a fit at a known frequency.

Shaker and structure

A shaker is not a pure force. Its coil pushes on its armature, a mass msh on a soft suspension, and the armature pushes the structure through the stinger:

The coil's force F0 both accelerates the armature, which moves with the drive point, and pushes the structure with F. With the drive point's accelerance Akk, its acceleration is AkkF, so F0 = F + mshAkkF and

\frac{F}{F_0} = \frac{1}{1 + m_{sh}A_{kk}}

At a resonance the structure moves freely and |Akk| peaks, so most of the coil's force goes into shaking the armature: the force that reaches the structure drops off, in a dip at every resonance. The suspension, and the coil's back-EMF when the amplifier holds the voltage (it acts as a damper), act in the same way: in general the shaker's impedance Zsh, the force it spends per unit velocity of the drive point, times that point's mobility iΩHkk, takes the place of mshAkk mcconnell-varoto.

  • The FRF is still right. The gauge measures F itself, where it enters the structure, so X/F is the structure's own FRF whatever the shaker does. That is why the gauge goes at the structure end of the stinger, not on the shaker.
  • But the force is smallest where it matters. At the resonances the force channel's signal dips, its noise doesn't, and H1 reads low there: the reason to use H2 at resonances (above). A lighter armature, or a current-driven amplifier (no back-EMF damping), makes the dip shallower.
  • The stinger is stiff along its axis and flexible across it, so the shaker pushes along one line and adds no moment or side force, which the single gauge couldn't measure.
  • Mass above the gauge. The part of the gauge between its sensing element and the structure moves with the structure, and its inertia is measured as if it were the structure's; for a light structure it is subtracted (mass cancellation).

The page's shaker is ideal: the force it asks for is the force applied. The worked example below sizes the dip a real one would have.

More than one reference

This page measures one column of H: one reference. Real tests often use several, two or more shakers (or fixed accelerometers, with the hammer roving), because one reference has blind spots:

  • Nodes. A mode with a node at the reference is missing from every FRF (the first trial). A second reference elsewhere catches it.
  • Close and repeated modes. Each mode's term in a column is its shape times its value at the reference, φjrφkr. Two modes at (nearly) the same frequency, as a symmetric structure has, add into one peak, and one column can't say how much of it belongs to each: it holds only one combination of their shapes. With two references, the two columns hold two different combinations, and both shapes can be separated.
  • Consistency. A mode's frequency and damping are the structure's, the same in every column; a fit across all the references at once gives one value for each instead of one per column.

With several shakers driven at once (MIMO, multiple input, multiple output), the forces must be uncorrelated (independent random signals), and the estimators become matrix ones: the forces' spectra make a vector F, the auto-spectra a matrix Gff, the cross-spectra another, and

G_{xf} = H\,G_{ff} \;\Rightarrow\; H_1 = G_{xf}\,G_{ff}^{-1}

which needs Gff invertible: correlated forces can't be told apart. The multiple coherence plays the part of γ², for each response against all the forces together bendat-piersol. Polyreference fitting methods fit every column at once: the polyreference complex exponential method in the time domain vold-polyref, and the poly-reference LSCF (PolyMAX) in the frequency domain guillaume-lscf peeters-polymax.

Modal parameter extraction

The measured FRF column is only data. Modal parameter extraction (curve fitting) finds the modal model behind it: for each mode a pole λr (natural frequency and damping) and residues Rjr (the mode shape), with

H_{jk}(\Omega) \approx \sum_r \left[ \frac{R_{jr}}{i\Omega - \lambda_r} + \frac{R_{jr}^*}{i\Omega - \lambda_r^*} \right] + U_j - \frac{L_j}{\Omega^2}

For a force at mass k, Rjr ∝ ψjrψkr: one column of H gives every mode's shape, as long as ψkr ≠ 0 (the force is not at a node of that mode). U and L, the residuals, stand in for the modes above and below the fitted band.

Peak picking treats each peak as a single-DOF resonance: fn at the peak, the shape from the FRF at the peak, and the damping from the half-power points f1 and f2, where the peak is down to 1/√2,

\zeta \approx \frac{f_2 - f_1}{2 f_n}

It needs well-separated, lightly damped modes and a fine line spacing. The other methods here:

  • Circle fit kennedy-pancu: for a single mode the mobility iΩH (velocity per unit force) is exactly a circle in the complex plane. How fast the points move round it peaks at resonance, and the angles give ωn and ζ even when ωn falls between two frequency lines. Still one mode at a time.
  • LSCF + LSFD fit every mode and every response at once guillaume-lscf. The model order (number of poles) is not known in advance, so the fit is repeated at every order and the poles are plotted in a stabilization diagram. Physical modes form vertical columns of stable poles; noise and leakage poles scatter. The residues of the chosen poles then follow from a linear least-squares fit (LSFD).

The Modes tab compares the result with the exact modes: frequency and damping errors, and the MAC (modal assurance criterion) allemang-brown between shapes

\mathrm{MAC}(\psi_a, \psi_b) = \frac{|\psi_a^{H}\psi_b|^2}{(\psi_a^{H}\psi_a)(\psi_b^{H}\psi_b)}

which is 1 for the same shape and 0 for orthogonal ones.

Nonlinearity: friction

Everything above assumes the structure is linear: double the force and every response doubles, so the FRF is a property of the structure alone. Real joints rub, and friction breaks this. Give the masses Coulomb friction (Ff under System parameters on the Simulation page) and the test simulates the stick-slip motion step by step. The FRF it estimates is then the best linear fit to a nonlinear chain, and it depends on how hard and how the chain is driven:

  • Force level. Friction takes out Ff times the distance slid, which grows only with the amplitude, while the energy a mode stores grows with its square. So friction damps small motions heavily and large ones lightly: as the force level rises, the peaks grow taller and sharper towards the exact (frictionless) FRF. At a low level the masses spend much of the time stuck, the peaks flatten out and can vanish, and the response is barely proportional to the force. The frequencies of the peaks hardly move.
  • The linearity check. In a lab the FRF is measured at two or three force levels and overlaid. If the curves differ, the structure is nonlinear and every modal parameter belongs to one level only. Use Hold for comparison to do the same here.
  • Coherence. The part of the response that is not proportional to the force (stick-slip distortion, harmonics) looks like noise to the estimator, so the coherence drops below 1 even with no noise. Continuous random excitation averages the nonlinearity into a best linear estimate; a stepped sine or a hammer each give a different one.
  • Outside the band. The distortion has frequencies the force does not, such as harmonics of a sine, so above the excited band the response is not zero while the force is, and the estimate there is meaningless.

The modal extraction still fits a linear model, so with friction the identified damping ratios and frequencies are those of the linear fit at this level, not properties of the chain.

Worked example

The page opens on the uniform 4-mass chain (m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m, c = 2 N·s/m between neighbours), hit by the hammer at m4 with a 50 ms pulse, displacement measured, Nb = 1024, 10 averages, the sample rate on Auto. Its Setup tab's table:

  1. The modes. A uniform chain fixed at one end has ωn = 2√(k/m) sin((2r − 1)π/18), 40 sin 10°, 40 sin 30°, 40 sin 50° and 40 sin 70°: 6.95, 20.0, 30.6 and 37.6 rad/s, or fn = 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz. The dampers sit beside the springs, so C = (c/k)K and ζ = cωn/2k = ωn/400: 0.0174, 0.0500, 0.0766 and 0.0940. The damped frequencies the table lists, fd = fn√(1 − ζ²), are 1.105, 3.179, 4.862 and 5.956 Hz.
  2. The sample rate. Auto wants the top mode at 80 % of the usable band: fs ≥ 1.25 × 5.956 × 2/0.8 = 18.6 Hz, rounded up to 19 Hz. Nyquist is 9.5 Hz and the usable band 7.6 Hz.
  3. The block. T = 1024/19 = 53.9 s and Δf = 0.0186 Hz; ten averages take 9 minutes of hits.
  4. Lines across each peak. 2ζfn = 0.0384, 0.318, 0.747 and 1.12 Hz, so 2.07, 17.2, 40.3 and 60.6 lines. Mode 1 just clears 2: Nb = 2048 would give it 4.1.
  5. Left at T. Mode 1 decays at ζωn = 0.0174 × 6.95 = 0.121 s−1, so e−0.121 × 53.9 = e−6.50 = 0.15 % is left at the block's end: under 1 %, no leakage, and the faster modes have long gone. With the exponential window at 5 %, τw = 53.9/ln 20 = 18.0 s would add Δζ = 1/(18.0 × 6.95) = 0.0080 to mode 1, nearly half its own 0.0174.
  6. The hammer. A 50 ms pulse is flat to about 1/0.05 = 20 Hz, its first zero at 30 Hz. At the top mode, 5.956 Hz, its spectrum is down 0.73 dB: the table rounds every mode's level to 0 dB but that one's to −1 dB.
  7. The reference. Mode 2's shape is (1, 1, 0, −1) (largest entry 1): m3 is a node. At m4 every mode moves, so the column finds all four; the first trial moves the hammer to m3, and mode 2 is gone.
  8. A real shaker's dip. Mass-normalized, mode 1's entry at m4 is φ41² = 1/(0.347² + 0.653² + 0.879² + 1²) = 0.431, so at its resonance |A44| ≈ φ41²/2ζ = 0.431/0.0347 = 12.4 /kg. A shaker there with a 0.1 kg armature would deliver |F/F0| = 1/|1 + 1.24i| = 0.63 of its coil's force: a 37 % dip at 1.105 Hz.

Things to try

Each of these sets up a test of the uniform 4-mass chain (Things to try, top left).

And one by hand: turn the anti-alias filter off and raise the stiffness on the Simulation page until the top mode is above Nyquist (untick fs Auto first). It appears at |f − fs|, at the wrong frequency.

  • A measured FRF is an estimate: sampling, blocks, windows, noise and averaging each leave their mark, and the coherence says how much to trust each line.
  • Resolution comes from the block's length: aim for two or more lines across each half-power width, 2ζfnT ≥ 2.
  • H1 for noise on the response (antiresonances), H2 for noise on the force (resonances).
  • Leakage and windows both widen peaks, so both read as extra damping; an exponential window's share, 1/τwωn, can be taken off exactly.
  • Filter before sampling, or a mode above Nyquist shows up as a false one below it.
  • A shaker's force drops at every resonance; the gauge at the structure keeps the FRF right, but the force channel is weakest there.
  • One reference misses the modes with a node there and can't split close modes; several references, and a fit across them all, can.
  • Curve fitting turns the FRFs into poles and shapes; the stabilization diagram separates the physical poles from the fit's.
  • If the FRF changes with the force level, the structure is nonlinear and its modal parameters belong to that level.
