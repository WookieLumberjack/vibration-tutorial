The Single oscillator page drove the mass with a force that went on for ever. Real forces start and stop: a hammer blow, a load put down, a door slammed. This page takes the same oscillator through five such inputs, from rest:

An impulse and the impulse response

A hammer blow lasts far less than a period. All that it leaves behind is its impulse I = ∫f dt: it changes the mass's momentum by I before the spring has time to stretch, so the mass starts from where it was with velocity I/m and then vibrates freely. Put x0 = 0 and v0 = I/m into the free vibration: for a unit impulse (1 N·s) on a mass at rest it is the impulse response

h(t) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin\omega_d t}{m\omega_d} \qquad (t \ge 0)

and 0 before the blow: nothing moves until it is struck (causality). A blow I gives x = I h(t), whose first swing is a little under I/mωn.

Convolution: Duhamel's integral

Any force is a string of small impulses: in the moment dτ at the time τ it gives the impulse f(τ) dτ. Each rings as its own impulse response, which at the later time t has been ringing for t − τ. The system is linear, so the responses add:

x(t) = \int_0^t f(\tau)\,h(t - \tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

This is Duhamel's integral (duhamel), the convolution of the force with h. The bottom plot draws it at one instant: h is flipped (time runs backwards from t) and slid along, multiplied by the force, and the shaded area is x(t). Recent force counts most; force from long ago has rung down. Drag the slider and the area traces out the response.

So h says everything about the system: knowing it, every response follows. The integral starts from rest; from x0 and v0, add the free vibration from there. (This page steps the response exactly instead, and sets the integral, worked out numerically, beside it: they agree to the integral's accuracy.)

h and H: one function, two views

Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency. Transform Duhamel's integral and it becomes X(Ω) = H(Ω)F(Ω), with X(Ω) and F(Ω) the Fourier transforms of the response and the force: the frequency response is the Fourier transform of the impulse response,

H(\Omega) = \int_0^\infty h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\Omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t

A long, lightly damped ring in time is a tall, narrow peak in frequency; a quick decay is a broad, low one. The h and H tab transforms h by the FFT and lays it on the receptance from the Single oscillator page. This is why a hammer test works: one blow excites every frequency at once, and the ring that follows holds the whole H.

The step response

A force F switched on and left on is a string of impulses F dτ without end, so its response is F times the integral of h:

x(t) = \frac{F}{k}\left[1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\zeta\omega_n}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right)\right]

(and the other way round, h is the slope of the unit step's response). It rises towards the static F/k but overshoots it, by the fraction Mp of it, at the peak time tp, half a damped period:

M_p = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}, \qquad t_p = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d}

and then rings down about it. Undamped it swings to twice the static deflection: suddenly applied, a load does double the damage it does when placed gently. It settles within 2 % once its envelope e−ζωnt has fallen to 0.02, after ts ≈ ln 50/ζωn, which is rounded to 4/ζωn, four time constants (ogata). Critically damped, it rises without overshoot; overdamped, slowly.

The Laplace transform

Duhamel's integral starts from rest, and working it out for a given force means integrating. The Laplace transform takes the start in too, and turns the equation of motion into algebra: each derivative becomes a multiplication by s, and the response becomes a fraction to be read back from a table. It is the standard tool for transients, and the language of control (the Active control page, later, uses its transfer functions). It turns a function of time x(t), from t = 0 on, into a function X(s) of a complex frequency s:

X(s) = \int_0^\infty x(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t

Think of s as the exponent of a motion est, as with the poles: on the imaginary axis, s = iΩ, it is a steady oscillation at Ω; with a negative real part, one that dies away. The transform weighs x(t) against e−st for every such s. With a large enough real part e−st dies away faster than any response on these pages grows, so the integral is finite.

The transforms needed here all follow from that one integral. A step, 1 from t = 0 on, gives ∫e−st dt = 1/s. An exponential es1t gives ∫e(s1 − s)t dt = 1/(s − s1), and with s1 = −ζωn ± iωd (and eiωdt = cos ωdt + i sin ωdt) the two together give the decaying cosine and sine. A unit impulse, all of it at t = 0 where e−st = 1, gives 1:

Four rules do the rest:

  • It is linear: the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms, and a constant factor stays a factor.
  • A derivative is a multiplication by s, less the start. Integrating ẋ e−st by parts gives s X(s) − x0, and doing it again s² X(s) − s x0 − v0 for ẍ.
  • A delay is a factor: x(t − T), started at T instead of 0, has the transform e−sTX(s).
  • A convolution is a product: Duhamel's integral has the transform H(s)F(s), as in time and frequency above.

The equation transformed: the transfer function

Transform each term of m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = f(t) with the derivative rule, starting from x(0) = x0 and ẋ(0) = v0, and collect the terms in X(s):

(m s^2 + c s + k)\,X(s) = F(s) + m(s x_0 + v_0) + c x_0

The differential equation has become an algebraic one, solved by dividing:

X(s) = \underbrace{H(s)\,F(s)}_{\text{forced}} + \underbrace{\frac{m(s x_0 + v_0) + c x_0}{m s^2 + c s + k}}_{\text{free}}

with the transfer function H(s) = 1/(m s² + c s + k). The first part is Duhamel's integral, the second the free vibration from the start, both at once. A unit impulse from rest (F(s) = 1) leaves X = H(s), so H(s) is the transform of h(t); to check, m s² + c s + k is m((s + ζωn)² + ωd²), and the table's last row turns H(s) back into the h(t) above.

The transfer function says everything about the oscillator in two ways:

  • Its poles, the roots of m s² + c s + k, are where it is infinite: the s-plane points of the Single oscillator page. Each pole s1 puts an es1t in the response, so the poles set the free response's decay and frequency.
  • On the imaginary axis, s = iΩ, it is the frequency response H(Ω): the Laplace transform there is the Fourier transform, for anything that has died away.

Back to time: partial fractions

Under a step F from rest, F(s) = F/s, so

X(s) = \frac{F}{s\,(m s^2 + c s + k)}

which is in no table. Split it into fractions that are, the partial fractions:

X(s) = \frac{F}{k}\left(\frac{1}{s} - \frac{s + 2\zeta\omega_n}{(s + \zeta\omega_n)^2 + \omega_d^2}\right)

(put the two over the common denominator to check: the top becomes ωn² = k/m). Write s + 2ζωn as (s + ζωn) + (ζωn/ωd)ωd and read each piece off the table: 1/s is the static step, the rest the decaying cosine and ζωn/ωd times the decaying sine. That is the step response above. Its final value needs no inverting at all: a response that settles ends at lims→0 s X(s), here F/k.

The delay rule builds the pulses from steps. A rectangle of length T is a step F at 0 less a step F at T:

so F(s) = F(1 − e−sT)/s, and its response is the step response less the same step response started T later. This page works it out that way.

Pulses: impulse, step, or in between

What a pulse of length T does depends on T against the natural period Tn = 2π/ωn, through r = T/Tn:

  • T ≪ Tn: it is an impulse. Only its area counts, and the peak grows with it: a rectangle reaches 2 sin πr times static, about 2πr.
  • T ≫ Tn: the mass follows it statically: a ramp's peak tends to 1 (its overshoot is |sin πr|/πr, and none at all for a whole number of periods); a rectangle, which is switched on and off suddenly, still throws it to 2.
  • In between: the worst case. A half-sine is worst at T ≈ 0.8Tn, 1.77 times static undamped.

The peak can come while the pulse lasts (the primary response) or in the free vibration it leaves (the residual response). Undamped, the residual swing has a simple meaning. Once the pulse is over, Duhamel's integral runs over the whole pulse, and with h = sin ωnt/mωn it is a free vibration of amplitude

\frac{1}{m\omega_n}\left|\int_0^T f(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_n\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right| = \frac{|F(\omega_n)|}{m\omega_n}

the force's Fourier transform at the natural frequency. A short pulse's transform is flat at its area I there, so it swings by I/mωn, as a blow does. A rectangle's is 2F|sin(ΩT/2)|/Ω, which at ωn is (F/k) 2|sin πr|: zero for a rectangle of a whole number of periods, which leaves the mass at rest. A ramp's overshoot is the residual of its slope, a rectangle F/T high, and so (F/k)|sin πr|/πr.

The Pulse spectrum tab plots the peak ratio against T/Tn: it is a shock response spectrum. The Shock spectrum page, later, turns it round: one pulse, a bank of oscillators of every frequency.

How this page computes it

An impulse's response is the free vibration from v = I/m, a step's and a rectangle's are sums of step responses, all in closed form. The ramp and the half-sine are stepped by the exact update the chain's simulator uses, which is exact for a force linear between steps (the ramp) and accurate to a few millionths for the half-sine on 200 steps a period. The convolution integral is worked out separately by the trapezium rule, to show that it gives the same.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on the Single oscillator's defaults, m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m, with a step of 1 N switched on at t = 0 and the convolution drawn at 0.3 s.

  1. The oscillator. ωn = √400 = 20 rad/s, so fn = 3.18 Hz and Tn = 2π/20 = 0.314 s; ζ = 2/(2√400) = 0.05, ζωn = 1 s−1 and ωd = 19.97 rad/s. The readout under the oscillator's boxes says the same.
  2. The transform. F(s) = 1/s, so X(s) = 1/(s(s² + 2s + 400)), which splits into (1/400)(1/s − (s + 2)/((s + 1)² + 19.97²)). From the table, x(t) is 2.5 mm times 1 − e−t(cos 19.97t + 0.0501 sin 19.97t), with t in seconds.
  3. The overshoot. ζπ/√(1 − ζ²) = 0.1573, so Mp = e−0.1573 = 0.854: 85.4 %, up to 2.5 × 1.854 = 4.64 mm, at tp = π/19.97 = 0.157 s.
  4. Settling. The rule gives 4/ζωn = 4 s; the response itself last leaves the 2 % band at 3.8 s. The Step response tab marks both.
  5. At 0.3 s, where the convolution is drawn: ωdt = 5.9925 rad, whose cosine is 0.95805 and sine −0.28661, and e−0.3 = 0.74082. So x is 2.5 mm times 1 − 0.74082 × (0.95805 − 0.0501 × 0.28661) = 0.3009, 0.752 mm: the 752 µm under the convolution plot, from its shaded area and exactly.
  6. h and H. The ring's time constant 1/ζωn = 1 s makes a peak 2ζfn = 0.318 Hz wide and 1/cωn = 1/(2 × 20) = 25 mm/N high, as the h and H tab says.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator, the input and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • An impulse I starts the mass at I/m; the response to a unit impulse, h(t), is a free vibration.
  • Any response is the convolution of the force with h (Duhamel's integral): every slice of force rings as h from its own moment on.
  • h(t) and H(Ω) are a Fourier pair; the Laplace transform of h is the transfer function H(s) = 1/(m s² + c s + k), infinite at the poles.
  • The Laplace transform turns the equation into algebra, the start included; partial fractions and a table bring the answer back to time.
  • A step overshoots its static value by e−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²) (twice the static deflection, undamped) and settles in about 4/ζωn.
  • A pulse much shorter than the period acts by its area, one much longer statically; the worst lengths lie near half a period to a period. Undamped, the swing it leaves is its spectrum at ωn.
