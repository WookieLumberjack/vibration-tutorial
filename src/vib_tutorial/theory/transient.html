An impulse and the impulse response

A hammer blow lasts far less than a period. All that it leaves behind is its impulse I = ∫F dt: it changes the mass's momentum by I before the spring has time to stretch, so the mass starts from where it was with velocity I/m and then vibrates freely. For a unit impulse (1 N·s) on a mass at rest this free vibration is the impulse response

h(t) = e−ζωnt sin(ωdt) / mωd   (t ≥ 0),

and 0 before the blow: nothing moves until it is struck (causality).

Convolution: Duhamel's integral

Any force is a string of small impulses: in the moment dτ at τ it gives the impulse F(τ)dτ. Each rings as its own impulse response, which at the later time t has been ringing for t − τ. The system is linear, so the responses add:

x(t) = ∫₀ᵗ F(τ) h(t − τ) dτ.

This is Duhamel's integral, the convolution of the force with h. The bottom plot draws it at one instant: h is flipped (time runs backwards from t) and slid along, multiplied by the force, and the shaded area is x(t). Recent force counts most; force from long ago has rung down. Drag the slider and the area traces out the response.

So h says everything about the system: knowing it, every response follows. (This page steps the response exactly instead, and sets the integral, worked out numerically, beside it: they agree to the integral's accuracy.)

h and H: one function, two views

Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency. Transform Duhamel's integral and it becomes X(ω) = H(ω)F(ω): the frequency response is the Fourier transform of the impulse response, H(ω) = ∫h(t)e−iωtdt. A long, lightly damped ring in time is a tall, narrow peak in frequency; a quick decay is a broad, low one. The h and H tab transforms h by the FFT and lays it on the receptance from the Single oscillator page. This is why a hammer test works: one blow excites every frequency at once, and the ring that follows holds the whole H.

The step response

A force F switched on and left on is the integral of h:

x(t) = (F/k)[1 − e−ζωnt(cos ωdt + (ζωn/ωd) sin ωdt)].

It rises towards the static F/k but overshoots it, by the fraction

Mp = e−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²),

at the peak time π/ωd, and then rings down about it. Undamped it swings to twice the static deflection: suddenly applied, a load does double the damage it does when placed gently. It settles within 2 % after about 4/ζωn (four time constants). Critically damped, it rises without overshoot; overdamped, slowly.

The Laplace transform and the transfer function

The Laplace transform turns the equation of motion into algebra. With x(0) = x0 and ẋ(0) = v0,

(ms² + cs + k)X(s) = F(s) + m(sx0 + v0) + cx0,

so X(s) = H(s)F(s) plus a term from the start, with the transfer function H(s) = 1/(ms² + cs + k). Its poles are the roots of ms² + cs + k, the s-plane points on the Single oscillator page: they set the free response's decay and frequency. On the imaginary axis, s = iω, H(s) is the frequency response; its inverse transform is h(t). A step is F/s, so its response is F/s(ms² + cs + k), which partial fractions turn back into the formula above.

Pulses: impulse, step, or in between

What a pulse of length T does depends on T against the natural period Tn:

  • T ≪ Tn: it is an impulse. Only its area counts, and the peak grows with it: a rectangle reaches 2 sin(πT/Tn) times static, about 2πT/Tn.
  • T ≫ Tn: the mass follows it statically: a ramp's peak tends to 1 (its overshoot is |sin πr|/πr, r = T/Tn, and none at all for a whole number of periods); a rectangle, which is switched on and off suddenly, still throws it to 2.
  • In between: the worst case. A half-sine is worst at T ≈ 0.8Tn, 1.77 times static undamped.

The Pulse spectrum tab plots this peak ratio against T/Tn: it is a shock response spectrum. The Shock spectrum page, later, turns it round: one pulse, a bank of oscillators of every frequency.

How this page computes it

An impulse's response is the free vibration from v = I/m, a step's and a rectangle's are sums of step responses, all in closed form. The ramp and the half-sine are stepped by the exact update the chain's simulator uses, which is exact for a force linear between steps (the ramp) and accurate to a few millionths for the half-sine on 200 steps a period. The convolution integral is worked out separately by the trapezium rule, to show that it gives the same.

Things to try

  • An impulse I starts the mass at I/m; the response to a unit impulse, h(t), is a free vibration.
  • Any response is the convolution of the force with h (Duhamel's integral): every slice of force rings as h from its own moment on.
  • h(t) and H(ω) are a Fourier pair; with the Laplace transform, H(s) = 1/(ms² + cs + k) and its poles.
  • A step overshoots its static value by e−ζπ/√(1 − ζ²) (twice the static deflection, undamped) and settles in about 4/ζωn.
  • A pulse much shorter than the period acts by its area, one much longer statically; the worst lengths lie near half a period to a period.
