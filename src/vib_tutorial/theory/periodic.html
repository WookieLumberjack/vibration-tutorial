A periodic force is a sum of harmonics

Many forces repeat: an engine's firing, a pump's pulses, a press's strokes, footsteps on a floor. A force that repeats every period T, however it is shaped, can be built from sines and cosines: one at the fundamental frequency f0 = 1/T, the rest at whole multiples of it, the harmonics. With ω0 = 2πf0 and harmonic number j, this is its Fourier series (fourier):

f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_j\cos j\omega_0 t + b_j\sin j\omega_0 t\bigr)

Only multiples of f0 can appear, because only they repeat every T too. So a periodic force has a line spectrum: its mean a0, and a line at each harmonic. The picture below builds a ±F square wave from its first three harmonics. Each one alone is a smooth sine; added, the 3rd and 5th flatten the 1st's top and steepen its sides, and the sum is already close to square. More harmonics square it further.

Finding the coefficients

Each coefficient is found by an average, because the harmonics are orthogonal: over a period, the product of two different harmonics (a sine and a cosine, or two at different j) averages to zero, and a harmonic times itself (sin² jω0t, say) averages to ½. Multiply the series by sin jω0t and average it over a period: every term drops out except bj's, which leaves bj/2. So

a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad a_j = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T f\cos j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t b_j = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T f\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t

In words: a0 is the mean, and bj is twice the mean of the force times the harmonic's sine. The figure does it for the square wave. Times sin ω0t, the product is never negative: the two agree in sign everywhere, the mean is 2F/π, and b1 = 4F/π. Times sin 2ω0t, each half period holds as much below the axis as above, and b2 = 0.

Worked out for every j, with f = F over the first half period and −F over the second (and ω0T = 2π),

b_j = \frac{2F}{T}\left(\int_0^{T/2}\!\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t - \int_{T/2}^{T}\!\sin j\omega_0 t\,\mathrm{d}t\right) b_j = \frac{2F}{\pi j}\,(1 - \cos j\pi) = \begin{cases} 4F/\pi j & j\ \text{odd} \\ 0 & j\ \text{even}\end{cases}

and every aj is zero: the series below. Tables of such series are in any engineering mathematics text (kreyszig).

Symmetry saves work. A force that is odd about t = 0 (f(−t) = −f(t), as this square wave is) has only sines; an even one (f(−t) = f(t)) only cosines and the mean. A force with half-wave symmetry, f(t + T/2) = −f(t) (each half period the last one upside down), has odd harmonics only. Moving the force along in time changes how each harmonic is split between its cosine and its sine, but not its height.

Height and phase: the complex amplitudes

Each harmonic's cosine and sine together are one sinusoid, with a height and a phase. As on the Single oscillator page, a complex number holds both: with Cj = aj − i bj (and C0 = a0),

f(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{j\ge 0} C_j\,\mathrm{e}^{ij\omega_0 t}, \qquad C_j = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T f\,\mathrm{e}^{-ij\omega_0 t}\,\mathrm{d}t

(the integral for j ≥ 1). |Cj| = √(aj² + bj²) is the harmonic's height, which the Line spectra tab plots, and its angle the harmonic's phase. (The Signals & spectra page, later, finds these lines in a sampled record with the FFT, which splits each into two half-height lines at ±jf0.)

The heights also share out the force's power. The mean of f² over a period is

\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f^2\,\mathrm{d}t = a_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\ge1}|C_j|^2

(Parseval's theorem, parseval): the cross terms average to zero, and each sinusoid's mean square is half its height squared. A ±F square wave's mean square is F²; its fundamental, 4F/π high, carries 8/π² = 81.1 % of it, and the first three harmonics 93.3 %.

Each harmonic through H

The oscillator is linear, so each harmonic drives it as a harmonic force on its own (the Single oscillator page's forced response, at Ω = jω0), and the steady response is the sum of their responses:

x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{j\ge 0} X_j\,\mathrm{e}^{ij\omega_0 t}, \qquad X_j = H(j\omega_0)\,C_j H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\Omega c}

Each harmonic is multiplied by its own |H| and lags by its own φ, with r = jω0/ωn:

|X_j| = \frac{|C_j|/k}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{j\omega_0}{\omega_n}
  • The mean only sags the spring: H(0) = 1/k.
  • A harmonic that lands near fn is amplified by about Q; the rest are not. So a force at f0 can resonate the oscillator at any multiple jf0 = fn it contains: a machine running at a third of a mode's frequency can still drive it, through its third harmonic.
  • The harmonics well above fn are cut by |H| ≈ 1/mΩ², so by 1/j² more than the force's: the response is smoother than the force.
  • Each harmonic is shifted by a different lag, so the response's shape is not the force's, and its peak is not the sum of the harmonics' heights: they don't peak at the same time.

On a machine, the harmonics of the running speed are called orders (once per revolution, twice, …); the Machinery diagnostics page, later, tracks them through a run-up.

Shapes and their harmonics

The page's waves, each as its series (the formulas for the |Cj| the Line spectra tab plots):

  • Square wave (±F): (4F/π) Σ sin jω0t / j over odd j. It has half-wave symmetry, so odd harmonics only.
  • Sawtooth: (2F/π) Σ (−1)j+1 sin jω0t / j, every harmonic.
  • Triangle: (8F/π²) Σ ± sin jω0t / j², odd only, the signs alternating.
  • Rectified sine F|sin πt/T|: 2F/π − (4F/π) Σ cos jω0t / (4j² − 1).
  • Rectangular pulses (F for a fraction d of each period): mean Fd, and |Cj| = (2F/πj)|sin πjd|, zero at every multiple of 1/d.
  • Impulse train (a blow of impulse I every T): I/T + (2I/T) Σ cos jω0t: every harmonic the same height.

How fast the harmonics fall depends on how smooth the wave is: as 1/j with a jump, 1/j² with a kink (a jump in slope), and not at all for an impulse. The smoother the force, the fewer harmonics it takes.

Cutting the series short: Gibbs

The page keeps the first N harmonics. Where the force is smooth, the series closes in on it as N grows. At a jump it goes through the midpoint (0 for the square wave), and on either side it overshoots: by 8.95 % of the jump, however many terms it keeps, the overshoot only getting narrower. This is the Gibbs phenomenon, described by Wilbraham (wilbraham) and again by Gibbs (gibbs). For the ±F square wave the jump is 2F, so the overshoot settles at 0.18F. The response doesn't jump (a mass can't), so its own series has no Gibbs overshoot and converges much faster: the Convergence tab measures it.

The steady state without the series

The page also works the steady state out directly. Write the oscillator's state, its position and velocity, as z = [x, ẋ]. Starting a period from z0, the free motion carries it to ΦTz0, and the force adds zp, where one period of the force would leave it from rest (both stepped exactly, the force by a straight line between closely spaced samples). In the steady state each period ends where it started:

z_0 = \Phi_T z_0 + z_p

two linear equations for the two numbers in z0. That is exact, and it is what the series converges to. Without damping, a harmonic exactly on fn leaves the equations with no solution: no steady state, the swing grows for ever.

Starting from rest

Switched on from rest, the response is the steady state plus a free vibration that makes up the start, as for a single sine; it dies away in a few time constants τ = 1/ζωn. The From rest tab steps it.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on the Single oscillator's defaults, m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m, driven by a ±1 N square wave at f0 = 1 Hz with 5 harmonics kept.

  1. The oscillator. fn = 3.18 Hz and ζ = 0.05, so fn/f0 = 3.18: the 3rd harmonic, at 3 Hz, sits just below resonance.
  2. The force's harmonics. Odd only: |C1| = 4/π = 1.273 N, |C3| = 4/3π = 0.4244 N, |C5| = 4/5π = 0.255 N.
  3. Each through H. r = 2πj/20 = 0.314, 0.942 and 1.571. At 1 Hz, 1 − r² = 0.9013 and 2ζr = 0.0314, so |H| = 2.5 mm/N / 0.902 = 2.77 mm/N; at 3 Hz it is the Single oscillator example's 17.10 mm/N; at 5 Hz, above resonance, 1 − r² = −1.4674 and 2ζr = 0.1571, so |H| = 2.5 mm/N / 1.476 = 1.69 mm/N. Then
|X_1| = 1.273 \times 2.77 = 3.53\ \mathrm{mm} |X_3| = 0.4244 \times 17.10 = 7.26\ \mathrm{mm} |X_5| = 0.255 \times 1.69 = 0.431\ \mathrm{mm}

lagging their harmonics of the force by 2.0°, 40.1° and 173.9°. The force's fundamental is three times its 3rd harmonic, but the response's 3rd harmonic is about twice its fundamental: it carries 80.6 % of the swing's power (7.26² over 3.53² + 7.26² + 0.431²), the 1st 19.1 % and the 5th 0.3 %, as the note under the Line spectra tab lists them. The response rings at three times the drive. By Parseval its RMS is

\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(3.53^2 + 7.26^2 + 0.431^2\right)}\ \mathrm{mm} = 5.72\ \mathrm{mm}

the exact steady state's RMS too. Its peak is not the heights' sum, 11.2 mm: with their different lags the harmonics never peak together, and the steady state swings between −10 mm and 10 mm, as the readout under the response says. The series with 5 harmonics is within 2.02 % of that swing.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator, the force and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • A periodic force is a line spectrum: its mean and harmonics at whole multiples of f0, each coefficient twice the mean of the force times that harmonic.
  • Odd symmetry gives sines only, even cosines only, half-wave symmetry odd harmonics only.
  • Each harmonic goes through H(jω0) on its own; a mode at any jf0 resonates.
  • The harmonics fall as 1/j after a jump, 1/j² after a kink; a jump's series overshoots it by 9 % (Gibbs).
  • The steady state can be found exactly from one period: the state that repeats, z0 = ΦTz0 + zp.
