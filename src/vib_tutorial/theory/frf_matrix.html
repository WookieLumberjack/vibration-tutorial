The FRF (receptance) matrix

For a harmonic force f(t) = F eiΩt the steady response is x(t) = X eiΩt with (K − Ω²M + iΩC)X = F, so

X = H(\Omega)\,F, \qquad H(\Omega) = (K - \Omega^2 M + \mathrm{i}\Omega C)^{-1}

H is an N × N matrix at every frequency. Its entry Hjk is the displacement of mass j per unit force at mass k. Column k is the response of every mass to a force at mk (the Frequency response tab on the Simulation page shows one column). Row j is how every force moves mass j.

  • Driving point (diagonal, j = k): the response where the force acts.
  • Transfer (off-diagonal, j ≠ k): the response somewhere else.
  • Reciprocity: M, C and K are symmetric, so H is too: Hjk = Hkj. Pushing at mk and measuring at mj gives exactly the same FRF as the other way round. For static loads this is Maxwell's reciprocal theorem maxwell-1864, generalized by Betti betti; Rayleigh carried it over to vibration rayleigh-sound.

At Ω = 0, H is the flexibility matrix K−1, whose column k is the static sag under 1 N on mk: the static compliance.

Three forms of the same FRF

H here is the receptance (or compliance, dynamic flexibility): displacement per unit force, in m/N. The same information is often given as velocity or acceleration per unit force, which for harmonic motion only multiplies H by iΩ or −Ω²:

The poles, mode shapes and damping are the same in all three; only the weighting of low and high frequencies changes. Tests usually measure acceleration, so a measured FRF is most often an accelerance (or inertance; see the Virtual modal test page, later). Plotting Im H against Re H (a Nyquist plot) turns each lightly damped resonance into a near-circle. For one mode with viscous damping the mobility's is exactly a circle, which the circle fit of Kennedy and Pancu kennedy-pancu uses. Transfer FRFs (j ≠ k) fall faster above the modes.

Several forces at once

Because the system is linear, the response to several forces is the sum of the responses to each, a sum along row j:

X_j = \sum_k H_{jk} F_k

Tick several forces (or Ctrl+click plots in one row) to see the terms and their sum. The terms are complex, so where they are out of phase they cancel, and the sum can be smaller than either term alone.

Every entry is a sum of modes

With mass-normalized undamped mode shapes φr and proportional damping, the modal coordinates uncouple (ΦᵀMΦ = I, ΦᵀKΦ and ΦᵀCΦ diagonal), so

H(\Omega) = \Phi\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{\omega_r^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega}\right)\Phi^{\mathsf T} H_{jk}(\Omega) = \sum_r \frac{\varphi_{jr}\,\varphi_{kr}}{\omega_r^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega}

Each mode is a single-DOF resonance, scaled by the modal constant φjrφkr (φjr is mode r's entry at mass j): how much mode r is excited at k times how much it shows at j. Every entry of the matrix is built from the same N resonances, only weighted differently.

  • Near ωr, mode r's term dominates, so every Hjk peaks there, unless mj or mk sits at a node of that mode (φ = 0).
  • Below its resonance a mode's term acts like a spring (in phase with the force, or exactly opposite if its modal constant is negative), above it like a mass (180° further behind). Between two resonances the lower mode is already mass-like and the upper still spring-like.
  • If both modal constants have the same sign, the two terms are in anti-phase there and cancel at some frequency: an antiresonance (a sharp dip). With opposite signs they add, and the curve just passes through a minimum. (This looks at the two neighbouring modes only; the others shift the dip, and a large contribution from them can remove it.)
  • At a driving point every modal constant φjr² is positive, so there is an antiresonance between every pair of resonances: peaks and dips alternate, and (lightly damped) the phase swings down by 180° at each resonance and back up at each antiresonance.

Where the dips are

For this chain the undamped Hjk (j ≤ k) has (j − 1) + (N − k) antiresonances, one for each mass outside the stretch of chain from mj to mk (two can fall at the same frequency). They are exactly the natural frequencies of the pieces of chain outside that stretch, with mj and mk held still: at those frequencies the outer pieces vibrate on their own and absorb the force, as a vibration absorber does.

Why: H is the inverse of K − Ω²M + iΩC, so Hjk is a cofactor over the determinant. The chain's matrix is tridiagonal, and the cofactor is the springs between mj and mk multiplied together, times the determinants of the two blocks outside them: masses 1 to j − 1 with mj held, and masses k + 1 to N with mk held. Undamped, those vanish at the outer pieces' natural frequencies. In the undamped uniform 4-mass chain, H22's dips are at 1.97 and 5.15 Hz (m3 and m4 with m2 held) and 4.50 Hz (m1 between the ground and m2). The end-to-end entry H1N has none, so its phase keeps falling by 180° at every resonance.

Truncation

Untick modes to see a truncated modal model, which is what you get from a finite-element model with only its lowest modes kept, or from a test that measured only some modes. Near the kept modes' peaks the sum is close. Elsewhere, especially at antiresonances and at low frequency, it misses. Well below their resonances the missing high modes act almost like springs, their residual flexibility

U_{jk} = \sum_{r\ \text{above}} \frac{\varphi_{jr}\varphi_{kr}}{\omega_r^2}

and leaving them out shifts the antiresonances. Compare the real part: the terms add directly, so you can see the offset left by a missing mode.

Leaving out a low mode does the opposite. Well above its frequency a mode's term is −φjrφkr/Ω², mass-like, so the missing low modes leave a residual mass (or residual inertia) term that grows towards low frequency as 1/Ω². Untick mode 1 and the sum is wrong by about that amount above it. A model or test band that starts above the lowest modes needs both corrections ewins:

H_{jk} \approx \sum_{r\ \text{kept}} \frac{\varphi_{jr}\varphi_{kr}}{\omega_r^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega} + U_{jk} - \frac{L_{jk}}{\Omega^2}

with U for the modes above and L = Σbelow φjrφkr for those below (the residuals fitted on the Virtual modal test page).

At Ω = 0 the sum of every mode is the static compliance (damping plays no part there, so even the classical sum is exact):

K^{-1} = \sum_r \frac{\varphi_r \varphi_r^{\mathsf T}}{\omega_r^2}

Each mode's share of it is φjrφkr/ωr² over the total, which is why the high modes matter so little statically: their share falls as 1/ωr². The header of the large plot lists every mode's share and the error of the ticked ones. That error is the residual flexibility that a truncated model leaves out. Far above every mode the opposite sum takes over: −Ω²H → ΦΦᵀ = M−1, so every mode counts by its φjrφkr, and the driving-point accelerance levels off at 1/mj.

Non-proportional damping

The formula above keeps only the diagonal of ΦᵀCΦ. With non-proportional damping (say, raise only c1), the Classical sum misses the full solution even with every mode ticked. The Exact expansion uses the complex modes of the state-space form instead (the Simulation page's ż = Az + Bu): each eigenvalue pair λr, λr* contributes

\frac{R_r}{\mathrm{i}\Omega - \lambda_r} + \frac{R_r^{*}}{\mathrm{i}\Omega - \lambda_r^{*}}

with complex residue matrices Rr (from the right and left eigenvectors), and their sum is exactly H for any damping. A heavily damped mode can split into two real (non-oscillatory) roots; each then contributes its own term. Where those complex modes come from, and how far they are from the real ones, is the next page's subject (Complex modes).

Worked example

The page opens on H11 of the uniform chain: four 1 kg masses, 400 N/m springs and 2 N·s/m dampers. Its modes come from the closed form: with N = 4, φ1r = (2/3) sin γr for γr = 20°, 60°, 100° and 140°, so

\varphi_{1r} = 0.2280,\ 0.5774,\ 0.6565,\ 0.4285 \varphi_{1r}^2 = 0.0520,\ 0.3333,\ 0.4310,\ 0.1836

(the solver's signs may differ; a driving point squares them). They add up to 1, which is 1/m1: far above the modes the accelerance levels off at 1 /kg.

Static. H11(0) = 1/k1 = 0.0025 m/N: a force on m1 stretches only spring 1. With ωr² = 48.25, 400, 938.9 and 1412.8 (rad/s)², the modes' shares φ1r²/ωr² are 1.078, 0.833, 0.459 and 0.130 (×10⁻³ m/N), or 43.1, 33.3, 18.4 and 5.2 % of it: the header's list. Modes 3 and 4 hold 23.6 %, the residual flexibility the Truncate the high modes trial leaves out.

The first peak. The dampers are proportional (C = K/200), so ζr = 0.0025 ωr: 0.0174 for mode 1. At ω1 mode 1's term is φ11²/(2iζ1ω1²), of size 0.0520/(2 × 0.0174 × 48.25) = 0.0310 m/N, 12.4 times the static compliance. The whole sum gives 0.0311 m/N: the other modes add little there.

The dips. j = k = 1, so there are 0 + (4 − 1) = 3 antiresonances: the natural frequencies of m2, m3 and m4 with m1 held. That is a uniform 3-mass chain on its own, whose closed form gives ω = 40 sin((2r − 1)π/14) = 8.90, 24.94 and 36.04 rad/s: 1.417, 3.969 and 5.736 Hz. They interlace the peaks at 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz. With the dampers the first two show as sharp dips (near 1.42 and 3.99 Hz on the magnitude plot); the third, between the peaks at 4.88 and 5.98 Hz where ζ is 0.08 to 0.09, is washed out, and so is the fourth peak.
