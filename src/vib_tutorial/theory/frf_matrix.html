The FRF (receptance) matrix

For a harmonic force f(t) = F eiωt the steady response is x(t) = X eiωt with (K − ω²M + iωC) X = F, so

  X = H(ω) F,    H(ω) = (K − ω²M + iωC)−1

H is an N × N matrix at every frequency. Its term Hjk is the displacement of mass j per unit force at mass k. Column k is the response of every mass to a force at mk (the Frequency response tab on the Simulation page shows one column). Row j is how every force moves mass j.

  • Driving point (diagonal, j = k): response where the force acts.
  • Transfer (off-diagonal, j ≠ k): response somewhere else.
  • Reciprocity: M, C and K are symmetric, so H is too: Hjk = Hkj. Pushing at mk and measuring at mj gives exactly the same FRF as the other way round (Maxwell–Betti).

Three forms of the same FRF

H here is the receptance (or compliance, dynamic flexibility): displacement per unit force, in m/N. The same information is often given per unit force as velocity or acceleration, which for harmonic motion only multiplies H by iω or −ω²:

The poles, mode shapes and damping are the same in all three; only the weighting of low and high frequencies changes. Tests usually measure acceleration, so a measured FRF is most often an accelerance (see the Virtual modal test page). Plotting Im H against Re H (a Nyquist plot) turns each lightly damped resonance into a near-circle; for mobility and viscous damping it is exactly a circle, which the circle fit uses. Transfer FRFs (j ≠ k) fall faster above the modes.

Several forces at once

Because the system is linear, the response to several forces is the sum of the responses to each: xj = Σk Hjk Fk, a sum along row j. Tick several forces (or Ctrl+click plots in one row) to see the terms and their sum. The terms are complex, so where they are out of phase they cancel, and the sum can be smaller than either term alone.

Every term is a sum of modes

With mass-normalized undamped mode shapes φr and proportional damping,

  Hjk(ω) = Σr φjr φkr / (ωr² − ω² + 2iζrωrω)

Each mode is a single-DOF resonance, scaled by the modal constant φjrφkr: how much mode r is excited at k times how much it shows at j. In matrix form H = Φ diag(1/(ωr² − ω² + 2iζrωrω)) ΦT: every term of the matrix is built from the same N resonances, only weighted differently.

  • Near ωr, mode r's term dominates, so every Hjk peaks there, unless mj or mk sits at a node of that mode (φ = 0).
  • Below its resonance a mode's term acts like a spring (in phase with the force, or exactly opposite if its modal constant is negative), above it like a mass (180° further behind). Between two resonances the lower mode is already mass-like and the upper still spring-like.
  • If both modal constants have the same sign, the two terms are in anti-phase there and cancel at some frequency: an antiresonance (a sharp dip). With opposite signs they add, and the curve just passes through a minimum. (This looks at the two neighbouring modes only; the others shift the dip, and a large contribution from them can remove it.)
  • At a driving point every modal constant φjr² is positive, so there is an antiresonance between every pair of resonances: peaks and dips alternate, and (lightly damped) the phase swings down by 180° at each resonance and back up at each antiresonance.
  • For this chain, the undamped Hjk (j ≤ k) has (j − 1) + (N − k) antiresonances, one for each mass outside the stretch of chain from mj to mk (two can fall at the same frequency, e.g. in a uniform chain). They are exactly the natural frequencies of the two pieces of chain outside that stretch, with mj and mk held still: at those frequencies the outer pieces can vibrate on their own and absorb the force, as a vibration absorber does. In the undamped uniform 4-mass chain, H22's dips are at 1.97 and 5.15 Hz (m3 and m4 with m2 held) and 4.50 Hz (m1 between the ground and m2). The end-to-end term H1N has none, so its phase keeps falling by 180° at every resonance.

Truncation

Untick modes to see a truncated modal model, which is what you get from a finite-element model with only its lowest modes kept, or from a test that measured only some modes. Near the kept modes' peaks the sum is close. Elsewhere, especially at antiresonances and at low frequency, it misses. The missing high modes act almost like springs there (their residual flexibility Σ φjrφkr/ωr²), and leaving them out shifts the antiresonances. Compare the real part: the terms add directly, so you can see the offset left by a missing mode.

Leaving out a low mode does the opposite. Well above its frequency a mode's term is −φjrφkr/ω², mass-like, so the missing low modes leave a residual mass (residual inertia) term that grows towards low frequency as 1/ω². Untick mode 1 and the sum is wrong by about that amount above it. A model or test band that starts above the lowest modes needs both corrections, H ≈ Σkept + U − L/ω² (the residuals fitted on the Virtual modal test page): U for the modes above, L for those below.

At ω = 0 the sum of every mode is the static compliance K−1 = Σr φrφrT/ωr² (damping plays no part there, so even the classical sum is exact). Each mode's share of it is φjrφkr/ωr² divided by the total, which is why the high modes matter so little statically: their share falls as 1/ωr². The header of the large plot lists every mode's share and the error of the ticked ones. That error is the residual flexibility that a truncated model leaves out.

Non-proportional damping

The formula above keeps only the diagonal of ΦTCΦ. With non-proportional damping (say, raise only c1), the Classical sum misses the full solution even with every mode ticked. The Exact expansion uses the complex modes of the state-space form instead: each eigenvalue pair contributes

  Rr/(iω − λr) + Rr*/(iω − λr*)

with complex residue matrices Rr (from the right and left eigenvectors), and their sum is exactly H for any damping. A heavily damped mode can split into two real (non-oscillatory) roots; each then contributes its own term. Where those complex modes come from, and how far they are from the real ones, is the next page's subject (Complex modes).

  • H(ω) = (K − ω²M + iωC)−1: term Hjk is the response at mj to a unit force at mk, and Hjk = Hkj.
  • Every term is a sum of the same N resonances, weighted by the modal constants φjrφkr; a node (φ = 0) hides a mode from a whole row and column.
  • A driving point's peaks and dips alternate; the dips are the frequencies of the chain outside, held at the force and the response.
  • A truncated sum misses the high modes as a residual flexibility and the low ones as a residual mass, most at the dips and away from the peaks.
  • With non-proportional damping only the complex-mode (exact) sum is exact.
