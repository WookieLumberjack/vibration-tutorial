When a beam is thick

Euler–Bernoulli's beam assumes plane sections stay plane and normal to the axis, and that only the axis's sideways motion has inertia. Both fail once a wavelength is no longer long against the depth:

  • Rotary inertia (Rayleigh): each section also turns, with inertia ρI per length. It adds to the mass, so it lowers the frequencies: ω² = EIk⁴ / (ρA + ρIk²).
  • Shear deflection: the sections also slide on each other, an extra flexibility κGA (κ = 5/6 for a rectangle: shear isn't uniform over the section). It lowers the frequencies more, since E/κG ≈ 3.

Timoshenko's beam keeps both, with the section's rotation ψ a second unknown beside the deflection w:

ρA wtt = κGA (wx − ψ)x,   ρI ψtt = EI ψxx + κGA (wx − ψ).

On a simply supported beam the modes are still sin(nπx/L), and each gives a 2 × 2 determinant, a quadratic in ω²: its lower root is the bending mode, its upper one a thickness-shear mode at very high frequency. The correction depends on the wavelength over the depth: under 2 % for the first mode of a beam with L/h = 10, a quarter by the fifth.

Waves that can't outrun sound

Euler–Bernoulli's bending wave has ω = √(EI/ρA) k², so its speed ω/k grows without limit as k does. A real beam can't carry a disturbance faster than its material's waves, and Timoshenko's doesn't: its bending branch levels off near the shear wave's speed √(κG/ρ). Wherever a beam is driven with wavelengths near its depth (impact, high-frequency machinery, ultrasonics), Timoshenko is the model to use.

Plates

A plate bends in two directions. With D = Eh³/12(1 − ν²) its bending stiffness and ρh its mass per area,

D ∇⁴w + ρh wtt = 0.

A simply supported rectangle a × b has the modes sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b), at ωmn = π²(m²/a² + n²/b²)√(D/ρh); mode (m, n) has m − 1 nodal lines across a and n − 1 across b. A clamped circle of radius a has Jn(λr/a) − (Jn(λ)/In(λ)) In(λr/a) times cos nθ, at ω = λ²√(D/ρh)/a², with n nodal diameters and a nodal circle for each root past the first. When two modes share a frequency (a square plate's (m, n) and (n, m), a circle's cos nθ and sin nθ), every mix of them is a mode too: Chladni's sand figures are such mixes on free plates.

Modal density

Count the modes below f. A plate's count grows as N ≈ (S/4π) ω √(ρh/D), in proportion to f, so it has a constant modal density, S/2 √(ρh/D) modes per hertz, whatever its shape. A beam's grows as √f (its modes spread apart), a room's or a solid's as f³, so its modal density grows as f² (they crowd together). At high frequencies, with many modes in every band, mode-by-mode analysis gives way to energy and statistics (statistical energy analysis).
