The continuum's waves Rayleigh's quotient

When a beam is thick

The Continuous systems page's beam (Euler and Bernoulli's) assumes that plane sections stay plane and normal to the axis, and that only the axis's sideways motion has inertia. On a simply supported beam of length L every theory's modes are the sines w = sin κx, κ = nπ/L, and Euler–Bernoulli's frequencies are

\omega_{\mathrm{EB}} = \kappa^2\sqrt{EI/\rho A}

Both assumptions fail once a wavelength is no longer long against the depth h:

  • Rotary inertia rayleigh-sound: each section also turns, by the slope wx, whose amplitude is κ times the deflection's. Its inertia ρI per length then adds ρIκ2 to the mass per length, and lowers the frequencies: ω2 = EIκ4/(ρA + ρIκ2).
  • Shear deflection: the sections also slide on each other, an extra flexibility in series with the bending. Its stiffness is κsGA, with G = E/2(1 + ν) the shear modulus and the shear coefficient κs = 5/6 for a rectangle (shear isn't uniform over the section). Two springs in series: ρAω2 = 1/(1/EIκ4 + 1/κsGAκ2). It lowers the frequencies more, since E/κsG ≈ 3.

Timoshenko's beam timoshenko keeps both, with the section's rotation ψ a second unknown beside the deflection w. The difference γ = wx − ψ is the shear angle:

\rho A\,w_{tt} = \kappa_s G A\,(w_x - \psi)_x \rho I\,\psi_{tt} = EI\,\psi_{xx} + \kappa_s G A\,(w_x - \psi)

On the simply supported beam w = W sin κx and ψ = Ψ cos κx fit both ends, and the equations become a 2 × 2 determinant, a quadratic in ω2:

\det\begin{bmatrix} \kappa_s G A\kappa^2 - \rho A\omega^2 & -\kappa_s G A\kappa \\ -\kappa_s G A\kappa & EI\kappa^2 + \kappa_s G A - \rho I\omega^2 \end{bmatrix} = 0

Its lower root is the bending mode, its upper one a thickness-shear mode at very high frequency. For a rectangle I/A = h2/12, and to first order in (κh)2 the lower root is

\frac{\omega_{\text{Timoshenko}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{EB}}} \approx 1 - \frac{(\kappa h)^2}{24}\Bigl(1 + \frac{E}{\kappa_s G}\Bigr)

The 1 is the rotary inertia's share and E/κsG the shear's, about three times as much. The correction depends on the wavelength over the depth: under 2 % for the first mode of a beam with L/h = 10, a quarter by the fifth.

Waves that can't outrun sound

Euler–Bernoulli's bending wave has ω = κ2√(EI/ρA), so its speed c = ω/κ grows without limit as κ does. A real beam can't carry a disturbance faster than its material's waves, and Timoshenko's doesn't. As κ → ∞ its two roots' speeds tend to the material's two wave speeds,

c_b = \sqrt{E/\rho}, \qquad c_s = \sqrt{\kappa_s G/\rho}

the bending branch to the lower, the shear wave's cs, and the thickness-shear branch to the bar's cb. At κ = 0 the thickness-shear branch starts at a cut-off, ω = √(κsGA/ρI), below which it doesn't travel. Wherever a beam is driven with wavelengths near its depth (impact, high-frequency machinery, ultrasonics), Timoshenko is the model to use.

Plates

A plate of thickness h bends in two directions kirchhoff. Per unit width its section's second moment is h3/12, and it is stiffer than a beam by 1/(1 − ν2), because the plate around a strip stops it narrowing as it bends. With that bending stiffness and ρh its mass per area,

D = \frac{E h^3}{12(1 - \nu^2)}, \qquad D\,\nabla^4 w + \rho h\,w_{tt} = 0

A simply supported rectangle a × b has the modes sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b). Each has a wavenumber mπ/a along a and nπ/b along b, and ∇4 turns it into the square of their squares' sum:

\omega_{mn} = \pi^2\Bigl(\frac{m^2}{a^2} + \frac{n^2}{b^2}\Bigr)\sqrt{\frac{D}{\rho h}}

Mode (m, n) has m − 1 nodal lines across a and n − 1 across b. A clamped circle of radius a, in polar coordinates r, θ, has the modes

\Bigl[J_n\Bigl(\frac{\lambda r}{a}\Bigr) - \frac{J_n(\lambda)}{I_n(\lambda)}\,I_n\Bigl(\frac{\lambda r}{a}\Bigr)\Bigr]\cos n\theta

where Jn and In are Bessel functions, ordinary and modified. Each sits at ω = λ2√(D/ρh)/a2, with λ a root of the clamped edge's equation leissa. It has n nodal diameters, and a nodal circle for each root past the first. When two modes share a frequency (a square plate's (m, n) and (n, m), a circle's cos nθ and sin nθ), every mix of them is a mode too: Chladni's sand figures chladni are such mixes on free plates.

Modal density

Count the modes below f. Each rectangle's mode is a point (mπ/a, nπ/b) in the plane of wavenumbers, one to every π2/S of it (S = ab the area). The modes below ω lie inside a quarter circle of radius κ, with κ2 = ω√(ρh/D), so there are about

N(f) \approx \frac{\pi\kappa^2/4}{\pi^2/S} = \frac{S}{4\pi}\,\omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho h}{D}}

That grows in proportion to f, so a plate has a constant modal density, dN/df = (S/2)√(ρh/D) modes per hertz, whatever its shape (the edges change only the next term). A beam's modes are one to every π/L of a line, N = κL/π with κ ∝ √f, so they spread apart; a room's or a solid's count grows as f3, so its modes crowd together. At high frequencies, with many modes in every band, mode-by-mode analysis gives way to energy and statistics (statistical energy analysis, on the Vibro-acoustics page, later).

Worked example

The page opens on a simply supported steel beam (E = 210 GPa, ν = 0.3, ρ = 7850 kg/m³), 1 m long, 100 mm deep and 50 mm wide, on mode 1. Then A = 0.005 m², I = 4.167 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ and

\sqrt{EI/\rho A} = 149.3\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}, \qquad \kappa = \pi\ \text{rad/m} \omega_1 = \pi^2 \times 149.3 = 1474\ \text{rad/s}, \quad 234.5\ \text{Hz}

With (κh)2/12 = 0.00822, rotary inertia alone gives 233.6 Hz. G = 80.8 GPa and κsG = 67.3 GPa, so E/κsG = 3.12 and shear alone gives 231.6 Hz. The first-order formula gives 1 − 0.00411 × 4.12 = 0.9831 of 234.5 Hz, 230.5 Hz; the determinant's root, the readout's Timoshenko, is 230.7 Hz, 1.64 % below Euler–Bernoulli. The thickness-shear root at κ = π is 16.4 kHz, just above its cut-off, 16.1 kHz. The wave speeds are cb = 5172 m/s and cs = 2928 m/s.

The plate trials use a 600 × 400 mm steel plate 3 mm thick: D = 519 N·m, ρh = 23.55 kg/m² and √(D/ρh) = 4.696 m²/s. Mode (1, 1) is at π2(1/0.36 + 1/0.16) × 4.696 = 418.4 rad/s, 66.6 Hz, and the plate has (0.24/2)/4.696 = 0.026 modes per hertz, one every 39 Hz.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the structure and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Shear deflection and rotary inertia lower a beam's frequencies once a wavelength nears the depth; Timoshenko's beam keeps both, the shear about three times the rotation.
  • Euler–Bernoulli's bending waves speed up without limit; Timoshenko's level off at the shear wave's speed.
  • A plate's modes have nodal lines in two directions; modes sharing a frequency mix into Chladni's figures.
  • A plate has a constant modal density, (S/2)√(ρh/D) per hertz; a beam's thins out as 1/√f.
