Feedback

Active control measures the motion and pushes back through an actuator. The simplest law is direct velocity feedback: a force on mass a from the velocity of mass s,

u = -g\,\dot{x}_s.

With s = a (collocated) it is an ideal damper of g from that mass to the ground: it can only take energy out, so it is stable at any gain. How much each mode gains follows from the modal equations. Write the motion as x = Σφrqr (mass-normalized shapes); the force u on mass a drives mode r by φr(a)u, and if the gain is light enough that the modes stay apart, ẋs ≈ φr(s)q̇r near mode r's frequency. So

\ddot{q}_r + \bigl(2\zeta_r\omega_r + g\,\varphi_r(a)\,\varphi_r(s)\bigr)\dot{q}_r + \omega_r^2 q_r = 0,

and the mode gains

\Delta\zeta_r = \frac{g\,\varphi_r(a)\,\varphi_r(s)}{2\omega_r},

the same formula as a damper's modal damping. The modes that move the mass most gain most. Too much gain holds the mass still and the rest of the structure vibrates around it, so there is an optimal gain, as there is for any one damper.

Collocation and spillover

When the sensor and actuator are at different places, φr(a)φr(s) can be negative for some modes: the feedback then adds energy to them.

On the uniform chain with the sensor on m1 and the actuator on m4, the two move together in mode 1, so it is damped, and against each other in mode 2, which the loop drives. A controller designed to damp one mode (with the others ignored) spills over into the modes it wasn't designed for, and the unmodelled ones can go unstable balas. Collocated, dual sensor–actuator pairs (a velocity sensor with a force actuator at the same point, along the same line) avoid this: their transfer functions have alternating poles and zeros, and the phase stays between 0 and −180°.

Lag

Real actuators, amplifiers, filters and digital controllers lag. The page puts two first-order lags of τ in the loop (a sensor filter and the actuator), so the force is u = −g ẋs/(1 + iΩτ)² for a motion at Ω. The power it takes out of a collocated mass goes as g times the real part of 1/(1 + iΩτ)², which is (1 − Ω²τ²)/(1 + Ω²τ²)²: positive damping below Ω = 1/τ, and above it the force, lagging the velocity by more than 90°, feeds the motion. One lag alone never lags more than 90°, so it only adds stiffness; two can, and with enough gain a mode of the loop pushed up there goes unstable. A time delay does it sooner. This is why practical active damping uses modest gains, roll-off filters, and collocated pairs.

State feedback and observers

Velocity feedback uses one measurement. With the whole state z (every mass's displacement and velocity, as in the state-space form) the force can be any combination of it, u = −Kcz, and the closed loop is ż = (A − BKc)z. If the actuators can reach every mode (the system is controllable), the gains can put the closed loop's poles anywhere (pole placement). On one mass m, with u = −kpx − kvẋ, the loop's stiffness is k + kp and its damping c + kv; to put it at ωc with damping ζc,

k_p = m\omega_c^2 - k, \qquad k_v = 2m\zeta_c\omega_c - c.

Moving a pole far takes force, so the linear-quadratic regulator chooses Kc to minimize a weighted sum of the response and the control effort over time, rather than placing the poles by hand.

The state is rarely all measured. An observer luenberger runs a model of the structure alongside it, driven by the same force, and corrects the model by how far its predicted measurement Coẑ strays from the real one ym:

\dot{\hat{z}} = \mathbf{A}\hat{z} + \mathbf{B}u + L_o\,(y_m - C_o\hat{z}).

The error z − ẑ then obeys A − LoCo, whose poles the gain Lo places as Kc placed the loop's: usually a few times faster, so the estimate settles before the control needs it. The two designs don't disturb each other (the separation principle), and fed ẑ instead of z, the controller's poles are the two sets together. With noisy measurements the best gain is the Kalman filter's kalman. The catch for structures is the model: an observer of the first few modes still sees the others in its measurement (observation spillover), and they can go unstable as above.

Positive position feedback

Positive position feedback fanson-caughey feeds a mode's displacement through a lightly damped filter η tuned near it, and back with a positive sign:

\ddot{q}_r + 2\zeta_r\omega_r\dot{q}_r + \omega_r^2 q_r = \gamma\,\omega_r^2\eta, \ddot{\eta} + 2\zeta_f\omega_f\dot{\eta} + \omega_f^2\eta = \omega_f^2 q_r.

Near the mode the filter lags the displacement by about 90°, so its output acts as a damper; far above it the filter rolls off as the square of frequency, so the loop leaves the higher modes, and the lag that troubles velocity feedback, alone. It is stable whenever the static stiffness stays positive: the pair's stiffness matrix has determinant ωr²ωf²(1 − γ), so the condition is γ < 1, whatever the structure's damping and the filter's tuning.

Piezoelectric shunts

A piezoelectric patch bonded to a structure turns its strain into charge. Wired to a resistor R, the current through it dissipates energy, and the patch acts as a viscoelastic layer. Wired to an inductor L and a resistor in series, it makes an electrical resonance with the patch's own capacitance Cpz at

\omega_e = 1/\sqrt{L\,C_{pz}},

and tuned to a mode it behaves as a tuned mass damper made of electricity hagood-von-flotow: the electromechanical coupling plays the mass ratio's part, and R the absorber's damper. It needs no power and cannot go unstable. The inductances are large for the patches' small capacitances, so they are usually synthesized from op-amps.

Active or passive?

A tuned mass damper (the Simulation page's absorber) needs no power and cannot go unstable, but it damps one mode, near one frequency, and needs mass. Den Hartog's optimum den-hartog for a mass ratio μ of the mode's modal mass is to tune it to f1/(1 + μ) with damping ζ = √(3μ/8(1 + μ)³). Active control damps many modes at once with a small actuator, at the cost of power, sensors and stability margins. Hybrid and semi-active devices (variable dampers, magnetorheological fluid) sit in between.

Skyhook

A passive isolator's damper connects the machine to the floor, so above √2 fn it passes the floor's motion through: more damping, less resonance, worse isolation. A skyhook damper karnopp acts between the machine and a fixed point in space: impossible passively, but an actuator fed the machine's absolute velocity does it.

The floor's motion y then reaches the machine through the spring alone, and the damper only resists the machine's own motion, so at the frequency ratio r

T = \frac{1}{\lvert 1 - r^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_{sky}\,r\rvert}:

a bounded peak, 1/(2ζsky√(1 − ζsky²)), and the full −40 dB/decade roll-off. Semi-active car suspensions approximate it with a damper whose setting follows the sign of the velocities.

Worked example

The page's chain is the uniform one: four 1 kg masses on 400 N/m springs with 2 N·s/m dampers.

  1. Its modes. 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz, damped 1.74, 5.00, 7.66 and 9.40 %; at m4 the mass-normalized shapes are 0.657, −0.577, −0.429 and 0.228.
  2. Velocity feedback at the tip (g = 20 N·s/m on m4). Mode 2 (at 20 rad/s, where 0.577² = ⅓): 20 × ⅓/(2 × 20) = 0.167, so 0.217 in all, against the page's 0.214. Mode 1: 20 × 0.657²/(2 × 2π × 1.105) = 0.622, so 0.639, against the page's 0.719: at this gain mode 1 is no longer light, and the estimate falls short. Mode 1's damping peaks at 0.98 near g = 25 N·s/m.
  3. Spillover (sensor on m1). In mode 2 m1 swings +0.577, so Δζ2 = 20 × (−⅓)/40 = −0.167: from 0.05 to −0.117 by the estimate, −0.10 exactly.
  4. Lag. With τ = 20 ms the loop feeds any motion above 1/τ = 50 rad/s, 7.96 Hz.
  5. A tuned mass damper at m4, μ = 5 %: mode 1's modal mass there is 1/0.657² = 2.32 kg, so 0.116 kg, tuned to 1.1055/1.05 = 1.053 Hz with ζ = √(0.15/(8 × 1.05³)) = 0.127.
  6. Skyhook (ζsky = 0.5): the peak is 1/(2 × 0.5 × √0.75) = 1.155, and at 5 fn 1/|1 − 25 + 5i| = 0.0408, against a passive ζ = 0.1's 0.0589.
  7. State feedback on one of the chain's elements (1 kg, 400 N/m, 2 N·s/m): to put it at 30 rad/s with ζc = 0.7, kp = 900 − 400 = 500 N/m and kv = 2 × 0.7 × 30 − 2 = 40 N·s/m.
  8. Its observer, measuring x, at 100 rad/s and 0.7: the error's characteristic polynomial is λ² + (l1 + c/m)λ + (k/m + l2 + l1c/m) for Lo = [l1, l2], so l1 = 140 − 2 = 138 /s and l2 = 10 000 − 400 − 276 = 9324 /s².
  9. A shunt: a patch of 100 nF tuned to a 50 Hz mode needs L = 1/((2π × 50)² × 10⁻⁷) = 101 H.

Things to try

Each of these loads the uniform chain into the Simulation page and sets up the loop (Things to try, top left).

  • Collocated velocity feedback is an active damper: stable at any gain (ideally), with an optimal gain.
  • Each mode gains Δζ = gφr(a)φr(s)/2ωr; non-collocated, it can be negative: spillover.
  • Lag in the loop limits the gain even when collocated: past 1/τ two lags turn the damper into a source.
  • State feedback can place every pole, an observer estimates the state it needs, and positive position feedback and piezo shunts damp chosen modes robustly.
  • A TMD damps one mode without power; active control damps many, with power and stability to look after.
  • Skyhook damping caps the resonance without giving up high-frequency isolation.
