Feedback

Active control measures the motion and pushes back through an actuator. The simplest law is direct velocity feedback: a force u = −g ẋs at mass a from the velocity at mass s. With s = a (collocated) it is an ideal damper from that mass to the ground: it can only take energy out, so it is stable at any gain, and for light gain each mode gains

Δζr = g φr(a) φr(s) / 2ωr

(φ mass-normalized), the same formula as a damper's modal damping. Too much gain holds the mass still and the rest of the structure vibrates around it, so there is an optimal gain, as there is for any one damper.

Collocation and spillover

When the sensor and actuator are at different places, φr(a)φr(s) can be negative for some modes: the feedback then adds energy to them. A controller designed to damp one mode (with the others ignored) spills over into the modes it wasn't designed for, and the unmodelled ones can go unstable. Collocated, dual sensor–actuator pairs avoid this: their transfer functions have alternating poles and zeros, and the phase stays between 0 and −180°.

Lag

Real actuators, amplifiers, filters and digital controllers lag. A lag turns the feedback's phase, so at high enough frequency the force that should oppose the velocity helps it. Two first-order lags of τ (as the page has) reach 180° far above 1/τ, and a high-gain loop goes unstable there; a time delay does it sooner. This is why practical active damping uses modest gains, roll-off filters, and collocated pairs.

Active or passive?

A tuned mass damper (the Simulation page's absorber) needs no power and cannot go unstable, but it damps one mode, near one frequency, and needs mass (Den Hartog's optimum: tune to f1/(1 + μ) with ζ = √(3μ/8(1 + μ)³)). Active control damps many modes at once with a small actuator, at the cost of power, sensors and stability margins. Hybrid and semi-active devices (variable dampers, magnetorheological fluid) sit in between.

Skyhook

A passive isolator's damper connects the machine to the floor, so above √2 fn it passes the floor's motion through: more damping, less resonance, worse isolation. A skyhook damper acts between the machine and a fixed point in space: impossible passively, but an actuator fed the machine's absolute velocity does it. Its transmissibility 1/(1 − r² + 2iζr) has a bounded peak and the full −40 dB/decade roll-off. Semi-active car suspensions approximate it.

  • Each mode gains Δζ = gφr(a)φr(s)/2ωr; non-collocated, it can be negative: spillover.
  • Lag in the loop limits the gain even when collocated.
  • A TMD damps one mode without power; active control damps many, with power and stability to look after.
