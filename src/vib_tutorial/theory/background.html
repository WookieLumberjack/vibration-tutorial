This page builds a chain from the One mass pages' oscillator. The pages in order are listed at the start of the Single oscillator page, and in the Theory window.

Start with one mass

Everything on these pages is built from the single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) oscillator, one mass m on a spring k and a damper c:

m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = f(t)

The Single oscillator page works through it; this is a summary. Set Number of masses to 1 to see it here on its own (with the defaults m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m, c = 2 N·s/m, the numbers in brackets below).

  • Natural frequency ωn = √(k/m), fn = ωn/2π (20 rad/s, 3.18 Hz): how fast it vibrates if nothing damps it.
  • Damping ratio ζ = c/2√(km) = c/2mωn (0.05): the damping as a fraction of the critical damping 2√(km), the least that stops it oscillating. ζ < 1 is underdamped, which nearly every structure is (typically 0.5 % to 5 %).
  • Free vibration swings at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²), barely lower than ωn for light damping, inside an envelope e−ζωnt. The envelope falls by e in the time constant τ = 1/ζωn (1 s), and by the logarithmic decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²) ≈ 2πζ per cycle (0.31), which is how damping is measured from a decay record.
  • Forced response. For f = F eiΩt the steady response is X = H(Ω)F with the receptance H(Ω) = 1/(k − Ω²m + iΩc). Well below ωn the spring carries the force (X ≈ F/k, in phase); well above it the mass does (X ≈ −F/Ω²m, 180° behind); near ωn only the damper limits the motion. At Ω = ωn the response lags the force by exactly 90° and |X| = (F/k)/2ζ: the quality factor Q = 1/2ζ (10).
  • Half-power bandwidth. The peak is about 2ζfn wide where |X| is above 1/√2 of its peak (0.32 Hz). A lightly damped peak is tall and narrow; the measurement pages depend on this.

The rest of this tab shows that a chain of N masses is N of these oscillators, one per mode, as long as the damping is proportional.

Equations of motion

The chain is N masses in a line, mass mi joined to the one before it (the first to the wall) by a spring ki and a damper ci:

Spring i stretches xi − xi−1 (x0 the wall's, 0), so V = ½Σki(xi − xi−1)², and Lagrange's equations (Deriving the equations) give

M\ddot x + C\dot x + Kx = f(t)

with M = diag(mi) and, for four masses,

K = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 & -k_2 & 0 & 0 \\ -k_2 & k_2 + k_3 & -k_3 & 0 \\ 0 & -k_3 & k_3 + k_4 & -k_4 \\ 0 & 0 & -k_4 & k_4 \end{bmatrix}

and C the same with the ci: tridiagonal, because each spring and damper links only two neighbouring masses.

Undamped normal modes (real)

Ignoring C, free vibration x = φ cos ωt requires

K\varphi = \omega^2 M\varphi, \qquad \det(K - \omega^2 M) = 0

The N roots are the natural frequencies ωr, each with a real mode shape φr: every mass moves exactly in phase or exactly out of phase with the others. They all pass through zero at the same instant, like a standing wave.

The eigenproblem fixes each mode's shape, not its size: any multiple of φr is the same mode. A convenient size is the mass-normalized one, scaled so that φrᵀMφr = 1 (its entries are then in 1/√kg). The modes are also orthogonal through M and K. Multiply Kφr = ωr²Mφr by φsᵀ, and Kφs = ωs²Mφs by φrᵀ; M and K are symmetric, so subtracting leaves (ωr² − ωs²)φsᵀMφr = 0. For two different frequencies

\varphi_r^{\mathsf T} M \varphi_s = 0, \qquad \varphi_r^{\mathsf T} K \varphi_s = 0 \qquad (r \ne s)

(The Modal coupling page, later, explains why one entry of a mass-normalized mode, squared, is a mass.) Collecting the mass-normalized shapes into Φ and writing x = Φq turns the equations into modal coordinates q:

\Phi^{\mathsf T} M \Phi = I, \qquad \Phi^{\mathsf T} K \Phi = \mathrm{diag}(\omega_r^2), \qquad \Phi^{\mathsf T} C \Phi = C_m

M and K always become diagonal. Cm in general does not.

A uniform chain's modes

When every mass is m and every spring k, as in the default chain, the modes can be found by hand. Each mass i not at the end has −kxi−1 + (2k − ω²m)xi − kxi+1 = 0, and a sine along the chain, xi = sin(iγ), satisfies it when 2k − ω²m = 2k cos γ: the wall's x0 = 0 comes free. The free end, (k − ω²m)xN = kxN−1, holds when sin((N + 1)γ) = sin(Nγ), which picks out N phase steps, and so

\gamma_r = \frac{(2r - 1)\pi}{2N + 1}, \qquad \omega_r = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,\sin\frac{\gamma_r}{2} \varphi_{r,i} = \frac{2\sin(i\gamma_r)}{\sqrt{(2N + 1)\,m}}

(the last mass-normalized). Mode r fits r − ½ half-waves into the chain, and the frequencies crowd together below the top, 2√(k/m): a chain can't swing faster than neighbouring masses moving against each other. As N grows, the low modes become a bar's (the Continuous systems page, later).

Estimating a frequency from a guessed shape

If the motion is x = v cos ωt for a shape v you guess, the largest strain energy, ½vᵀKv, equals the largest kinetic energy, ½ω²vᵀMv, so rayleigh-sound

\omega^2 \approx R(v) = \frac{v^{\mathsf T} K v}{v^{\mathsf T} M v}

the Rayleigh quotient. R is exact when v is a mode shape, and it is never below ω1²: forcing the chain into a shape it would not take by itself can only make it stiffer. It is also forgiving: an error in the shape gives a much smaller error in the frequency.

Using several shapes at once, x = Wa with the shapes as W's columns and a their weights, gives the Rayleigh–Ritz method ritz, the small eigenproblem

(W^{\mathsf T} K W)\,a = \omega^2\,(W^{\mathsf T} M W)\,a

Its frequencies are upper bounds too, mode by mode (its first is above the chain's first, its second above the chain's second, …), and they fall towards the true ones as shapes are added. The Modal coupling and Substructuring pages, later, build their reduced models this way.

Proportional damping

If Cm is diagonal, every mode obeys its own independent single-mass equation

\ddot q_r + 2\zeta_r\omega_r\dot q_r + \omega_r^2 q_r = \varphi_r^{\mathsf T} f, \qquad \zeta_r = \frac{C_{m,rr}}{2\omega_r}

This happens for Rayleigh damping, C = αM + βK rayleigh-sound. Then ΦᵀCΦ = αI + β diag(ωr²), so

\zeta_r = \frac{\alpha}{2\omega_r} + \frac{\beta\omega_r}{2}

The mass-proportional part damps the low modes most, the stiffness-proportional part the high ones. In this app the simplest case is every damper being the same multiple of its spring (all ci/ki = β), as in the default chain: C = βK with β = 2/400 = 0.005 s, so ζr = βωr/2 grows in proportion to frequency. (Mass-proportional damping cannot be built from this chain: it would need a damper from every mass to the ground.) The damped modes then have the same real shapes as the undamped ones, and ζ modal is exact. The general condition, Caughey and O'Kelly's, is CM−1K = KM−1C caughey; Rayleigh damping is the most-used case of it.

Watching the modal coordinates

Set Plot coordinates (above the time histories) to Modal to plot the motion in modal coordinates instead of mass displacements: one curve per mode, in the mode colors. Each curve is scaled to metres: it is that mode's share of the displacement of the mass the mode moves most. It follows the Method selector:

  • Classical: q = ΦᵀMx. Since x = Φq exactly, the curves hold all of the motion. With proportional damping each qr is its own damped oscillator. With non-proportional damping the off-diagonal terms of Cm make each qr push on the others, and you see motion move from one curve to another.
  • State-space: the complex modes' coordinates, which stay independent for any damping (the Complex modes page, later).

This is also how any free vibration is worked out by hand: project the starting state onto the modes,

q_r(0) = \varphi_r^{\mathsf T} M x(0), \qquad \dot q_r(0) = \varphi_r^{\mathsf T} M \dot x(0)

let each qr decay as its own single-mass oscillator, and add them back, x(t) = Σφrqr(t) (exact for proportional damping). A mode release starts with one qr only; a pluck starts with all of them.

Under a harmonic force tuned to a natural frequency, the modal view shows which mode takes up the energy. The physical view shows the same motion as a mix of all of them.

Energy

The panel beside the animation tracks the energy. The stored energy is kinetic plus potential, T + V = ½ẋᵀMẋ + ½xᵀKx. Multiplying the equations of motion by ẋᵀ gives the power balance

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(T + V) = f^{\mathsf T}\dot x - \dot x^{\mathsf T} C \dot x

The force adds power fᵀẋ, which can be negative when it pushes against the motion, and the dampers always remove ẋᵀCẋ ≥ 0. Integrated since the last reset: energy given + work by the force = stored + dissipated. The simulator integrates both terms exactly over every step, so the In and Out columns match to rounding error. "Energy given" counts the jumps when a mode is released or a parameter is edited.

Friction. A mass given friction Ff (under System parameters) feels a force of that size against its velocity while it slides, and none of its energy comes back: friction takes out Ff|ẋi|, Ff times the distance slid. A stuck mass does not move, so its friction does no work. The balance becomes energy given + work = stored + dissipated by the dampers + by friction. (See Friction below for what it does to the motion.)

By mode. Because ΦᵀMΦ = I and ΦᵀKΦ = diag(ωr²), the stored energy splits exactly into one term per undamped mode, T + V = Σ½(q̇r² + ωr²qr²), whatever the damping. Each mode's energy changes at the rate

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\tfrac12\left(\dot q_r^2 + \omega_r^2 q_r^2\right) = \dot q_r\,\varphi_r^{\mathsf T} f - \dot q_r \sum_s C_{m,rs}\,\dot q_s

With proportional damping only the s = r term is left, so each mode loses its own energy and nothing else. The off-diagonal terms of Cm pass energy from one mode to another. The complex modes do not split the energy this way: they are not orthogonal with respect to M and K, so their energies have cross terms. The panel always uses the classical modes.

Sweeps and ground motion

A chirp is a sine whose frequency sweeps from a start to an end frequency. Each mode swells as the sweep passes its natural frequency, so a slow sweep traces the frequency response out in time. A sweep that is fast compared with a mode's decay (its time constant 1/ζrωr) leaves it no time to build up: the peak comes late and low, and the mode rings on after the sweep has moved on. A log sweep spends equal time in every octave.

With ground motion the wall moves as xg(t) instead of a force pushing a mass. Element 1 then stretches by x1 − xg, so the ground reaches the chain only through k1 and c1:

M\ddot x + C\dot x + Kx = (k_1 x_g + c_1\dot x_g)\,e_1

(e1 = [1, 0, …, 0]: on mass 1 alone). For a harmonic xg the transmissibility Xi/Xg is the receptance's first column times k1 + iΩc1. It is 1 at low frequency (the chain moves with the ground), peaks at every mode, and falls away above the highest one: isolation. For a single mass on k and c, |X/Xg| is below 1 only above √2 fn, whatever the damping (Vibration isolation). In the chain each mass has its own crossover, and some fall below 1 well before the highest mode (in the default chain, m3 above 1.65 Hz). The damper c1 passes on the ground's velocity, so at high frequency x1 falls only as 1/Ω rather than 1/Ω². The work the ground does is the tension N1 in element 1 times the ground's velocity, with a minus sign, −∫N1ẋg dt. While the ground moves, the modal coordinates and the energy by mode use the motion relative to it.

Which modes the ground drives. In those relative coordinates, u = x − xg1 (1 a vector of ones: every mass moving with the ground), the equations become

M\ddot u + C\dot u + Ku = -M1\,\ddot x_g

The ground's acceleration pushes every mass in proportion to its own mass. Mode r is driven by Γr = φrᵀM1, its participation factor, and Γr² is its effective mass: the mass that swings on that mode's spring when the base shakes. The effective masses of all the modes add up to the total mass. The Random vibration and Shock spectrum pages, later, use Γ the same way, and the Modal coupling page explains where it comes from.

Vibration absorbers and tuned mass dampers

A machine or structure that resonates near its operating frequency can be fixed by hanging a small mass ma on it with a spring ka (and perhaps a damper ca), an idea Frahm patented in 1911 frahm. In this app that is one more mass on the end of the chain (System parameters → Preset).

Undamped absorber. For a primary mass m1 on k1 driven by F sin Ωt, the primary's receptance is

\frac{X_1}{F} = \frac{k_a - \Omega^2 m_a}{(k_1 + k_a - \Omega^2 m_1)(k_a - \Omega^2 m_a) - k_a^2}

The numerator vanishes at ωa = √(ka/ma): an antiresonance. At that frequency m1 stands still and the absorber moves just enough that its spring pushes back with −F. Tuned to the primary's own frequency it cancels the resonance, but the one peak becomes two, one either side, and they are further apart the heavier the absorber (mass ratio μ = ma/m1). It only works at one frequency: a machine whose speed drifts, or starts up through the peaks, needs damping.

Tuned mass damper. Add a damper ca and the peaks come down, but too much locks the absorber to m1 and the old resonance comes back. If the primary itself is undamped, every curve of |X1/F|, whatever ca, passes through the same two fixed points ormondroyd. Den Hartog's optimum tunes the absorber so the fixed points are equally high, then picks the damping that puts the peaks on them den-hartog (strictly, Brock's average of the two values of ζa² that each put one peak on its fixed point brock):

\frac{\omega_a}{\omega_1} = \frac{1}{1 + \mu}, \qquad \zeta_a = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1 + \mu)^3}}

and then both peaks are

\frac{|X_1|\,k_1}{F} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2}{\mu}}

Here ζa = ca/2maω1, measured against the primary's frequency ω1 = √(k1/m1), as in Den Hartog's derivation. (Some textbooks use the absorber's own ωa instead; the two differ by the factor 1 + μ, so check which one a formula assumes.) With μ = 5 % and an undamped primary both peaks are √41 = 6.4 times the static deflection. Damping in the primary lowers them further, and the fixed points are then only approximate.

On a chain, such as a building, a damper on the roof is tuned to one mode. Near that mode the structure behaves like a single mass on a spring whose modal mass at the roof is 1/φroof² (φ mass-normalized), so μ and the tuning use that mass and the mode's frequency. The other modes are hardly changed.

Tick Compare with the chain without its last mass on the Frequency response tab to see the structure before the absorber was added, dashed.

Friction

A mass given friction Ff (under System parameters) feels a force of that size against its velocity while it slides (the Damping models page's Coulomb friction). It does not grow with the motion, so the chain is no longer linear: double the force and the response does not double.

  • A straight-line decay. Released from rest, a single mass still swings at ωn, but each half-swing it works against the same force, so it loses the same amplitude, 2Ff/k, every half cycle: 4Ff/k a cycle, not a fixed fraction. Small vibrations die out sooner, relative to their size, than large ones. It stops for good at the first turn within Fs/k of rest, wherever the spring can no longer pull it free, not at the rest position.
  • Stick-slip. Static friction Fs (the Static / sliding ratio times Ff) can hold more than sliding friction resists: a stuck mass waits until the pull exceeds Fs, then lurches off against the smaller Ff. That is a squeaking hinge or a bowed string.
  • Equivalent damping. Over a cycle of amplitude X at frequency Ω friction takes out 4FfX, and a viscous damper c takes out πcΩX². Equal losses give ceq = 4Ff/πΩX: a damper that weakens as the motion grows. The modal table's ζ friction column is each mode's ζ from these dampers, at the Initial displacement a release would start from.
  • The describing function. Under a steady sine the same idea gives a frequency response: assume each mass moves as a sine, keep only the part of the friction force at the drive's frequency (a square wave's fundamental, 4Ff/π), and solve the linear chain that results, again and again until the amplitudes agree den-hartog-1931. This is harmonic balance (the Duffing & Mathieu page, much later, uses it on a nonlinear spring). The Frequency response tab draws it dash-dot at the Excitation panel's amplitude; where friction holds a mass still, the curve has a gap.

The simulation itself includes friction exactly, stepping the chain between the moments a mass sticks or breaks free. The modes and the solid frequency responses leave it out.

Non-proportional damping and "coupling"

Otherwise Cm has off-diagonal terms: damping forces produced by motion in mode r push on mode s, so the modal equations are coupled. The coupling index in the note under the modal table is the largest off-diagonal term relative to the diagonal,

\max_{r \ne s} \frac{|C_{m,rs}|}{\sqrt{C_{m,rr}\,C_{m,ss}}}

It is 0 for proportional damping and approaches 1 when cross-coupling is as strong as each mode's own damping. It cannot exceed 1: dampers only dissipate energy, so Cm is positive semidefinite, and then |Cm,rs| ≤ √(Cm,rrCm,ss) (the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality).

The ζ modal column keeps only the diagonal of Cm and ignores the rest. That is the standard "classical damping" approximation used throughout practice. It works well when coupling is modest or the modes are well separated in frequency, and degrades as either assumption fails. For example, with the default chain and only c1 changed:

Exact damped modes are complex

The table's ζ exact and fd columns come from the damped problem solved exactly, in first-order (state-space) form with z = [x, ẋ]:

\dot z = A z, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ -M^{-1}K & -M^{-1}C \end{bmatrix}

Its eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, λ = −ζωn ± iωd. With proportional damping the eigenvectors are the real shapes φ. Otherwise they are complex: each mass gets an amplitude and a phase, peaks at its own time, and the nodes drift, so the motion looks partly like a wave travelling along the chain. This is physics, not an artifact of the solver. Method → State-space on the Modal analysis tab shows these modes; the Complex modes page, later, explains them (why there are 2N eigenvalues, what a real or overdamped root is, how to release one) and what the classical approximation misses.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on four 1 kg masses joined by 400 N/m springs and 2 N·s/m dampers, the first to the wall.

  1. The matrices. M = I kg, and K has 800 down its diagonal but 400 in the last place, −400 beside it; C = K/200, every damper the same 0.005 s times its spring.
  2. The frequencies. With N = 4 the phase steps are γr = (2r − 1)π/9: 20°, 60°, 100° and 140°. 2√(k/m) = 40 rad/s, so ωr = 40 sin 10°, 40 sin 30°, 40 sin 50° and 40 sin 70° = 6.946, 20.00, 30.64 and 37.59 rad/s: 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz. Mode 2 is exactly a single mass's 20 rad/s.
  3. Mode 1's shape. φ1,i = (2/3) sin(20° × i) = 0.228, 0.429, 0.577 and 0.657: every mass the same way, the far end most.
  4. The damping. C = βK, so ζr = βωr/2 = 0.0025ωr = 0.0174, 0.0500, 0.0766 and 0.0940, exact; the damped frequencies, fr√(1 − ζr²), are 1.105, 3.179, 4.862 and 5.956 Hz.
  5. A guess. The straight line v = [1, 2, 3, 4] stretches every spring by 1, so vᵀKv = 4 × 400 = 1600, and vᵀMv = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 30: R = 53.33, 7.303 rad/s, 1.162 Hz. That is 5 % above 1.105, though R is 10.5 % above ω1² = 48.25: the square root halves the error.
  6. The ground. Γ1 = 0.228 + 0.429 + 0.577 + 0.657 = 1.890 √kg, so mode 1's effective mass is 3.57 of the 4 kg (89 %); modes 2, 3 and 4 carry 0.33, 0.08 and 0.01 kg, which is why a shaken base mostly excites mode 1.

These are the modal table's f, ζ modal, ζ exact and fd columns, and mode 1's row in the mode-shape plot.

Things to try

Pick these under Things to try on the left: each sets the chain and the controls up and says what to look for.

  • One mass on a spring and damper has a natural frequency √(k/m), a damping ratio ζ, and a resonance 1/2ζ times its static deflection, 2ζfn wide.
  • A chain of N masses has N modes. With proportional damping each mode is one of those oscillators, in its own coordinate qr, and the motion is their sum.
  • The undamped shapes are orthogonal through M and K, so the energy splits mode by mode. Non-proportional damping couples them, and the exact damped modes become complex.
  • A guessed shape gives a frequency through the Rayleigh quotient, always from above.
  • A shaken base drives each mode through its effective mass; an absorber tuned to a mode splits it in two; friction is a damper that weakens as the motion grows.
