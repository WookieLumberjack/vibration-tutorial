Where the energy goes

The viscous damper, a force proportional to velocity, is a model chosen because it makes the equations linear and easy. Real structures lose energy in other ways: internal friction in the material, rubbing in joints and bolted connections, the shearing of rubber and polymers, the air around them. Each has its own force law, and they differ in how the loss depends on the amplitude and on the frequency. This page puts four of them on the same mass and spring, matched so that at fn they all take the same energy per cycle as a viscous damper of ratio ζ.

The hysteresis loop

Cycle the oscillator's spring and damper through x = X sin Ωt and plot the force they carry against x. A spring alone traces the line kx out and back. Anything that loses energy traces a loop, because the force is larger going one way than coming back, and the area inside it is the energy lost per cycle:

W_d = \oint F\,\mathrm{d}x

A material test machine draws exactly this: the loop's tilt is the stiffness, its width the damping. The peak energy the spring stores in the cycle is U = ½kX², and the ratio of the two is what the rest of the page compares.

Viscous damping

F = cẋ. Over a harmonic cycle the damping force is cΩX cos Ωt, a quarter cycle ahead of x, so the loop is an ellipse and

W_d = \pi c\,\Omega X^2

The loss grows in proportion to the frequency. Written as a complex stiffness, the force is K*X with

K^* = K' + iK'' = k + i\Omega c

The real part K′ is the storage stiffness, in phase with the motion; the imaginary part K″ the loss stiffness, a quarter cycle ahead. For any of the models here the loss per cycle is Wd = πK″X².

Hysteretic (structural) damping

Tests on most metals, and on built-up structures, find a loss per cycle that hardly depends on frequency. The model for it is a complex stiffness that doesn't depend on frequency either:

K^* = k(1 + i\eta), \qquad W_d = \pi\eta k X^2

η is the loss factor. At resonance it gives the same peak as a viscous damper with η = 2ζ, so the two are often used interchangeably (as the matching here does), but away from resonance they differ: the hysteretic loss is the same at every frequency. Its frequency response,

H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\eta k}

peaks exactly at fn, at 1/ηk; the viscous one peaks a little lower, at fn√(1 − 2ζ²), and a little higher, 1/(2ζk√(1 − ζ²)). It works for steady harmonic motion (and in frequency-domain analysis, where it is standard: the Rigid body on mounts page, later, puts it in its mounts), but it has no honest time-domain form: a constant K″ at every frequency, negative ones included, would make the mass respond before it is struck (crandall). The free decay draws its viscous equivalent instead.

Coulomb friction

A dry contact pushes back with a force of fixed size against the sliding, Ff sgn ẋ, the law Coulomb measured (coulomb): the mass below slides on its floor, and the floor pushes it back against its velocity, whatever its speed.

The loop is a rectangle (with the spring, a parallelogram) and

W_d = 4F_f X

proportional to X, not X². Matching it to a viscous damper gives the equivalent viscous damping

c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{4F_f}{\pi\Omega X}

so friction is heavy damping for small motions and light for large ones. Released, each half cycle is a swing about the point Ff/k off centre on the side friction pushes to, so the swing decays in a straight line, losing 4Ff/k of amplitude a cycle, and stops dead at the first turn within Ff/k of the centre, where the spring can no longer beat the friction (later, the Simulation page's friction steps it exactly). Under a harmonic force F it can't limit a resonance at all once F > 4Ff/π: the force then puts in πFX per cycle at resonance, more than the 4FfX friction takes out, at every amplitude. Den Hartog's exact solution of the sliding motion has the same threshold (den-hartog-1931).

Viscoelastic materials: the Zener element

Rubber, polymers and damping layers store and lose energy in a way that depends strongly on frequency. The simplest model that captures it is the standard linear solid (zener): a spring k0 in parallel with a spring k1 and a dashpot c1 in series, the series pair being a Maxwell arm (maxwell).

With the relaxation time τ = c1/k1,

K^*(\Omega) = k_0 + k_1\,\frac{i\Omega\tau}{1 + i\Omega\tau}
  • Slowly (Ωτ ≪ 1) the dashpot gives way and only k0 carries load: the relaxed stiffness.
  • Fast (Ωτ ≫ 1) the dashpot locks: k0 + k1, the unrelaxed stiffness.
  • In between the stiffness climbs and the loss factor peaks, at Ωτ = 1/√(1 + k1/k0), where it is
\eta_{\max} = \frac{k_1}{2\sqrt{k_0(k_0 + k_1)}}

Far below its peak the Zener element acts like a viscous damper (η ∝ Ω), far above it like a stiffer spring with little loss. Real materials have many such arms with a spread of τ, and temperature shifts them all; designers put a damping material's loss peak where the structure's modes are (nashif).

The loss factor, compared

For any of them the loss factor

\eta = \frac{K''}{K'} = \frac{W_d}{2\pi U}, \qquad U = \tfrac{1}{2}K'X^2

measures damping without saying where it comes from; at resonance η = 2ζ = 1/Q.

  • Viscous: η = cΩ/k, rising with frequency.
  • Hysteretic: constant.
  • Friction: 4Ff/πkX, constant in frequency but falling with amplitude.
  • Zener: a peak.

Equivalent viscous damping

Whatever the force law, a loss of Wd a cycle at amplitude X and frequency Ω takes as much as the viscous damper

c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{W_d}{\pi\Omega X^2}

which is how a nonlinear damper is put into a linear analysis, one amplitude at a time: the friction's ceq above is this with Wd = 4FfX. A body moving fast through air or water feels a drag that grows as the velocity squared, F = aẋ|ẋ|; over a cycle it takes

W_d = \tfrac{8}{3}\,a\,\Omega^2 X^3, \qquad c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{8a\Omega X}{3\pi}

the opposite of friction: light damping for small motions, heavy for large ones (thomson). Each law's ceq holds only at the amplitude it was worked out for; the matching on this page holds friction's at X.

Measuring the loss factor

Three measurements give the same number for light damping, whatever the mechanism:

  • The loop: its area over 2π times the peak stored energy, η = Wd/2πU.
  • The decay: the logarithmic decrement is δ ≈ 2πζ, so η ≈ δ/π (friction's decay, a straight line, has no single δ: that itself says the loss isn't viscous).
  • The bandwidth: the half-power points of the response are 2ζfn apart, so η ≈ (f2 − f1)/fn = 1/Q.

A vibration losing Wd a cycle, Ω/2π cycles a second, loses on average the power P = ΩηU: its loss factor times its frequency times its energy. The Vibro-acoustics & SEA page, later, balances whole structures' power this way.

How this page computes it

The loops, the energies and the loss factors come from each model's complex stiffness (friction's true force for its loop, its describing function elsewhere). The frequency response is 1/(K*(Ω) − Ω²m), and friction's √(F² − (4Ff/π)²)/|k − Ω²m|. The free decays are exact: the viscous one in closed form, friction's half cycle by half cycle, the Zener element's (three states: the position, the velocity and the dashpot's end) by the matrix exponential.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on the Single oscillator's m = 1 kg and k = 400 N/m, with every model matched to ζ = 0.05 and cycled at fn through X = 10 mm.

  1. The oscillator. ωn = √(k/m) = 20 rad/s, fn = 3.18 Hz, and the viscous damper is c = 2ζ√(km) = 2 N·s/m. The peak stored energy is U = ½kX² = ½ × 400 × 0.01² = 20 mJ.
  2. The loss to match. η = 2ζ = 0.1, so each model takes Wd = 2πηU = 12.57 mJ a cycle at fn; the viscous damper's πcωnX² = π × 2 × 20 × 0.01² is the same.
  3. Friction. 4FfX = 12.57 mJ gives Ff = 0.314 N. Released from 10 mm it loses 4Ff/k = 3.14 mm a cycle, 1.57 mm a half cycle: its turns are at 8.43, 6.86, 5.29, 3.72, 2.15 and 0.58 mm, the last inside Ff/k = 0.785 mm, so it sticks after three cycles, 0.58 mm off centre (575 µm, carried exactly, as the readout under the decay gives it). The viscous swing instead keeps e−δ = 0.730 of itself a cycle, with δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²) = 0.315.
  4. Driven by 1 N. The hysteretic peak is (F/k)/η = 25.0 mm, the viscous one 25.03 mm. Friction's threshold is 4Ff/π = 0.4 N, so 1 N runs it away.
  5. The Zener element. With its loss peak at fn it must peak at η = 0.1, so k1/k0 = 0.221 (from ηmax above). Its storage stiffness at fn must be k, which sets k0 = 363.8 N/m and k1 = 80.4 N/m, and Ωτ = 1/√1.221 at 20 rad/s gives τ = 45.2 ms.

These are the numbers the readouts give: under the oscillator's controls (Ff = 0.3142 N, τ = 0.04525 s), under the loops (12.6 mJ each) and under the decay.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator, the matching and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • The area of the force–displacement loop is the energy lost per cycle; the loss factor η = K″/K′ = Wd/2πU measures it, and at resonance η = 2ζ.
  • Viscous damping loses energy in proportion to frequency, hysteretic damping not at all; they agree at resonance, which is why η = 2ζ swaps between them.
  • Hysteretic damping is a frequency-domain model: it has no causal free decay.
  • Friction loses 4FfX per cycle: it decays in a straight line, stops off centre, and can't bound a resonance driven harder than 4Ff/π.
  • A viscoelastic material stiffens across its relaxation frequency, where its loss peaks: viscous below, elastic above.
  • Any loss can be stood in for by ceq = Wd/πΩX², at one amplitude; the loop, the decay and the bandwidth all measure η.
