Where the energy goes

The viscous damper, a force proportional to velocity, is a model chosen because it makes the equations linear and easy. Real structures lose energy in other ways: internal friction in the material, rubbing in joints and bolted connections, the shearing of rubber and polymers, the air around them. Each has its own force law, and they differ in how the loss depends on the amplitude and on the frequency. This page puts four of them on the same mass and spring, matched so that at fn they all take the same energy per cycle as a viscous damper of ratio ζ.

The hysteresis loop

Cycle the oscillator's spring and damper through x = X sin Ωt and plot the force they carry against x. A spring alone traces the line kx out and back. Anything that loses energy traces a loop, because the force is larger going one way than coming back, and the area inside it is the energy lost per cycle:

Wd = ∮ F dx.

A material test machine draws exactly this: the loop's tilt is the stiffness, its width the damping.

Viscous damping

F = cẋ. Over a harmonic cycle the damping force is cΩX cos Ωt, a quarter cycle ahead of x, so the loop is an ellipse and

Wd = πcΩX².

The loss grows in proportion to the frequency. Written as a complex stiffness, F = K*X with K* = k + iΩc: the real part is the storage stiffness K', the imaginary part the loss stiffness K''.

Hysteretic (structural) damping

Tests on most metals, and on built-up structures, find a loss per cycle that hardly depends on frequency. The model for it is a complex stiffness that doesn't depend on frequency either:

K* = k(1 + iη),   Wd = πηkX².

η is the loss factor. At resonance it gives the same peak as a viscous damper with η = 2ζ, so the two are often used interchangeably (as the matching here does), but away from resonance they differ: the hysteretic loss is the same at every frequency. It works for steady harmonic motion (and in frequency-domain analysis, where it is standard), but it has no honest time-domain form: a constant K'' at every frequency, negative ones included, would make the mass respond before it is struck. The free decay draws its viscous equivalent instead.

Coulomb friction

A dry contact pushes back with a force of fixed size against the sliding, Ff sign(ẋ). The loop is a rectangle (with the spring, a parallelogram) and

Wd = 4FfX,

proportional to X, not X². Matching it to a viscous damper gives the equivalent viscous damping ceq = 4Ff/πΩX: friction is heavy damping for small motions and light for large ones. So it decays in a straight line, losing 4Ff/k of amplitude a cycle, and stops dead within Ff/k of the centre (later, the Simulation page's friction steps it exactly). Under a harmonic force F it can't limit a resonance at all once F > 4Ff/π: the force then puts in πFX per cycle at resonance, more than the 4FfX friction takes out, at every amplitude.

Viscoelastic materials: the Zener element

Rubber, polymers and damping layers store and lose energy in a way that depends strongly on frequency. The simplest model that captures it is the standard linear solid (Zener): a spring k0 in parallel with a spring k1 and a dashpot c1 in series (a Maxwell arm). With the relaxation time τ = c1/k1,

K*(Ω) = k0 + k1 iΩτ/(1 + iΩτ).

  • Slowly (Ωτ ≪ 1) the dashpot gives way and only k0 carries load: the relaxed stiffness.
  • Fast (Ωτ ≫ 1) the dashpot locks: k0 + k1, the unrelaxed stiffness.
  • In between the stiffness climbs and the loss factor peaks, at Ωτ = 1/√(1 + k1/k0).

Far below its peak the Zener element acts like a viscous damper (η ∝ Ω), far above it like a stiffer spring with little loss. Real materials have many such arms with a spread of τ, and temperature shifts them all; designers put a damping material's loss peak where the structure's modes are.

The loss factor, compared

For any of them the loss factor η = K''/K' = Wd/(2πU), U = ½K'X² the peak stored energy, measures damping without saying where it comes from; at resonance η = 2ζ = 1/Q. Viscous: η = cΩ/k, rising with frequency. Hysteretic: constant. Friction: 4Ff/πkX, constant in frequency but falling with amplitude. Zener: a peak.

How this page computes it

The loops, the energies and the loss factors come from each model's complex stiffness (friction's true force for its loop, its describing function elsewhere). The frequency response is 1/(K*(Ω) − Ω²m), and friction's √(F² − (4Ff/π)²)/|k − Ω²m|. The free decays are exact: the viscous one in closed form, friction's half cycle by half cycle, the Zener element's (three states: the position, the velocity and the dashpot's end) by the matrix exponential.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator, the matching and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • The area of the force–displacement loop is the energy lost per cycle; the loss factor η = K''/K' measures it, and at resonance η = 2ζ.
  • Viscous damping loses energy in proportion to frequency, hysteretic damping not at all; they agree at resonance, which is why η = 2ζ swaps between them.
  • Hysteretic damping is a frequency-domain model: it has no causal free decay.
  • Friction loses 4FfX per cycle: it decays in a straight line, stops off centre, and can't bound a resonance driven harder than 4Ff/π.
  • A viscoelastic material stiffens across its relaxation frequency, where its loss peaks: viscous below, elastic above.
