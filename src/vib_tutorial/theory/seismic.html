The shock response spectrum The chain's modes on a pulse

A design spectrum

The Shock spectrum page's idea, used for earthquakes, where it began biot: a single oscillator's peak response to the ground's motion, plotted against its period T. Earthquake codes plot the pseudo-acceleration Sa = ω2|z|max, which times a mass is the oscillator's peak spring force. An earthquake's spectrum is jagged and no two are alike, so a code smooths and envelopes many into a design spectrum. The page uses the shape of the European code's elastic spectrum (Eurocode 8, its type 1 en1998-1), for a peak ground acceleration ag on rock:

S_a = a_g S \left[1 + \frac{T}{T_B}(2.5\eta - 1)\right] \quad (T < T_B) S_a = 2.5\,\eta\,a_g S \quad (T_B \le T \le T_C) S_a = 2.5\,\eta\,a_g S\,\frac{T_C}{T} \quad (T_C \le T \le T_D) S_a = 2.5\,\eta\,a_g S\,\frac{T_C T_D}{T^2} \quad (T > T_D)

A rise from the ground's own acceleration at T = 0 (a rigid building moves with the ground), a plateau, then falls at constant pseudo-velocity and at constant displacement. The ground type sets the soil factor S and the corners TB, TC, TD: soft soil amplifies and widens the plateau. Damping scales it by

\eta = \sqrt{\frac{10}{5 + \zeta\%}} \ \ge 0.55

1 at the 5 % the spectrum is drawn for.

The chain as a building

The page reads the Simulation page's chain as a shear building: each mass a floor m, each spring a storey's sideways stiffness k (its columns bending with the floors kept level), the first on the ground.

Measured from the moving ground, the floors' displacements u obey

M\ddot{u} + C\dot{u} + Ku = -M\mathbf{1}\,a_g(t)

each floor pushed by its own inertia. Two things a designer reads off them: a storey's drift Δj = uj − uj−1, how far its top moves against its bottom, which bends its columns and cracks its walls; and its storey shear Vj = kΔj, the sideways force it carries for every floor above it, the largest at the bottom, the base shear Vb.

Participation and effective mass

In the modes each is a single oscillator driven by Γrag, with the participation factor

\Gamma_r = \frac{\varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} M \mathbf{1}}{\varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} M \varphi_r}

Its peak modal coordinate is ΓrSa(Tr)/ωr2, its floors' displacements φr times that, and its base shear Meff,rSa(Tr), with

M_{\text{eff},r} = \frac{(\varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} M \mathbf{1})^2}{\varphi_r^{\mathsf{T}} M \varphi_r}

the effective modal mass. The effective masses add up to the building's mass, so their running sum says how many modes are enough: codes ask for 90 %.

Combining the modes

The spectrum gives each mode's peak Rr, not when it happens. Adding them (ABS) assumes they all peak together: safe, usually far too safe. SRSS, √(ΣRr2), assumes they are independent, which holds when their frequencies are well apart. NRL (the largest plus the SRSS of the rest ohara-belsheim) sits between. When two modes are close they move together much of the time, and SRSS underestimates; the complete quadratic combination (CQC)

R = \sqrt{\sum_i \sum_j R_i\,\rho_{ij}\,R_j}

keeps the cross terms, with Der Kiureghian's correlation der-kiureghian for equal damping and β = ωj/ωi:

\rho_{ij} = \frac{8\zeta^2(1 + \beta)\beta^{3/2}}{(1 - \beta^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2\beta(1 + \beta)^2}

1 on the diagonal, falling off as the frequencies part, faster the less the damping. CQC is SRSS when the modes are far apart, so codes now use it throughout.

Checking against records

The page synthesizes seven ground motions whose spectra match the design spectrum (sines at random phases under a rise, strong-shaking and decay envelope, each scaled until its spectrum meets the target), steps the building on each mode by mode, exactly, and takes the mean of their peaks, as a code does with a set of records. The rules are estimates of that mean; each record scatters about it by some 10 %.

Storey drift

The base shear sizes the structure; the drift decides the damage. Partitions, glazing and cladding fixed to the frame are racked by each storey's drift, so a code limits it as a fraction of the storey's height h, under a smaller and more frequent earthquake than the one the structure is designed to survive. Eurocode 8 takes that as the design one scaled by ν (0.5 for ordinary buildings) and asks for νΔ ≤ 0.005h with brittle partitions fixed to the frame, 0.0075h with ductile ones and 0.010h where they are fixed so they don't feel it. A soft storey, much less stiff than those above it (the open ground floor of a car park or shop), takes most of the drift (Things to try, 7).

Ductility and the behaviour factor

The elastic spectrum's forces are large: this page's five storeys would need a base shear of nearly half their weight. Buildings are not designed to stay elastic in the design earthquake but to yield in a controlled way, in chosen places (the beams' ends, braces), and keep their strength while they do. An elastic–plastic oscillator of yield force Fy and yield displacement uy that reaches umax has a ductility

\mu = \frac{u_{\max}}{u_y}

How much weaker can it be than the elastic one, q = Fel/Fy, for a given μ? Veletsos and Newmark's study of oscillators on records veletsos-newmark found that a flexible oscillator, of period past the plateau, reaches about the same peak displacement either way (the equal-displacement rule), so q ≈ μ; a stiffer one, on the plateau, takes about the same energy, the areas under the two curves equal, which gives

q \approx \sqrt{2\mu - 1}

and a rigid one, at T = 0, moves with the ground whatever its strength: q = 1. Plotting the strength an oscillator needs for a given μ against its period draws an inelastic spectrum: the elastic one divided by q(μ, T). A code does it the other way round: it gives each kind of structure a behaviour factor q for the ductility its detailing can be relied on for (about 1.5 for a frame that isn't detailed for it, up to 4 to 6 for a well-detailed steel or concrete frame), divides the elastic spectrum by it (with a floor of 0.2ag) to get the forces to design for, and multiplies the displacements those forces give by q again to get the real ones: by the equal-displacement rule, the elastic displacements.

Base isolation

The spectrum also says how to need less strength: move the building's period off the plateau. Base isolation puts it on a layer of soft bearings (laminated rubber, often with a lead core to damp it, or sliding pendulums), an isolator stiffness kb so much softer than the storeys that the first mode, of period Tb ≈ 2π√(Σm/kb) for the whole mass Σm above, is the building moving rigidly on the bearings naeim-kelly. At 2 to 3 s it sits on the spectrum's falls, where Sa is a fraction of the plateau, and it carries almost all the mass, so the higher modes, which would bend the building, are barely driven. The price is the bearings' displacement, a few hundred millimetres, which the services and the gap round the building must allow, and which the bearings' damping (10 to 30 %) is there to keep down. It works on firm ground; on soft soil, whose spectrum stays high to long periods, it gains less.

Worked example

The page opens on five 10 t floors on 5 MN/m storeys, under ag = 0.3 g on ground type C (S = 1.15, TB = 0.2 s, TC = 0.6 s, TD = 2 s) at 5 % (η = 1): a plateau of 2.5 × 0.3 × 1.15 = 0.8625 g (the readout's 0.863 g). A uniform shear building's first mode is

\omega_1 = 2\sqrt{k/m}\,\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2N + 1)} = 2\sqrt{500} \times \sin\frac{\pi}{22} = 6.36\ \text{rad/s}

so T1 = 0.987 s, past TC: Sa = 0.8625 × 0.6/0.987 = 0.524 g. Mode 1's effective mass is 88.0 % of the 50 t, so its base shear is 44.0 t × 0.524 g = 226 kN. Mode 2 (T2 = 0.338 s, on the plateau, 8.7 % of the mass) gives 37 kN of its own, and the five by CQC give 230 kN, against 235 kN averaged over the seven records; ABS gives 277 kN. The roof moves 159 mm (mode 1's Sa/ω12 = 127 mm times Γ1φ = 1.25 at the roof), and the first storey drifts 46 mm.

On 3 m storeys (the model has no heights) that drift is 1.5 % of the storey; halved by ν, 0.77 %: within the limit for partitions fixed so they don't feel it, not for brittle ones. Designed as a frame with q = 3, the building needs a base shear of 230/3 = 77 kN, and since T1 is past TC its floors still move about as far: its storeys yield, to a ductility of about 3.

Isolated (Things to try, 8) on a 10 t slab on bearings of 300 kN/m at 15 %: Tb = 2.94 s and η = 0.71, so the shear in the first storey above the bearings falls from 230 to 42 kN and its drift from 46 to 8 mm, while the bearings move 166 mm.

Things to try

Each of these loads a building into the Simulation page and sets up the spectrum (Things to try, top left).

  • A design spectrum smooths many earthquakes' spectra into a plateau and 1/T, 1/T2 falls; soil and damping scale it.
  • Each mode is an oscillator driven by Γag; its effective mass is its share of the base shear, and the shares add up to the whole mass.
  • ABS adds the modes' peaks and overestimates; SRSS is right for well-separated modes.
  • Close modes are correlated: CQC keeps the cross terms and SRSS underestimates.
  • Drift decides the damage; ductility lets a building be designed for the elastic forces over q, and isolation moves it off the plateau.
