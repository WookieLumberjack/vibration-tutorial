Proportional damping, again

The undamped modes φr diagonalize the mass and stiffness matrices: in modal coordinates the chain is N separate oscillators. Damping keeps them separate only if ΦTCΦ is diagonal too: proportional (or classical) damping, such as C = βK, a damper per spring in proportion to it. Then each mode keeps its real shape, its damping ratio is ζr = φrTCφr/2ωr (φ mass-normalized), and its pole is −ζrωr ± iωr√(1 − ζr²).

Complex modes

A damper on its own (a shock absorber, a joint, a tuned mass damper) is not proportional: ΦTCΦ has off-diagonal terms crs that couple the modes. The exact modes are then the eigenvectors of the 2N × 2N state matrix, and their shapes ψ are complex: each mass has its own phase and peaks at its own time, the nodes move, and the motion is partly a wave travelling along the chain. To first order in the damping,

ψr ≈ φr + Σs≠r iωrcsr / (ωr² − ωs²) φs:

the imaginary part grows with the coupling and as the other modes come close. Close modes, as a tuned mass damper makes, go complex first; a mode far from the others stays nearly real even under a heavy coupling.

The state-space form: 2N eigenvalues

The exact modes come from the equations written in first order, z = [x, ẋ]: ż = Az with A = [ 0  I ; −M−1K  −M−1C ]. That doubles the size of the eigenproblem, so there are 2N eigenvalues and 2N eigenvectors. Method → State-space on the Simulation page's Modal analysis tab lists them.

  • Conjugate pairs. A is real, so complex eigenvalues come in pairs λ = σ + iωd and λ* = σ − iωd, with conjugate shapes ψ and ψ*. Neither one alone is a real motion; together they are: x(t) = ψeλt + ψ*eλ*t = 2 Re(ψ eλt). In the Simulation page's complex-plane plot the arrows of λ turn anticlockwise and those of λ* clockwise; their real parts, the displacements, are identical. Each oscillatory mode of the classical table corresponds to one pair.
  • Real eigenvalues. A heavily damped (overdamped) mode gives two real eigenvalues instead of a pair: pure exponential decays with time constant τ = −1/λ and no oscillation. A chain with no spring to the ground (k1 = 0) has λ = 0: rigid-body motion, a position it keeps. With the damper c1 still there, a second real root goes with it, close to −c1/(m1 + … + mN) (−0.50 s−1 for the default chain): a push slides the whole chain to a new position as its velocity dies away through c1. With c1 = 0 too, λ = 0 is a repeated root with only one eigenvector (defective): the chain can drift at constant velocity, which no pair of exponentials describes.
  • The eigenvector includes velocities. The state eigenvector is [ψ ; λψ]. To start the chain in exactly one mode you must set the displacements and the velocities: x(0) = Re(ψ), ẋ(0) = Re(λψ). That is what Release selected mode does in this method. It is why a complex mode can be released cleanly with non-proportional damping, while the classical release (real shape, at rest) excites other modes too.
  • Orthogonality. A is not symmetric, so the ψ are not orthogonal in the usual sense. Decoupling the equations needs the left eigenvectors of A as well (or the symmetric "Duncan" form of the state equations). This is why the classical method, with its simple ΦTMΦ = I, is preferred whenever damping is light or nearly proportional.

Is this an artifact of the solver? No, it is physics. Both calculations are exact for the problem they solve: the undamped modes from the symmetric eigenproblem Kφ = ω²Mφ (real by construction), the damped ones from the general eigenproblem of A (complex when the damping is non-proportional). When the damping is proportional the two agree to machine precision. The only approximation is the classical ζ, which describes the damped chain with the undamped shapes; the time simulation always uses the full M, C and K.

Modal coordinates in state space. With the eigenvectors [ψ; λψ] as the columns of Y, the state's modal coordinates are η = Y−1z. They need velocities as well as displacements. The coordinates of λ and λ* are conjugates, so each pair is one curve, 2 Re(η), in the Simulation page's modal plot. They are independent for any damping: each is a pure decaying oscillation (or decay) unless a force drives it. (The energy panel still splits the energy with the classical modes: the complex ones are not orthogonal through M and K, so their energies have cross terms.)

How complex

  • MPC, the modal phase collinearity:

MPC = [(sxx − syy)² + 4sxy²] / (sxx + syy)²,

where sxx = Σ(Re ψj)², syy = Σ(Im ψj)² and sxy = Σ Re ψj Im ψj. It is 1 when the entries lie on a line through the origin, 0 when they spread evenly round it.

  • MPD, the mean phase deviation: the entries' angles from their best-fit line, weighted by their size. 0° for a real mode.
  • MAC(φ, ψ) = |φTψ|² / (φTφ ψHψ): how much of the exact shape the undamped one explains, 1 for the same shape.

Measured modes are always a little complex (noise, a slightly non-linear structure, a sensor out of phase), so a small MPD says little; a large one in an analysis says the damping couples the modes.

What ignoring it costs

The usual approximation keeps ΦTCΦ's diagonal and drops the rest. It is exact for proportional damping and good when the damping is light and the modes are well apart. It fails where a damper is strong enough to change the shapes: a damper that hardly moves in the exact mode (its two ends move together) is counted as if it did, and the approximation's ζ can be ten times too high. It cannot see a mode go overdamped, or a damper lock a joint. The FRF tab compares the receptances; the Free decay tab releases the chain in an undamped mode's shape: exactly that mode, if the damping is proportional; otherwise the damping hands some of the motion to the other modes, close ones most.

Sweeping a damper

The Sweep tab follows each mode's exact pole as one damper goes from almost nothing to very stiff, the rest of the chain as it is: the root locus. With a light damper every mode's damping grows in proportion to it, as the approximation says. But a damper only works while its ends move apart: as it stiffens it holds them together, and the chain's modes turn into those of the chain with that joint rigid, whose frequencies are different and whose damping falls back towards zero. So each mode's damping rises to a peak and falls again, and on the way one pair (the motion across the damper) leaves as two real, overdamped roots. There is a best damper for each mode, and it is not the stiffest. A damper at the base of the default chain, its other dampers taken out, gives mode 1 at most ζ = 0.147, at about 60 N·s/m; the approximation's ζ keeps growing for ever. The modes are most complex near those peaks, where the damper both works hard and changes the shapes. Sweeping a spring instead moves the modes, and how complex they get depends on how close they come.

Identification

This tab looks ahead to the Virtual modal test page, which explains how modes are fitted to a measurement; come back to it after that page. A modal test fits a model to measured FRFs. The Identification tab fits one column (a force on one mass, every response, with a little noise) on the exact poles two ways, by least squares (LSFD): with complex modes, residues of any phase, which fit any viscous damping; and with real modes, Ar/(ωr² − ω² + 2iζrωrω) with Ar real, the model of a proportionally damped structure. With proportional damping the two agree. Otherwise the real-mode fit can't take the modes' phases: it fits the FRF worse and gets the shapes wrong, and its shapes are not the undamped ones either. Noise makes even real modes come out a little complex from the complex-mode fit, which is why a small MPD in a measurement says little.

Things to try

Pick a chain under Things to try on the left (it loads into the Simulation page too):

  • Proportional damping keeps the undamped modes: real shapes, nodes that stand still, and ζ = φTCφ/2ω exactly.
  • A damper that isn't proportional couples the modes; the exact modes, from the 2N × 2N state matrix, are then complex, most of all where modes are close.
  • The usual approximation keeps only the diagonal of ΦTCΦ: good for light damping and separated modes, wrong (even ten times) where a damper changes the shapes.
  • A damper has a best size for each mode: past it, it locks its joint and damps less.
  • MPC and MPD measure how complex a shape is; a measured mode is always a little complex, so only a large value means much.
