Proportional damping, again

The undamped modes φr (mass-normalized) diagonalize the mass and stiffness matrices, so in modal coordinates x = Φq the chain is N separate oscillators. Damping keeps them separate only if the modal damping matrix Cm = ΦᵀCΦ is diagonal too. This is proportional (or classical) damping, and the condition for it is CM−1K = KM−1C caughey. A damper beside every spring, each the same multiple of it (ci = βki, so C = βK), meets it. Each mode then keeps its real shape, and

\zeta_r = \frac{C_{m,rr}}{2\omega_r}, \qquad \lambda_r = -\zeta_r\omega_r \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega_r\sqrt{1 - \zeta_r^2}

A damper on its own (a shock absorber, a joint, a tuned mass damper) does not meet it. A single damper c1 at the base gives Cm,rs = c1φ1rφ1s: every off-diagonal term is as large as it can be beside the diagonal, a coupling index of exactly 1.

Complex modes

With off-diagonal terms in Cm, a mode's damping force pushes on the other modes. The exact modes are then the eigenvectors of the 2N × 2N state matrix, and their shapes ψr are complex: each mass has its own phase and peaks at its own time, the nodes move, and the motion is partly a wave travelling along the chain. How complex they get follows from the modal equations,

\ddot q_s + \omega_s^2 q_s + \sum_k C_{m,sk}\,\dot q_k = 0

Look for mode r, qs = asr eλt with arr = 1. When the damping is light, λ ≈ iωr and the other asr are small, so in row s ≠ r only the terms in asr and arr count:

(\omega_s^2 - \omega_r^2)\,a_{sr} + \mathrm{i}\,\omega_r C_{m,sr} \approx 0 \psi_r \approx \varphi_r + \sum_{s \ne r} \frac{\mathrm{i}\,\omega_r C_{m,sr}}{\omega_r^2 - \omega_s^2}\,\varphi_s

The imaginary part grows with the coupling and as the other modes come close. Close modes, such as the pair a tuned mass damper makes, go complex first. A mode far from the others stays nearly real even under a heavy coupling.

Row r gives the pole. To first order it is λ2 + Cm,rrλ + ωr2 = 0, the approximation's pole. Putting the asr back in adds a second-order term, which moves the frequency but, to this order, not the decay rate Cm,rr/2:

\omega_r^2 \to \omega_r^2\Bigl(1 + \sum_{s \ne r} \frac{C_{m,rs}^2}{\omega_s^2 - \omega_r^2}\Bigr)

The modes above pull a mode's frequency up and the modes below push it down. For the lowest mode every term is positive: a damper stiffens the chain a little, as it starts to hold its ends together.

The state-space form: 2N eigenvalues

The exact modes come from the equations in first order, with the state z = [x, ẋ]:

\dot z = A z, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \\ -M^{-1}K & -M^{-1}C \end{bmatrix}

That doubles the size of the eigenproblem, so there are 2N eigenvalues and 2N eigenvectors. Method → State-space on the Simulation page's Modal analysis tab lists them.

  • Conjugate pairs. A is real, so complex eigenvalues come in pairs λ and λ*, with conjugate shapes ψ and ψ*. Neither alone is a real motion; together they are, x(t) = ψeλt + ψ*eλ*t = 2 Re(ψeλt). In the Simulation page's complex-plane plot the arrows of λ turn anticlockwise and those of λ* clockwise, and their real parts, the displacements, are the same. Each oscillatory mode of the classical table is one pair, with λ = −ζωn ± iωd: the exact ζ is −Re λ/|λ|.
  • Real eigenvalues. A mode damped past critical gives two real eigenvalues instead of a pair: pure decays with time constants τ = −1/λ and no oscillation. A chain with no spring to the ground (k1 = 0) has λ = 0: rigid-body motion, a position it keeps. With the damper c1 still there, a second real root goes with it, close to −c1/(m1 + … + mN) (−0.50 s−1 for the default chain): a push slides the whole chain to a new position as its velocity dies away through c1. With c1 = 0 too, λ = 0 is a repeated root with only one eigenvector (defective): the chain can drift at constant velocity, which no pair of exponentials describes.
  • The eigenvector holds velocities. The state eigenvector is [ψ; λψ]. To start the chain in exactly one mode, set the displacements and the velocities: x(0) = Re ψ, ẋ(0) = Re(λψ). That is what Release selected mode does in this method. It is why a complex mode can be released cleanly with non-proportional damping, while the classical release (the real shape, at rest) sets off other modes too.

Orthogonality. A is not symmetric, so its eigenvectors are not orthogonal in the usual sense. Writing the equations of motion with the identity Mẋ − Mẋ = 0 as a second row makes them symmetric foss:

\begin{bmatrix} C & M \\ M & 0 \end{bmatrix} \dot z + \begin{bmatrix} K & 0 \\ 0 & -M \end{bmatrix} z = 0

Both matrices are symmetric, so two eigenvectors yr = [ψr; λrψr] and ys with λr ≠ λs are orthogonal through each, as φr and φs are through M and K. Through the first, that is

\psi_r^{\mathsf T}\bigl((\lambda_r + \lambda_s)M + C\bigr)\psi_s = 0

The shapes alone are not orthogonal through M: the damping and the eigenvalues come into it. This is why the classical method, with its plain ΦᵀMΦ = I, is preferred whenever the damping is light or nearly proportional.

Is this an artifact of the solver? No, it is physics. Both calculations are exact for the problem they solve: the undamped modes from the symmetric eigenproblem Kφ = ω2Mφ (real by construction), the damped ones from the general eigenproblem of A (complex when the damping is non-proportional). When the damping is proportional the two agree to machine precision. The only approximation is the classical ζ, which describes the damped chain with the undamped shapes; the time simulation always uses the full M, C and K.

Modal coordinates in state space. With the eigenvectors [ψ; λψ] as the columns of Y, the state's modal coordinates are η = Y−1z. They need the velocities as well as the displacements. The coordinates of λ and λ* are conjugates, so each pair is one curve, 2 Re η, in the Simulation page's modal plot. They are independent for any damping: each is a pure decaying oscillation (or decay) unless a force drives it. (The energy panel still splits the energy with the classical modes: the complex ones are not orthogonal through M and K, so their energies have cross terms.)

How complex

Plot a shape's entries ψj in the complex plane (the page's left-hand plot). A real mode, turned by any phase, puts them on one line through the origin. Three numbers say how far a shape is from that.

MPC, the modal phase collinearity pappa, comes from the scatter's sums sxx = Σ(Re ψj)2, syy = Σ(Im ψj)2 and sxy = Σ Re ψj Im ψj:

\mathrm{MPC} = \frac{(s_{xx} - s_{yy})^2 + 4 s_{xy}^2}{(s_{xx} + s_{yy})^2}

The two eigenvalues of [sxx, sxy; sxy, syy] are the scatter's spread along its long and short axes, and MPC is the square of their difference over their sum: 1 when the entries lie on a line, 0 when they spread evenly round the origin.

MPD, the mean phase deviation, measures the angle θj of each entry from the scatter's long axis (the best-fit line, at ½ atan2(2sxy, sxx − syy), the dashed line on the plot), and averages them weighted by size:

\mathrm{MPD} = \frac{\sum_j |\psi_j|\,|\theta_j|}{\sum_j |\psi_j|}

It is 0° for a real mode. An entry pointing back along the line is on it too, so θj runs from −90° to 90°.

MAC allemang-brown sets the exact shape against the undamped one: how much of ψ the shape φ explains, 1 for the same shape at any phase.

\mathrm{MAC}(\varphi, \psi) = \frac{|\varphi^{\mathsf T}\psi|^2}{(\varphi^{\mathsf T}\varphi)(\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi)}

Measured modes are always a little complex (noise, a slightly non-linear structure, a sensor out of phase), so a small MPD says little; a large one in an analysis says the damping couples the modes.

What ignoring it costs

The usual approximation keeps Cm's diagonal and drops the rest. In effect it puts in the proportional damping

C_p = M\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(C_{m,11}, \ldots, C_{m,NN})\,\Phi^{\mathsf T} M

whose modal matrix is that diagonal. It is exact for proportional damping, and good to first order when the damping is light and the modes are well apart (the pole's error is the second-order term above). It fails where a damper is strong enough to change the shapes. A damper that hardly moves in the exact mode (its two ends move together) is counted as if it stretched as in the undamped one, and the approximation's ζ can be ten times too high. It cannot see a mode go overdamped, or a damper lock a joint. The FRF tab compares the receptances, exact (K − Ω2M + iΩC)−1 and with Cp. The Free decay tab releases the chain in an undamped mode's shape: it stays in exactly that mode if the damping is proportional; otherwise the damping hands some of the motion to the other modes, close ones most.

Sweeping a damper

The Sweep tab follows each mode's exact pole as one damper goes from almost nothing to very stiff, the rest of the chain as it is: the root locus. With a light damper every mode's damping grows in proportion to it, as the approximation says. But a damper only works while its ends move apart. As it stiffens it holds them together, and the chain's modes turn into those of the chain with that joint rigid, whose frequencies are different and whose damping falls back towards zero. A damper at the base, locked, holds m1 still: the modes go to those of the 3-mass chain above it, at 1.42, 3.97 and 5.74 Hz, the same frequencies as H11's dips. So each mode's damping rises to a peak and falls again, and on the way one pair (the motion across the damper) leaves as two real, overdamped roots. There is a best damper for each mode, and it is not the stiffest. A damper at the base of the default chain, its other dampers taken out, gives mode 1 at most ζ = 0.147, at about 60 N·s/m; the approximation's ζ keeps growing for ever. The modes are most complex near those peaks, where the damper both works hard and changes the shapes. Sweeping a spring instead moves the modes, and how complex they get depends on how close they come.

Identification

This tab looks ahead to the Virtual modal test page, later, which explains how modes are fitted to a measurement; come back to it after that page. A modal test fits a model to measured FRFs. The Identification tab fits one column (a force on mass k, every response, with a little noise) on the exact poles two ways, by least squares (LSFD). With complex modes the residues may have any phase, which fits any viscous damping. With real modes each entry is

H_{jk} = \sum_r \frac{\varphi_{jr}\varphi_{kr}}{\omega_r^2 - \Omega^2 + 2\mathrm{i}\,\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega}

with real modal constants φjrφkr, the model of a proportionally damped structure. With proportional damping the two agree. Otherwise the real-mode fit can't take the modes' phases: it fits the FRF worse and gets the shapes wrong, and its shapes are not the undamped ones either. Noise makes even real modes come out a little complex from the complex-mode fit, which is why a small MPD in a measurement says little.

Worked example

The page opens on the default chain, four 1 kg masses on 400 N/m springs with a 2 N·s/m damper beside each: C = K/200, coupling index 0, every mode real (MPC 1, MPD 0°) and the approximation exact. The Simulation page's example works out its poles. Load One damper, at the base to see what a single damper does: c1 = 20 N·s/m and no others. The undamped modes are the same as before, from the FRF matrix example:

\omega_r = 6.946,\ 20.00,\ 30.64,\ 37.59\ \text{rad/s} \varphi_{1r}^2 = 0.0520,\ 0.3333,\ 0.4310,\ 0.1836

The damper stretches by φ1r in mode r, so Cm,rr = 20φ1r2 and the approximation gives

\zeta_r = \frac{10\,\varphi_{1r}^2}{\omega_r} = 0.0748,\ 0.167,\ 0.141,\ 0.0489

The table's exact values are 0.0726, 0.178, 0.168 and 0.0225: close for mode 1, more than twice too high for mode 4. The exact frequencies are 1.124, 3.434, 4.569 and 5.823 Hz against the undamped 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz.

Mode 1 is far below the others and lightly damped, so the first-order shape works for it. Its coefficients as1 = iω1Cm,s1/(ω12 − ωs2) are −0.052i, −0.023i and 0.010i for modes 2, 3 and 4, small and imaginary. They give MPD 2.97°, against the exact 2.98°. The second-order frequency is

\omega_1^2 \bigl(1 + 0.0197 + 0.0101 + 0.0028\bigr) = (7.058\ \text{rad/s})^2

against the exact |λ1| = 7.061 rad/s (1.124 Hz), up from 6.946. For modes 2 to 4 the coefficients are not small (mode 2's from mode 3 is 0.28), and the first-order shapes miss: the exact mode 3 is the most complex, MPC 0.20 and MPD 28.9°, as the readout says. Released in mode 2's undamped shape, the chain hands 45 % of it to mode 1.

Things to try

Pick a chain under Things to try on the left (it loads into the Simulation page too):

  • Proportional damping keeps the undamped modes: real shapes, nodes that stand still, and ζr = Cm,rr/2ωr exactly.
  • A damper that isn't proportional couples the modes; the exact modes, from the 2N × 2N state matrix, are then complex, most of all where modes are close.
  • The usual approximation keeps only the diagonal of Cm: right to first order for light damping and separated modes, wrong (even ten times) where a damper changes the shapes.
  • A damper has a best size for each mode: past it, it locks its joint and damps less.
  • MPC and MPD measure how complex a shape is; a measured mode is always a little complex, so only a large value means much.
