Building a rotor

The Jeffcott rotor page has one disc on one shaft between two bearings, worked out by hand. A real machine has a shaft stepped to carry its parts, several discs (impellers, a flywheel, a coupling), bearings of different kinds and seals between them. This page builds one the way the Frame builder built a frame: from parts, each knowing its own matrices, added up into the rotor's M, C, G and K. Then it solves for the whirl modes at a speed.

The shaft runs along z. Every node has four DOFs: x (sideways), its slope θx = dx/dz, y (up) and its slope θy = dy/dz (xi, θxi, yi, θyi in the tables). A node sits at every section's ends and at every part, and each section is cut into its elements between them.

The shaft's elements

A section is a round tube, outside diameter D and bore d (0: solid), so A = π(D² − d²)/4 and its diametral second moment I = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64; the polar one, about the spin axis, is twice that. In each plane an element is the Frame builder's beam on (w1, θ1, w2, θ2): with slopes as the turning DOFs in both planes, the x plane's matrices and the y plane's are the same. By default it is Timoshenko's, which shears (φ = 12EI/κGAL², κ Cowper's coefficient for a tube) and whose sections' turning has inertia; or Euler–Bernoulli's with that inertia kept (a Rayleigh beam). A rotor's shaft is often short and thick between its bearings, and shear matters more than on most frames.

A hollow shaft is stiffer for its mass: I/A = (D² + d²)/16, so boring it out takes mass faster than stiffness, and the frequencies, which go as √(EI/ρA), rise.

Discs

A disc is rigid, at one node: its mass m on x and y, its diametral moment of inertia Id on θx and θy. From the library it is cut from a material: an annulus of radii R and r and thickness t has m = ρπ(R² − r²)t, Ip = m(R² + r²)/2 and Id = m(3(R² + r²) + t²)/12. A thin disc has Ip ≈ 2Id, a long one (a coupling hub) Ip < Id.

Bearings

A bearing pushes the shaft back from the ground at its node: f = −Kb[x, y] − Cb[x', y'], 2 × 2 matrices on the node's x and y. With springs and dampers they are what you give: kxx and kyy may differ (a pedestal stiffer sideways than up), and cross terms kxy and kyx may couple them.

An oil film (a plain journal bearing, as on the Jeffcott rotor page's Fluid-film bearing tab) has no stiffness of its own: the journal floats where the film's pressure holds up its load W. So each bearing's load comes first, its share of the rotor's weight with every bearing held (on a vertical rotor there is none, and the film runs centred). From W, the oil's viscosity, the bearing's size and the speed, the short-bearing theory gives where the journal sits (its eccentricity ε) and the film's eight coefficients, a full Kf(Ω) and Cf(Ω), both changing with the speed. Kf is not symmetric: the oil, dragged round at about half the shaft's speed, pushes a displaced journal on round, and past about twice a mode's frequency that feeds its forward whirl (oil whip).

A magnetic bearing (the Magnetic bearing tab's) pulls the journal toward each magnet harder the nearer it comes: a negative stiffness −km on its x and y. A PID controller behind an amplifier sets the current that holds it, so the bearing has states of its own (the integral, the filtered derivative, the amplifier's current, each axis), which follow q and q' in the state: the rotor's state matrix is A = [[0, I, 0], [−M⁻¹K, −M⁻¹(C + ΩG), M⁻¹Fc], [Bc, 0, Ac]].

Seals

An annular seal (between an impeller's stages, or at a shaft's end) is a thin film of the fluid the machine handles: a direct stiffness k and damping c on x and y, an added mass, and, because the fluid swirls round at a fraction λ of the shaft's speed Ω, a cross-coupled stiffness kxy = λΩc, kyx = −kxy. That is a skew stiffness growing with the speed, Ω H in K(Ω): it pushes a forward whirl along its path, taking damping from it (and giving it to the backward whirl). At the speed where it takes it all, the forward whirl grows by itself.

Gyroscopic moments

A spinning disc that tilts has to turn its angular momentum IpΩ, and that takes a moment: in the slopes' equations, Idθx'' + ΩIpθy' = Mx and Idθy'' − ΩIpθx' = My. So the disc puts +Ip and −Ip between its two slopes in a matrix G, which enters with the velocities, as Ω G. G is skew (Gᵀ = −G), so q'ᵀGq' = 0: it does no work and dissipates nothing. The shaft's sections spin too, each with a polar inertia twice its diametral one, so each element's share of G is twice its rotary inertia matrix, between the x plane's slopes and the y plane's.

A whirling, tilting disc meets the gyroscopic moment as a stiffness: positive for a forward whirl (its frequency rises with the speed), negative for a backward one (it falls). A free body, held by nothing, shows it on its own: its tilt becomes a slow forward whirl, the nutation, at IpΩ/Id (the Gyroscope page's top).

Assembly

Each part adds its matrices at its nodes' DOFs, as the Frame builder's elements did, but no transformation is needed: every element lies along z, so its own axes are the rotor's. The equations are

M q'' + (C(Ω) + Ω G) q' + K(Ω) q = f,

K(Ω) = K + ΩH + Kf(Ω) and C(Ω) = C + Cf(Ω), the speed's terms the seals' and the films'. Nothing is struck out: a bearing is a spring, not a support, so every DOF is free, and a rotor without bearings has modes at 0 Hz. The Assembly tab shows a matrix at the speed with the picked part's share tinted, and the Element tab the part's own.

Modes at a speed

G, the films' and the seals' terms make the damping non-proportional and K unsymmetric, so the modes come from the state space (the Complex modes page's): z = [q, q'] (and the controllers' states), z' = A(Ω)z, and the eigenvalues λ = −ζω ± iωd of A at that speed. The table lists each one's damped frequency, its damping ratio and its logarithmic decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²), the measure the API's stability rules use. A negative ζ (and δ) grows: at that speed the rotor is unstable. Change the speed and the modes move: that is the Campbell diagram, point by point.

Forward and backward whirl

Each mode's shape is complex: at each node x = Re(X eiωt), y = Re(Y eiωt), an ellipse. The whirl ratio weighs the ellipses by kinetic energy: +1 when every node goes round circularly with the spin (forward, x toward y), −1 against it (backward), 0 for a straight line. A round rotor not spinning has each frequency twice, and any mix of the pair is a mode: the page picks the circular forward and backward ones. On anisotropic bearings the pair splits into two straight lines, the soft way and the stiff way, and the spin couples them into ellipses, one forward and one backward. The Whirl tab draws the mode in 3D, the side view the up-and-down part of it.

The Campbell diagram

Work the modes out at every speed from rest to the top one and plot their frequencies against the speed: the Campbell diagram (the Campbell tab). Each pair fans out, its forward branch rising and its backward one falling, most where the discs tilt. The straight line f = N/60 is 1X, the shaft's own speed: anything that turns with the shaft, an unbalance above all, pushes once a revolution. Where a branch meets it the push is at that mode's own frequency: a critical speed. Each is worked out by bisection between the sweep's speeds.

How hard a critical bites is its amplification factor, AF = 1/2ζ, the peak's height over the response well away from it. API 684 (2nd edition, 2005) doesn't count a critical with AF under 2.5 (ζ over 20 %; hollow circles): a rotor on oil films passes its bounce on them without noticing. The separation margin is how far the running speed is from the nearest critical, as a fraction of the running speed; machines are run well clear of their criticals, or through them quickly.

The sweep works in a Ritz basis: the lowest 32 undamped modes of a symmetric stand-in for the rotor (its films' direct stiffness, each magnet's proportional spring kikp), into which M, C, G and K are projected, G and the cross terms included. The low whirl modes come out as the full model's to a few parts in a million (with oil films, whose stiffness changes, the basis is made again at each speed), at a millisecond a speed however fine the mesh.

The unbalance response

A disc whose centre of mass sits a distance e off the shaft's has an unbalance U = me. Keeping it on its circle takes a force UΩ², turning with the shaft: fx + ify = UeiψΩ²eiΩt, ψ the heavy spot's angle from the keyphasor mark. So Fx = UeiψΩ² and Fy = −iFx at its node, and the steady response, exactly, is

(K(Ω) − Ω²M + iΩ(C(Ω) + ΩG) + D(iΩ)) Q = F,

D the magnets' controllers at that frequency. The Unbalance tab plots one node's orbit, its major semi-axis |X + iY|/2 + |X − iY|/2 (its forward circle's radius plus its backward one's), and how far its x lags the heavy spot. Through a forward critical the orbit peaks and the lag swings from 0 to 180°, 90° at the peak; above it the disc turns about its centre of mass, the heavy spot inside the orbit. A backward critical hardly shows: the unbalance pushes forward, and on round bearings it does no work on a backward whirl. On anisotropic ones the modes are part forward, part backward, and it does.

Stability

The same sweep gives each branch's damping: the Stability tab plots its logarithmic decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²), the log of one free swing's peak over the next, against the speed. API 684 asks for δ ≥ 0.1 at the running speed (dashed). Where the least damped mode's δ crosses 0 the rotor goes unstable: that mode grows by itself, from nothing, with no force driving it, until something nonlinear stops it (or the machine). The red line marks the onset, found by bisection. Its sources here are all skew stiffnesses, Ω H and the films' cross terms, which push a forward whirl along its path: an oil film past about twice a mode's frequency (oil whip), a seal's swirl, and the shaft's internal damping.

Internal damping

A shaft's material damping (or a shrink fit slipping on it) resists its bending as the shaft feels it, turning with it: the bending rate in the shaft is q' − ΩJq, J turning x toward y. So it is −βKshaft(q' − ΩJq): ordinary damping βKshaft in C, and a skew βKshaft between the planes in Ω H. Below a critical speed the whirl outruns the shaft and it damps; at a forward critical the whirl is synchronous, the turning shaft doesn't flex at all, and it does nothing; above, the shaft outruns its whirl and the damping drags the whirl on round, feeding it. With some damping in the bearings the onset comes above the first critical, at roughly Ωc(1 + cbearings/cinternal): more internal damping brings it down, more external damping pushes it up. That is why built-up rotors with shrink fits whirled themselves apart in the 1920s, and why the cure is damping in the bearings.

Things to try

  • A rotor is assembled like any structure, part by part: beams for the shaft, rigid discs, bearings as springs and dampers (or as films and magnets, with terms that change with the speed), seals as cross-coupled springs.
  • Each node has x, y and their two slopes; with slopes in both planes the two planes share their beam matrices, and only G (and any cross-coupling) joins them.
  • The spin adds Ω G, skew: it does no work, but it splits each pair into a forward whirl that stiffens and a backward one that softens, most where the discs tilt.
  • Oil films and seals add a skew stiffness growing with the speed; it feeds forward whirl, and past some speed a mode's ζ goes negative: the rotor is unstable.
  • The Campbell diagram's crossings with 1X are the critical speeds; an unbalance drives the forward ones, and their amplification factor 1/2ζ says how hard.
  • Internal damping damps at rest but drives the forward whirl above a critical speed; damping in the bearings holds it off.
  • Shear matters on a stubby shaft: Timoshenko's beams are softer than Euler–Bernoulli's, the higher modes most.
