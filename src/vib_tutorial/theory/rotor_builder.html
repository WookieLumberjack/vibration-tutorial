Building a rotor

The Jeffcott rotor page has one disc on one shaft between two bearings. A real machine has a shaft stepped to carry its parts, several discs (impellers, a flywheel, a coupling), bearings of different kinds, and seals between them. This page builds one the way the Frame builder built a frame: from parts, each knowing its own matrices, added up into the rotor's M, C, G and K. Then it solves for the whirl modes at a speed. Every matrix here is derived in the Jeffcott rotor page's part 2 (see The equations of motion): the gyroscopic moment, how each matrix is built, the beam elements, and why the eigenproblem is hard. This page applies them to many parts at once.

A rotor as the page builds it. Three sections, the middle one thicker (outside diameter D) and bored (bore d), each cut into elements between nodes. A disc of radius R sits at a node, a bearing at each end is a spring and a damper to the ground, and a seal rings the first section.

The shaft runs along z. Every node has four DOFs: x (sideways), its slope θx = dx/dz, y (up) and its slope θy = dy/dz. The tables call them xi, θxi, yi and θyi. A node sits at every section's ends and at every part, and each section is cut into its elements between them.

The shaft's elements

A section is a round tube, outside diameter D and bore d (0 for a solid one). Its area is As = π(D² − d²)/4, and its diametral second moment of area is I = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64. The polar one, about the spin axis, is twice that. In each plane an element is the Frame builder's beam on (w1, θ1, w2, θ2). With slopes as the turning DOFs in both planes, the x plane's matrices and the y plane's are the same (see Beam finite elements, Jeffcott rotor page).

By default the element is Timoshenko's, which shears and whose sections' turning has inertia (Nelson's rotating element nelson-1980, with Friedman and Kosmatka's matrices friedman-kosmatka). The alternative is Euler–Bernoulli's with that inertia kept, a Rayleigh beam rayleigh (Nelson and McVaugh's element nelson-mcvaugh). The shear enters through

\varphi_s = \frac{12EI}{\kappa_s G_m A_s L_e^2}

with Le the element's length, Gm = E/2(1 + ν) the shear modulus, and κs Cowper's shear coefficient for a tube cowper. A rotor's shaft is often short and thick between its bearings, so shear matters more than on most frames.

A hollow shaft is stiffer for its mass. I/As = (D² + d²)/16, so boring a shaft out takes mass faster than stiffness, and the frequencies, which go as √(EI/ρAs), rise.

Discs

A disc is rigid, at one node. It adds its mass m on x and y, its diametral moment of inertia Id on θx and θy, and its polar one Ip to G. One from the library is cut from a material. An annulus of radii R and r and thickness td has

m = \rho\pi(R^2 - r^2)\,t_d, \qquad I_p = \tfrac12 m(R^2 + r^2) I_d = \tfrac{1}{12} m\bigl(3(R^2 + r^2) + t_d^2\bigr)

A thin disc has Ip ≈ 2Id, and a long one, a coupling hub, has Ip < Id.

Bearings

A bearing pushes the shaft back from the ground at its node, f = −Kb[x, y] − Cb[ẋ, ẏ], with Kb and Cb 2 × 2 matrices on the node's x and y. With springs and dampers they are what you give: kxx and kyy may differ (a pedestal stiffer sideways than up), and cross terms kxy and kyx may couple them.

An oil film (a plain journal bearing, as on the Jeffcott rotor page's Fluid-film bearing tab) has no stiffness of its own: the journal floats where the film's pressure holds up its load W. So each bearing's load comes first: its share of the rotor's weight with every bearing held. On a vertical rotor there is none, and the film runs centred. From W, the oil's viscosity η, the bearing's size and the speed, short-bearing theory ocvirk-1952 gives where the journal sits (its eccentricity ε) and the film's eight coefficients: a full Kf(Ω) and Cf(Ω), both changing with the speed. Kf is not symmetric. The oil, dragged round at about half the shaft's speed, pushes a displaced journal on round, and past about twice a mode's frequency that feeds its forward whirl (oil whip).

A magnetic bearing (the Magnetic bearing tab's) pulls the journal toward each magnet harder the nearer it comes: a negative stiffness −km on its x and y. A PID controller behind an amplifier sets the current that holds it, so the bearing has states of its own: the integral, the filtered derivative and the amplifier's current, on each axis. They follow q and q̇ in the state, and the rotor's state matrix becomes (with 0 and 1 the zero and identity blocks)

A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -M^{-1}K & -M^{-1}(C + \Omega G) & M^{-1}F_c \\ B_c & 0 & A_c \end{bmatrix}

Seals

An annular seal (between an impeller's stages, or at a shaft's end) is a thin film of the fluid the machine handles. It has a direct stiffness k and damping c on x and y, and an added mass. The fluid swirls round at a fraction λ of the shaft's speed Ω, which gives a cross-coupled stiffness kxy = λΩc, kyx = −kxy. That is a skew stiffness growing with the speed, ΩH in K(Ω). It pushes a forward whirl along its path, taking damping from it and giving it to the backward whirl. At the speed where it takes all of it, the forward whirl grows by itself.

Gyroscopic moments

A spinning disc that tilts has to turn its angular momentum IpΩ, and that takes a moment (derived in The gyroscopic moment from angular momentum, Jeffcott rotor page):

I_d\ddot\theta_x + \Omega I_p\dot\theta_y = M_x, \qquad I_d\ddot\theta_y - \Omega I_p\dot\theta_x = M_y

So the disc puts +Ip and −Ip between its two slopes in a matrix G, which enters with the velocities, as ΩG. G is skew (GT = −G), so q̇TGq̇ = 0: it does no work and dissipates nothing. The shaft's sections spin too, each with a polar inertia twice its diametral one, so each element's share of G is twice its rotary inertia matrix, between the x plane's slopes and the y plane's.

A whirling, tilting disc meets the gyroscopic moment as a stiffness: positive for a forward whirl (its frequency rises with the speed), negative for a backward one (it falls). A free body, held by nothing, shows it on its own: its tilt becomes a slow forward whirl, the nutation, at IpΩ/Id (the Gyroscope page's top).

Assembly

Each part adds its matrices at its nodes' DOFs, as the Frame builder's elements did. No transformation is needed: every element lies along z, so its own axes are the rotor's. The equations are

M\ddot q + \bigl(C(\Omega) + \Omega G\bigr)\dot q + K(\Omega)\,q = f

with K(Ω) = K + ΩH + Kf(Ω) and C(Ω) = C + Cf(Ω), the speed's terms being the seals' and the films'. Nothing is struck out. A bearing is a spring, not a support, so every DOF is free, and a rotor without bearings has modes at 0 Hz. The Assembly tab shows a matrix at the speed with the picked part's share tinted, and the Element tab shows the part's own.

Modes at a speed

G, the films' terms and the seals' make the damping non-proportional and K unsymmetric, so the modes come from the state space (see Solving: modes that move with the speed, Jeffcott rotor page): Z = [q, q̇] and the controllers' states, Ż = A(Ω)Z, and the eigenvalues s = −ζω ± iωd of A at that speed. The table lists each one's damped frequency, its damping ratio and its logarithmic decrement δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²), the measure the API's stability rules use. A negative ζ (and δ) grows: at that speed the rotor is unstable. Change the speed and the modes move. That is the Campbell diagram, point by point.

Forward and backward whirl

Each mode's shape is complex. At each node x = Re(Xeiωt) and y = Re(Yeiωt): an ellipse. The whirl ratio weighs the ellipses by kinetic energy. It is +1 when every node goes round circularly with the spin (forward, x toward y), −1 against it (backward), and 0 for a straight line. A round rotor not spinning has each frequency twice, and any mix of the pair is a mode, so the page picks the circular forward and backward ones. On anisotropic bearings the pair splits into two straight lines, the soft way and the stiff way, and the spin couples them into ellipses, one forward and one backward. The Whirl tab draws the mode in 3D, and the side view its up-and-down part.

The Campbell diagram

Work the modes out at every speed from rest to the top one and plot their frequencies against the speed: the Campbell diagram campbell-1924 (the Campbell tab). Each pair fans out, its forward branch rising and its backward one falling, most where the discs tilt. The straight line at the shaft's own speed, the rpm over 60 in Hz, is 1X: anything that turns with the shaft, an unbalance above all, pushes once a revolution. Where a branch meets it, the push is at that mode's own frequency: a critical speed. Each is worked out by bisection between the sweep's speeds.

How hard a critical bites is its amplification factor, AF = 1/2ζ: the peak's height over the response well away from it. API 684 api-684 doesn't count a critical with AF under 2.5 (ζ over 20%, drawn as hollow circles): a rotor on oil films passes its bounce on them without noticing. The separation margin SM is how far the running speed is from the nearest critical, as a fraction of the running speed. Machines are run well clear of their criticals, or through them quickly, and the API's rules ask for more margin from a sharper critical.

The sweep works in a Ritz basis ritz: the lowest 32 undamped modes of a symmetric stand-in for the rotor (its films' direct stiffness, and each magnet's proportional spring kikp), into which M, C, G and K are projected, G and the cross terms included. The low whirl modes come out as the full model's to a few parts in a million, at a millisecond a speed however fine the mesh. With oil films, whose stiffness changes, the basis is made again at each speed.

The unbalance response

A disc whose centre of mass sits a distance e off the shaft's has an unbalance U = me. Keeping it on its circle takes a force UΩ², turning with the shaft: fx + ify = UeiψΩ²eiΩt, with ψ the heavy spot's angle from the keyphasor mark. So Fx = UeiψΩ² and Fy = −iFx at its node, and the steady response, exactly, is

\bigl(K(\Omega) - \Omega^2 M + \mathrm{i}\Omega(C(\Omega) + \Omega G) + K_c\bigr)\,Q = F

with Kc the magnets' controllers' push at that frequency, kiGc(iΩ). The Unbalance tab plots one node's orbit, its major semi-axis |X + iY|/2 + |X − iY|/2 (its forward circle's radius plus its backward one's), and how far its x lags the heavy spot. Through a forward critical the orbit peaks and the lag swings from 0 to 180°, 90° at the peak. Above it the disc turns about its centre of mass, the heavy spot inside the orbit. A backward critical hardly shows: the unbalance pushes forward, and on round bearings it does no work on a backward whirl. On anisotropic ones the modes are part forward, part backward, and it does.

Stability

The same sweep gives each branch's damping. The Stability tab plots its logarithmic decrement, δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²), the log of one free swing's peak over the next, against the speed. API 684 asks for δ ≥ 0.1 at the running speed (dashed). Where the least damped mode's δ crosses 0 the rotor goes unstable: that mode grows by itself, from nothing, with no force driving it, until something nonlinear stops it (or the machine). The red line marks the onset, found by bisection. Its sources here are all skew stiffnesses, ΩH and the films' cross terms, which push a forward whirl along its path: an oil film past about twice a mode's frequency (oil whip), a seal's swirl, and the shaft's internal damping.

Internal damping

A shaft's material damping, or a shrink fit slipping on it, resists its bending as the shaft feels it, turning with it. The bending rate in the shaft is q̇ − ΩJq, with J turning x toward y. So the damping force is −τiKshaft(q̇ − ΩJq), with τi its loss time: ordinary damping τiKshaft in C, and a skew τiKshaft between the planes in ΩH. Below a critical speed the whirl outruns the shaft and it damps. At a forward critical the whirl is synchronous, the turning shaft doesn't flex at all, and it does nothing. Above, the shaft outruns its whirl, and the damping drags the whirl on round, feeding it. With damping ce in the bearings, the onset comes above the first critical, at roughly ωc(1 + ce/ci) (see Cross-coupling and internal damping, Jeffcott rotor page). More internal damping brings it down, and more external damping pushes it up. That is why built-up rotors with shrink fits whirled themselves apart in the 1920s kimball-1924, and why the cure is damping in the bearings.

How fine a mesh

Each element bends as a cubic. That is exact for a static load between nodes but not for a vibrating shaft, so a mode needs a few elements per half wave. A mode n of a uniform shaft has n half waves. On the opening shaft, pinned, the third pair comes out at 912.7, 893.2, 891.3 and 890.5 Hz with 1, 2, 3 and 6 elements per section (4 to 24 in all), against Timoshenko's exact 890.3 Hz. The first pair is within 0.04 Hz of its exact 101.25 Hz with only 4. The consistent mass keeps every error on the high side, as a Rayleigh–Ritz model's must (see Beam finite elements). Three elements a section, the page's default, leave the third pair 0.12% high.

Worked example: the opening shaft

The page opens on a steel shaft 1 m long and 50 mm across (E = 210 GPa, ν = 0.3, ρ = 7850 kg/m³). It is four sections of three Timoshenko elements, 13 nodes and 52 DOFs, on stiff bearings at its ends, so it is nearly pinned. Its modes table reads 101.25, 401.5 and 891.3 Hz, each twice.

  1. The section. As = 1963.5 mm² and I = 3.068 × 10−7 m⁴, so EI = 64.43 kN·m² and the shaft has 15.41 kg/m, 15.41 kg in all.
  2. Euler's frequencies. A pinned uniform beam euler-1744 has fn = (n²π/2)√(EI/ρAsL⁴): 101.56, 406.2 and 914.0 Hz.
  3. Rotary inertia. To first order it lowers mode n by ½(nπrg/L)², with rg = √(I/As) = D/4 = 12.5 mm (the Beams & plates page's correction, see When a beam is thick). That is 0.077% for the first mode and 0.69% for the third: 101.48 and 907.7 Hz. Switch Beams to Euler–Bernoulli, and the page gives 101.48 and 908.0 Hz.
  4. Shear. Timoshenko's beam timoshenko multiplies that correction by 1 + E/κsGm. For steel κs = 0.886 and E/Gm = 2.6, so the factor is 3.933: 0.30% off the first mode, 101.25 Hz, and 2.7% off the third, 889.1 Hz, against the page's 101.25 and 891.3 Hz. The third is off by more because the correction is only first order, and the exact 890.3 Hz lies between.
  5. The shear parameter. Over one element, 83.3 mm long, φs = 0.792: each element is stubby. Over the whole span it is 0.0055. Mode n bends over L/n, where φs is n² times that, which is why shear softens the higher modes most.

Things to try

  • A rotor is assembled like any structure, part by part: beams for the shaft, rigid discs, bearings as springs and dampers (or as films and magnets, with terms that change with the speed), and seals as cross-coupled springs.
  • Each node has x, y and their two slopes. With slopes in both planes the two planes share their beam matrices, and only G (and any cross-coupling) joins them.
  • The spin adds Ω G, which is skew: it does no work, but it splits each pair into a forward whirl that stiffens and a backward one that softens, most where the discs tilt.
  • Oil films and seals add a skew stiffness growing with the speed. It feeds forward whirl, and past some speed a mode's ζ goes negative: the rotor is unstable.
  • The Campbell diagram's crossings with 1X are the critical speeds. An unbalance drives the forward ones, and their amplification factor 1/2ζ says how hard.
  • Internal damping damps at rest but drives the forward whirl above a critical speed; damping in the bearings holds it off.
  • Shear matters on a stubby shaft: Timoshenko's beams are softer than Euler–Bernoulli's, the higher modes most.
