Every method on this page works from the chain's two energies, without solving its eigenproblem. Before computers that was the point: a pencil, the energies, and a bound on the answer. Now the same ideas are how big models are cut down to size (Rayleigh–Ritz) and checked (a quick Rayleigh estimate against a solver's f1).

The equations from the energies

The chain's kinetic and potential energies are (with x0 = 0, the ground)

T = \tfrac12 \sum_i m_i \dot x_i^2 = \tfrac12\,\dot x^{\mathsf T} M \dot x V = \tfrac12 \sum_i k_i (x_i - x_{i-1})^2 = \tfrac12\,x^{\mathsf T} K x

and Lagrange's equations lagrange, in any coordinates qi,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot q_i} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_i} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} = Q_i

turn them into Mẍ + Kx = f with no free-body diagrams: M and K are the energies' second derivatives,

M_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial \dot x_i\,\partial \dot x_j}, \qquad K_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}

Spring i stretches by xi − xi−1, so it adds ki to the two diagonal entries of the masses it joins and −ki to the two between them: the assembly the Simulation page uses (the Lagrange tab works them out for the chain you have). The same recipe works in any coordinates: angles, a beam's modal amplitudes, the generalized coordinates of the pages that follow.

Rayleigh's quotient: an upper bound

In a mode, x = v sin ωt, the largest kinetic energy ½ω²vᵀMv equals the largest potential energy ½vᵀKv, so

\omega^2 = R(v) = \frac{v^{\mathsf T} K v}{v^{\mathsf T} M v}

Put in any shape v and this Rayleigh quotient rayleigh-sound is an estimate of ω1². To see how good, write the shape in the mass-normalized modes, mode 1 and some of each other mode, v = φ1 + Σ εrφr. The modes are orthogonal (φrᵀMφs = 0 and φrᵀKφs = 0 for r ≠ s), so the cross terms drop out and

R(v) = \frac{\omega_1^2 + \sum_r \varepsilon_r^2 \omega_r^2}{1 + \sum_r \varepsilon_r^2} = \omega_1^2 + \frac{\sum_r \varepsilon_r^2 (\omega_r^2 - \omega_1^2)}{1 + \sum_r \varepsilon_r^2}

So R is never below ω1² (every added term is positive), and its error is second order in the shape's: a shape 10 % wrong (ε = 0.1) puts ω² about 1 % times (ωr/ω1)² − 1 high, and the frequency half that.

A good shape to try is the static deflection under the masses' own weights: mode 1's inertia forces, miω²xi, are spread much like the weights. Then Kv is the weights, vᵀKv = g Σ mivi, and the quotient needs only the sag:

\omega_1^2 \approx g\,\frac{\sum_i m_i v_i}{\sum_i m_i v_i^2}

Improving the shape

The static deflection under the inertia forces of a guessed motion is a better guess:

v' = K^{-1} M v

Since K−1Mφr = φr/ωr², each mode's share is divided by its ωr²: against mode 1's, mode r's falls by ω1²/ωr² per pass, εr′ = εrω1²/ωr², and the quotient's error by the square of that. This is inverse iteration. The static-deflection shape is one pass from every mass moving together (v = 1, whose inertia forces are the weights' shape).

Dunkerley: a lower bound

The flexibility matrix K−1 turns forces into deflections: its column i is the chain's sag under 1 N on mass i, and (K−1)ii is how far mass i itself moves. On the chain the force is carried by the springs between mass i and the ground, and the rest ride along:

(K^{-1})_{ii} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{k_i}

The eigenvalues of K−1M are the 1/ωr² (from Kφ = ω²Mφ), and a matrix's trace is the sum of its eigenvalues. With M diagonal the trace is Σ mi(K−1)ii, so

\frac{1}{\omega_1^2} < \sum_r \frac{1}{\omega_r^2} = \sum_i m_i (K^{-1})_{ii} = \sum_i \frac{1}{\omega_{ii}^2}

Each ωii is mass i alone on the massless chain, so Dunkerley's estimate dunkerley "adds up the masses one at a time". It is below ω1 by what the higher modes add to the sum, so it does best when one mass dominates. With Rayleigh's it brackets the exact value.

Rayleigh–Ritz

Restrict the motion to a mix of a few shapes, the columns of W, with weights a: x = Wa. Making Rayleigh's quotient stationary in a ritz gives a small eigenproblem, one row per shape:

(W^{\mathsf T} K W)\,a = \omega^2\,(W^{\mathsf T} M W)\,a

It gives as many frequencies as there are shapes, and each is above its mode's exact value (the separation, or min–max, theorem poincare). Adding a shape brings every estimate down, the new ones interlacing the old; with all N shapes it is exact.

The answer is only as good as the shapes. Shapes the structure makes itself do far better than generic polynomials that ignore where it is stiff: the static deflection, then K−1M applied to it again and again (Krylov, or load-dependent Ritz, vectors wilson-ritz). Each such shape adds the next pass of inverse iteration, so the first mode converges fastest. Every reduced model on the later pages (substructuring, modal coupling, finite elements) is a Rayleigh–Ritz model.

Holzer's table

For a chain there is a way with no matrices at all holzer. Guess ω, set x1 = 1 and walk along the chain. Spring 1 pulls mass 1 back with N1 = k1x1. Moving as xi sin ωt, mass i needs a net miω²xi towards the ground, so the next spring is left to carry the rest:

N_{i+1} = N_i - m_i \omega^2 x_i, \qquad x_{i+1} = x_i + \frac{N_{i+1}}{k_{i+1}}

Past the last mass there is no spring, so at a natural frequency NN+1 is zero. At any other ω it is the residual, a polynomial in ω² (it is det(K − ω²M) over k2k3…kN) that changes sign at each natural frequency, so they can be found by bisection, mode by mode, with a pencil. Holzer's method was the standard for torsional drive trains (see the Torsional vibration page, later in the course) before computers.

Worked example

The page opens on the uniform chain: four 1 kg masses on four 400 N/m springs, whose exact frequencies (from the closed form) are 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz, ω1² = 48.246 (rad/s)².

The static deflection. Under 1 N on each mass, spring 1 carries all four newtons, spring 2 three, spring 3 two and spring 4 one, so they stretch 4, 3, 2 and 1 (in 1/400 m) and the masses sag v = (4, 7, 9, 10)/400 m. With Kv the 1 N loads,

R = \frac{\sum v_i}{\sum v_i^2} = \frac{30 \times 400}{16 + 49 + 81 + 100} = \frac{12000}{246} = 48.78

so ω = 6.984 rad/s and f1 ≈ 1.112 Hz, 0.55 % high: the readout's Rayleigh line.

Dunkerley. From the figure, (K−1)ii = i/400 m/N, so mass i alone is at ωii² = 400/i: 20, 14.14, 11.55 and 10 rad/s, or 3.18, 2.25, 1.84 and 1.59 Hz. The sum Σ mi(K−1)ii = 10/400 = 0.025 s² gives ω = √40 = 6.325 rad/s, f1 ≥ 1.007 Hz, 8.9 % low. (The sum is also Σ 1/ωr² exactly: mode 1 alone is 0.0207 of the 0.025.) So 1.007 ≤ f1 ≤ 1.112 Hz, with no eigenvalue solved.

Rayleigh–Ritz with two Krylov shapes (the page's default). The second shape is the sag under the first's inertia forces: loads (4, 7, 9, 10) give spring forces 30, 26, 19 and 10, so w2 = (30, 56, 75, 85) (scaled; a shape's scale doesn't matter). Since Kw1 and Kw2 are 400 times the loads (1, 1, 1, 1) and w1, the reduced matrices are sums of products:

W^{\mathsf T} M W = \begin{bmatrix} 246 & 2037 \\ 2037 & 16886 \end{bmatrix} W^{\mathsf T} K W = 400 \begin{bmatrix} 30 & 246 \\ 246 & 2037 \end{bmatrix}

Their determinant equation is 4587 ω⁴ − 2.1912×10⁶ ω² + 9.504×10⁷ = 0, so ω² = 48.246 and 429.45: f1 = 1.105 Hz, exact to five figures, and f2 = 3.298 Hz against 3.183, 3.6 % high. Both above, as the theorem says.

Holzer at 1.00 Hz (ω² = 39.48, the Holzer tab's start). Spring 1 pulls 400 N; mass 1 keeps 39.5 N and passes 360.5 N, so x2 = 1 + 360.5/400 = 1.901; mass 2 keeps 75.1 N and passes 285.5 N, x3 = 2.615; mass 3 keeps 103.2 N and passes 182.2 N, x4 = 3.071; mass 4 keeps 121.2 N and leaves N5 = 61.0 N. Positive, so 1 Hz is below f1; the sign changes at 1.105 Hz.
