The equations from the energies

The chain's kinetic and potential energies are

T = ½ Σ miẋi² = ½ ẋᵀMẋ,   V = ½ Σ ki(xi − xi−1)² = ½ xᵀKx   (x₀ = 0, the ground).

Lagrange's equations, d/dt(∂T/∂ẋi) − ∂T/∂xi + ∂V/∂xi = Qi, turn these into Mẍ + Kx = f with no free-body diagrams: M and K are just the energies' second derivatives (the Lagrange tab works them out for the chain you have). The same recipe works for any coordinates: angles, a beam's modal amplitudes, the generalized coordinates of the pages that follow.

Rayleigh's quotient: an upper bound

In a mode x = X sin ωt, the largest kinetic energy ½ω²XᵀMX equals the largest potential energy ½XᵀKX, so

ω² = XᵀKX / XᵀMX.

Put in any shape X and this Rayleigh quotient R(X) is an estimate of ω₁². Written in the modes, X = φ₁ + Σ εrφr (mass-normalized),

R(X) = (ω₁² + Σ εr²ωr²) / (1 + Σ εr²) = ω₁² + Σ εr²(ωr² − ω₁²) / (1 + Σ εr²).

So R is never below ω₁² (every added term is positive), and its error is second order in the shape's: a shape 10 % wrong (ε = 0.1) gives ω² about 1 % wrong, so the frequency about 0.5 %, times (ωr/ω₁)² − 1. A good shape to try is the static deflection under the masses' weights: the inertia forces of mode 1 are distributed much like the weights.

Improving the shape

The static deflection under the inertia forces of a guessed motion, X' = K⁻¹MX, is a better guess: each mode's share is divided by its ωr², so mode r's share against mode 1's falls by ω₁²/ωr² per pass (inverse iteration, or Stodola's method). The static-deflection shape is one pass from a rigid guess.

Dunkerley: a lower bound

With the flexibility matrix F = K⁻¹, the trace of FM is Σ 1/ωr², so

1/ω₁² < Σr 1/ωr² = Σi mifii.

Each term mifii is 1/ω² of mass i alone on the massless chain, so Dunkerley's estimate "adds up the masses one at a time". It is below ω₁ by how much the higher modes contribute to the trace: it does best when one mass dominates. With Rayleigh's it brackets the exact value.

Rayleigh–Ritz

Restrict the motion to a combination of r shapes, x = Qa, and make Rayleigh's quotient stationary in a: that is the small eigenproblem

(QᵀKQ) a = ω² (QᵀMQ) a.

It gives r frequencies at once, and each is above its mode's exact value (the Poincaré separation, or min–max, theorem), falling as shapes are added (with one more shape, the old estimates and the new interlace); with all N shapes it is exact. The quality depends on the shapes: shapes the structure makes itself (static deflections, and K⁻¹M applied to them again: Krylov or load-dependent Ritz vectors) do far better than generic polynomials that ignore where the structure is stiff. Every reduced model on the later pages (substructuring, modal coupling, finite elements) is a Rayleigh–Ritz model.

Holzer's table

For a chain, guess ω, set x₁ = 1 and walk along it: the ground spring pulls k₁x₁; mass i keeps miω²xi of the force reaching it and passes the rest, Fi+1, to the next spring, which stretches by Fi+1/ki+1. At the free end nothing should be left. The force that is left, R(ω), is a polynomial in ω² whose roots are the natural frequencies; it changes sign at each one, so they can be found by bisection, mode by mode, with a pencil. Holzer's method was the standard for torsional drive trains (see the Torsional vibration page) before computers.
