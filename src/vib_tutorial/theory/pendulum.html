Turning instead of sliding

A body turning about a fixed axis is the single oscillator with every quantity rotated:

slidingturning
x (m)θ (rad)
m (kg)J (kg·m²), the moment of inertia about the axis
k (N/m)kθ (N·m/rad)
c (N·s/m)cθ (N·m·s/rad)
F (N)M (N·m), a moment

so

J\ddot{\theta} + c_\theta\dot{\theta} + k_\theta\theta = M \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k_\theta}{J}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c_\theta}{2\sqrt{k_\theta J}}

and every formula of the Single oscillator page holds with these in place of m, k and c. A moment of inertia is the mass weighted by the square of its distance from the axis: mL² for a bob on a string L long, mr²/2 for a disc of radius r about its own axis, mL²/12 for a uniform rod L long about its middle. About a pivot a distance d from the centre of mass G it is (parallel axes)

J = J_G + md^2 = m\left(k_G^2 + d^2\right)

where kG = √(JG/m) is the radius of gyration: the distance from G at which the whole mass would have the same JG (L/√12 for the rod).

Gravity as a spring

A pendulum has no spring: its weight does the job. Turned by θ, the weight mg acts at G, a horizontal distance d sin θ from the pivot O:

Taking moments about O, the pin's force passes through it and drops out, so

J\ddot{\theta} = -mgd\sin\theta

with J about O. That holds because O doesn't move: about a point that moves, the moment of the inertia force has to be added too, as Deriving the equations, later, shows. Energy gives the same equation without the pin's force: the swing keeps ½Jθ̇² + mgd(1 − cos θ) constant, and differentiating that by time gives it back.

For small swings sin θ ≈ θ, so the weight is a spring kθ = mgd, and

\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{mgd}{J}}

and for a simple pendulum, a bob on a string (J = mL², d = L),

\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}

The mass cancels for a simple pendulum, so its period depends only on L and g. A body of any shape swings as a simple pendulum of the equivalent length

L_\mathrm{eq} = \frac{J}{md} = d + \frac{k_G^2}{d}

For a uniform rod, J = m(L²/12 + d²), so the period is least when d equals the radius of gyration L/√12, and each longer period is reached at two distances from G, d and kG²/d. Pivot it on the other side of G at the second, and the two pivots are Leq apart:

That is Kater's reversible pendulum kater: a bar with two knife edges, adjusted until it swings with the same period on either one. Then Leq is the distance between the edges, which can be measured far more precisely than the centre of mass can be found, and g = 4π²Leq/T². It measured gravity for a century. The second pivot is also the centre of percussion of the first: a blow there, square to the line from the pivot, leaves no jolt at the pivot (a bat's sweet spot).

The page's other two kinds turn on a torsion spring kt: a disc of radius r on a wire, and a mass on an upright arm with the spring at its foot:

Upside down, the weight pushes the body over, so it takes away from the spring:

k_\theta = k_t - mgd

If the spring is weaker than mgd it is negative: upright is unstable, and the body falls to a leaning balance, where ktθ = mgd sin θ, as a column buckles. Gravity (and any steady load) changes stiffness: this is the geometric stiffness a tension or compression adds to a string, a cable or a column. A disc on a wire has no gravity in its stiffness at all: kθ = kt, the wire's twist.

Measuring a moment of inertia

The period turns the formula round: swing a part on a pivot and time it, and its moment of inertia follows. From T0 = 2π√(J/mgd), with the mass and d measured,

J_G = J - md^2 = md\left(\frac{gT_0^2}{4\pi^2} - d\right)

A 1 kg rod 1 m long, hung from its end (d = 0.5 m), swings in 1.638 s: J = 1 × 9.81 × 0.5 × 1.638²/4π² = 0.3334 kg·m², and JG = 0.3334 − 0.25 = 0.0834 kg·m², the formula's mL²/12 = 0.0833 kg·m² to the rounding of T0. It is a difference, so errors grow: here J is four times JG, and 0.1 % in T0 (0.2 % in J) is 0.8 % in JG. A part with no handy pivot hangs on a wire instead, a torsion pendulum: timed with it and then with a disc of known J on the same wire, J = Jdisc(T/Tdisc)², since kt is the same. Large parts (an engine, a car body) hang on three wires, the trifilar pendulum, whose twist the weight resists as gravity resists a swing.

Large swings

sin θ grows more slowly than θ, so a hanging pendulum's spring softens as its swing grows, and its period lengthens. Energy gives it exactly. With V(θ) the potential energy, the swing between ±A takes

T = 4\int_0^A \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{2\left(V(A) - V(\theta)\right)/J}}

For the simple pendulum, with K the complete elliptic integral of the first kind,

T = T_0\,\frac{2}{\pi}\,K\!\left(\sin^2\frac{A}{2}\right) T \approx T_0\left(1 + \frac{A^2}{16} + \frac{11A^4}{3072}\right)

1.7 % longer than T0 at 30°, 18 % at 90°, and without bound as A → 180°. Keeping one more term of the sine, sin θ ≈ θ − θ³/6, makes it a Duffing oscillator duffing, a spring with a cubic term kθθ + k3θ³, here with k3 = −mgd/6. Its first harmonic balance (assume θ = A cos ωt and keep the cos ωt terms, since cos³ = ¾cos + ¼cos 3) gives

\omega^2 \approx \omega_n^2\left(1 + \frac{3k_3A^2}{4k_\theta}\right), \qquad T \approx T_0\left(1 + \frac{A^2}{16}\right)

good to 1 % in the period up to about 75°. The inverted pendulum's cubic has the other sign, so it hardens. A torsion wire is linear, so a disc on one keeps its period at any twist.

In the phase plane (θ, θ̇) the undamped motion follows the curves of constant energy. Small orbits are ellipses; larger ones flatten at the ends; the separatrix through the upside-down balance divides swinging from going round and round, and an orbit near it lingers at the top.

A worked example: the page as it opens

The page opens on a simple pendulum: a 1 kg bob on a 1 m string, g = 9.81 m/s², no damping, let go from 10°. Each step below can be checked against the readouts.

  1. Inertia and stiffness. J = mL² = 1 kg·m², and gravity's spring is kθ = mgL = 9.81 N·m/rad (the line under the controls).
  2. Natural frequency. ωn = √(9.81/1) = 3.132 rad/s, so fn = 3.132/2π = 0.4985 Hz and T0 = 2π/3.132 = 2.006 s.
  3. How far from linear. 10° is A = 0.1745 rad. The weight's moment there is 9.81 sin 10° = 1.703 N·m, against the linear spring's 9.81 × 0.1745 = 1.712 N·m: 0.5 % less, so on the moment plot the curve still lies on its dashed tangent.
  4. The period. A²/16 = 0.0019, so the swing is 0.19 % slower: T ≈ 2.006 × 1.0019 = 2.010 s. The readout under the plots gives the exact period, 2.01 s, +0.191 %, and Duffing's estimate, the same to three figures. Over the eight swings drawn the whole swing falls 0.03 s behind the linearized one, too little to see.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the pendulum and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • Turning is sliding with J for m, kθ for k and moments for forces; about a pivot, J = JG + md².
  • A weight a distance d below the pivot is a spring kθ = mgd; above it, a negative one.
  • A simple pendulum's period, 2π√(L/g), doesn't depend on its mass; a rod's is least pivoted at its radius of gyration, and timing a swing measures J.
  • Large swings soften a hanging pendulum (a Duffing oscillator with a negative cubic) and harden an inverted one.
