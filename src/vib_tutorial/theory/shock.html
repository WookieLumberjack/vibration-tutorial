The shock response spectrum

A shock is a short, strong acceleration a(t) of a structure's base: a drop, a collision, a stage separation. Its time history alone says little about what it does. What matters is what it does to the things mounted on the structure, and they are, to a first approximation, single-degree-of-freedom oscillators: a mass on a spring and damper, natural frequency f (ω = 2πf), damping ζ. The shock response spectrum (SRS) is the largest absolute acceleration of such an oscillator on that base, against f, all at one Q = 1/2ζ (10 by custom). Biot biot drew the first such spectra, for earthquakes. A component whose natural frequency is f sees that peak; its spring carries that peak times its mass.

In the coordinate z relative to the base,

\ddot z + 2\zeta\omega\,\dot z + \omega^2 z = -a(t)

and the oscillator's absolute acceleration is ẍ = −2ζωż − ω2z.

Three regions

  • Stiff oscillators (fT ≫ 1, T the pulse's length) follow the pulse: the spectrum tends to the pulse's peak A. A pulse that jumps (a square, an initial-peak sawtooth) makes them overshoot, to nearly twice it.
  • Near fT ≈ 1 the oscillator amplifies: 1.65 × at Q = 10 for a half-sine.
  • Soft oscillators (fT ≪ 1) barely move while the pulse lasts. They take it as an impulse, a change of velocity Δv = ∫a dt: afterwards the mass is still where it was and the base moves away at Δv, so z starts at 0 with ż = −Δv and rings with amplitude Δv/ω, an acceleration of ω2 × Δv/ω = ωΔv. The spectrum rises as 2πfΔv. Only the pulse's area matters there, not its shape. A transient with no net Δv is far lower there: only the damper's push, 2ζω times the base's velocity, reaches a soft oscillator.

Primary, residual, positive, negative

The primary spectrum is the largest response while the pulse lasts, the residual one the free decay after it (soft oscillators peak there); each has a positive and a negative peak. The maximax is the largest of the four. Pulses with the same maximax can load a part very differently one way and the other: a terminal-peak sawtooth's drop rings stiff oscillators backwards, so its negative spectrum is nearly its positive one, which is why tests use it.

Damping

The primary peak barely depends on Q: the shock is over before the damping has acted. That is unlike a steady sine, whose resonant response is Q times larger. The residual decays with ζ, but its first peak, which the spectrum counts, hardly does.

The chain's peak from the spectrum

The SRS was meant for more than single oscillators. On the shaken base each of the chain's modes is an oscillator at its own frequency and damping, driven by Γr = φrTM1 times the base's acceleration, so the chain's absolute acceleration at mass j is Σ φjrΓr times each oscillator's, and its peak term is φjrΓr times the SRS at that mode. The spectrum loses when each peak comes, so the terms are combined by a rule. ABS, their sum, never falls short; SRSS, the root of the sum of squares, assumes the peaks come at unrelated times, as in a long random record, and a shock's don't: it sets every mode off at once, and they peak early, nearly together, so the SRSS falls short. NRL (the Naval Research Laboratory's rule ohara-belsheim, used for shipboard shock) adds the largest term in full to the SRSS of the rest. The Chain tab sets them against the exact response, mass by mass and spring by spring. Γr2 is the mode's effective mass (the modes' add up to the total mass): the modes that carry most of it carry the shock.

Four-way paper

The relative displacement's peak |z|max, times ω, is the pseudo-velocity PV, and times ω2 the pseudo-acceleration PA, close to the SRS when damping is light. Plotted log–log against f, PV = 2πf|z|max = PA/2πf: lines of constant |z|max rise at 45° and lines of constant PA fall at 45°, so one curve reads all three. A shock's pseudo-velocity is flat at Δv at the left (a soft oscillator stays put while the base moves away, then rings at that speed) and falls along the peak acceleration's line at the right.

Synthesis

Tests are often specified by an SRS (with a tolerance, ±3 dB here), not a time history, and a shaker or shock machine needs one that meets it. It is built from short components at frequencies across the band, their amplitudes scaled round by round by the target over the SRS achieved. Damped sines are simple but each leaves a net velocity, so the record ends moving and the table would drift away. Wavelets, sin(ωt/Nw) sin ωt over Nw half-cycles, Nw odd, start and end at rest in velocity and displacement, so a shaker can play them. The SRS is matched only at the components' frequencies; between them it strays a little, and the same record at another Q no longer matches.

Measured shocks

A component mounted on a structure sees the structure's response, not the base's pulse: here a mass on the chain, ringing at the chain's modes. Its spectrum, an in-structure shock spectrum, peaks at those modes. A pyrotechnic shock is a burst of high-frequency ringing with almost no net velocity change, and its spectrum falls steeply below its frequencies. An accelerometer's zero shift, a small offset after the shock, integrates into a large false Δv and lifts the low end: a measured spectrum is checked for it by its slope there.

How this page computes it

Each oscillator is stepped by the same exact update as the chain (the input linear between samples), and its peaks are refined between samples. The residual's largest value lies within one damped period of the pulse's end, so that is all the free decay that is followed.

Test specifications

A shock test is specified one of two ways lalanne-2:

  • A classical pulse: its shape (half-sine, terminal-peak sawtooth, trapezoid), peak and length, with a tolerance band round the time history, given by a drop table's programmer or a shaker. On a shaker the pulse needs compensation: a half-sine leaves the table moving at Δv, so small pre- and post-pulses of the opposite sign are added to bring its velocity and displacement back to zero. They take away the pulse's area, so the spectrum's low end, where only Δv counts, falls well below the uncompensated pulse's.
  • An SRS: measured in-structure spectra (several shots, several places) are enveloped by straight lines on log–log axes, usually a ramp in dB per octave up to a knee and flat above it, at Q = 10, with a margin added for qualification. The test passes if the achieved SRS lies within a tolerance band round the specification (a few dB either way) over the band, positive and negative both. Any record that meets it will do: damped sines or wavelets on a shaker, or a resonant fixture struck by a projectile for a pyroshock.

The SRS rates a shock by its peaks only. Two records with the same SRS can differ in their velocity, their length, how many cycles they put through a part, and so in the fatigue damage they do; and the SRS at Q = 10 says nothing exact about a part whose own damping is far from it. A specification therefore also bounds the record's length, and is applied along each axis, both ways.

Worked example

The page opens on a 10 g half-sine, 100 ms long (A = 98.1 m/s², T = 0.1 s), at Q = 10. Its area, the base's velocity change, is

\Delta v = \int_0^T A\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{2AT}{\pi} = 6.24\ \text{m/s}
  • At the left, f = 0.2 Hz (fT = 0.02): the impulse estimate is 2π × 0.2 × 6.24 = 7.84 m/s² = 0.80 g, and the spectrum is 0.74 g, already a little under it.
  • Its peak, 16.5 g, is 1.65 × the pulse's, at fT = 0.825, f = 8.25 Hz: a period a little longer than the pulse.
  • At the right, f = 500 Hz (fT = 50): 10.0 g, the pulse's own peak.

The oscillator at 0.2 Hz would stretch Δv/ω = 6.24/(2π × 0.2) = 4.97 m undamped; its damping holds it to the four-way paper's 4.6 m. A soft mount needs room as well as softness.

  • The SRS is the peak response of a bank of single oscillators on the shaken base, at one Q: it rates a shock by what it does, not by its time history.
  • Stiff oscillators follow the pulse, soft ones feel only its velocity change, and those near fT ≈ 1 amplify it, barely more with less damping.
  • On a chain each mode is one of the bank's oscillators, scaled by φΓ; the modes' peaks come nearly together, so ABS or NRL, not SRSS, bound the response.
