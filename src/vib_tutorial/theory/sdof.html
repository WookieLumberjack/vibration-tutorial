The keys:

Who it's for, and what it assumes

The course is written for engineering students from about the third year of a degree, and for engineers who want a refresher or a place to try things out. It assumes calculus and linear differential equations, complex numbers (eiΩt, and a response's size and phase as one complex number), and matrices with their eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The rest comes later, on the page that first needs it: the Laplace transform on Transient response, Fourier series on Periodic forcing, Lagrange's equations on Deriving the equations, the statistics of random signals on Random vibration; having met them before helps.

Some pages quote design criteria from standards (API, ISO, ASTM, the Eurocodes), with the edition they come from. They are there to show how such a criterion works, not to design to: the figures are for teaching, and a design should go to the current standard itself.

  • measure the damping ratio from how fast a free vibration dies away;
  • see why critical damping is the quickest return without overshooting;
  • find how far resonance amplifies a force, and what limits it;
  • match four damping laws by the energy they take out each cycle;
  • tell friction from viscous damping by how a free vibration dies away;
  • see where a rubber mount's damping peaks, and why.

    One mass on a spring

    Every vibration on these pages is built from one oscillator: a mass m on a spring k and a damper c, pushed by a force f(t), with x measured from where it rests:

    m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = f(t)

    The spring pulls back in proportion to the stretch, the damper in proportion to the velocity, and whatever is left over accelerates the mass. Three numbers describe it, whatever m, k and c are:

    • the natural frequency ωn = √(k/m), fn = ωn/2π: how fast it swings when nothing damps it. A stiffer spring is faster, a heavier mass slower;
    • the damping ratio ζ = c/cc, the damper against the critical damping cc = 2√(km) = 2mωn;
    • the static compliance 1/k, how far a steady force pushes it.

    Dividing by m gives the form every later page uses:

    \ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \frac{f}{m}

    Handling it: changes while it runs

    The mass on this page is worked out as it runs, a step at a time (exactly, as Numerical integration later explains), not drawn from a formula. So you can change it while it moves, and each change is a physical event, decided by what really happens, not by what would look smooth:

    • A stiffer or softer spring (its handle, or the box): the mass is where it was, moving as it was. Only the spring's stored energy jumps, by ½Δk x²: the work of whoever changed it at that stretch. Changed at the middle of a swing it costs nothing; at the end, the most.
    • More or less damping: nothing jumps. Only the rate the energy drains away changes.
    • A heavier mass: the extra is a lump at rest, caught and carried on (a plastic capture). It brings no momentum, so the momentum mẋ is kept and the mass slows,
    \dot{x}' = \frac{m\,\dot{x}}{m + \Delta m}, \qquad \text{lost in the capture: } \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2\,\frac{\Delta m}{m + \Delta m}
    • A lighter mass: the piece leaves at the mass's speed, so the rest carries on at ẋ, and the piece takes its own kinetic energy, ½Δm ẋ², with it.

    The time plots keep what came before a change, with a dashed line where it happened, and the Energy tab keeps the books: what the drive and your hand put in, what the damper took out, and each change's own term, so that at every instant

    T + V = W_\mathrm{in} - W_\mathrm{damper} + \text{the changes' terms}

    with T = ½mẋ² and V = ½kx². It closes to rounding through every change: a change that seems to make energy from nothing (a softer spring at full stretch) was paid for by whoever made it.

    Where m, k and c come from

    The model is three numbers, so the first job with a real part is to find them. The mass is whatever moves with the point that swings: weighed, or worked out from the drawing. The stiffness is the force per unit deflection at that point, and for simple parts it has a formula:

    • a bar stretched along its length: k = EA/L;
    • a cantilever pushed at its tip: k = 3EI/L³;
    • a beam on two simple supports, pushed at mid-span: k = 48EI/L³,

    with E the material's Young's modulus (about 200 GPa for steel, 70 GPa for aluminum), A the cross-section's area, I its second moment of area (bh³/12 for a rectangle b wide and h deep in the direction it bends) and L the length. Parts joined together add up in one of two ways:

    k_\mathrm{parallel} = k_1 + k_2, \qquad \frac{1}{k_\mathrm{series}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}

    Side by side, both springs deflect as much as the mass moves and their forces add, so the pair is stiffer than either. End to end, one force goes through both and their stretches add, so the pair is softer than the softer one: a stiff shaft on soft bearings is only as stiff as the bearings let it be. Two of the page's 400 N/m springs give 800 N/m side by side and fn = 4.50 Hz, or 200 N/m end to end and 2.25 Hz: a factor of √2 either way from 3.18 Hz.

    Gravity drops out. Hung on its spring, the mass sags by mg/k, to where the spring holds its weight. Measured from there, the spring's extra pull is kx and the weight is balanced, so the equation is the same as without gravity. Only the sag is left, and Vibration isolation, later in this group, turns it into a way to find fn.

    The damping rarely has a formula. It comes from the joints, the material and the air, so it is measured rather than worked out: the decay and the half-power width, below, are the two usual ways. As a guide, ζ is about 0.5–1 % for a welded steel structure, 2–5 % for a bolted one or a building, 5–10 % for rubber mounts, and 20–40 % for a car's suspension, which is there to stop the body bouncing.

    Free vibration

    Pull the mass to x0 and let go (drag it on the page: that's a pluck). Below critical damping (ζ < 1) it swings at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²), inside an envelope that shrinks exponentially:

    x(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(x_0\cos\omega_d t + \frac{v_0 + \zeta\omega_n x_0}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right)

    The envelope falls by a factor of e in the time constant τ = 1/ζωn. For the 0.5 % to 5 % damping of most structures ωd is within 0.13 % of ωn: damping shortens the ringing, hardly changes its pitch.

    Under, critical and over

    Pluck it with c = 4, 40 and 80 N·s/m (ζ = 0.1, 1 and 2), holding the curves (H) between, to see the three cases together:

    • Underdamped (ζ < 1): it overshoots and rings down.
    • Critically damped (ζ = 1): it returns as x(t) = \bigl(x_0 + (v_0 + \omega_n x_0)\,t\bigr)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\omega_n t} Released from rest it returns without crossing zero, and faster than any heavier damping does. Door closers and instrument needles are made near it.
    • Overdamped (ζ > 1): two plain decays, es1t and es2t. The slow one, s1 = −ωn(ζ − √(ζ² − 1)), gets slower as the damping grows: a mass in treacle creeps back.

    Measuring damping from a decay

    Successive peaks of a free decay, one damped period apart, keep the same ratio. Its logarithm is the logarithmic decrement

    \delta = \ln\frac{x_k}{x_{k+1}} = \frac{2\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \approx 2\pi\zeta

    So ζ ≈ δ/2π. Over n cycles, δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn), which averages out the noise of a real record. The readout under the time plot does exactly this on the peaks since the last pluck or change, marked as they come. The phase plane shows it too: each turn of the spiral is eδ times smaller than the last.

    The poles

    Try x = est in the free equation: ms² + cs + k = 0, so

    s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm i\omega_d \qquad (\zeta < 1) s = -\omega_n\left(\zeta \mp \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\right) \qquad (\zeta > 1)

    These two poles say everything about the free motion: the real part is the decay rate, the imaginary part the frequency. Below critical they sit on a circle of radius ωn at an angle sin⁻¹ζ from the imaginary axis; at ζ = 1 they meet on the real axis and then split along it. Every mode of every later page is such a pair (the state-space method's eigenvalues λ are these s).

    Forced response

    Under f = F cos Ωt, once the start has died away, the mass swings at the drive's frequency with x = Re(X eiΩt), and X = H(Ω)F with the receptance

    H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\Omega c} = \frac{1/k}{1 - r^2 + 2i\zeta r}, \qquad r = \frac{\Omega}{\omega_n}

    Its size is the amplitude per newton, and its angle the lag φ of the motion behind the force:

    |X| = \frac{F/k}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2}
    • Well below resonance the spring carries the force: X ≈ F/k, in phase.
    • Well above it the mass does: X ≈ −F/mΩ², 180° behind, smaller the faster.
    • At resonance (r = 1) the spring's force and the mass's cancel and only the damper resists: |X| = (F/k)/2ζ, lagging by exactly 90°. The free-body diagram beside the mass shows it: the damper's arrow alone balances the drive's.

    The ratio |X|k/F is the dynamic amplification; at resonance it is the quality factor Q = 1/2ζ. The displacement's own peak is a little lower, at r = √(1 − 2ζ²), and there is none at all for ζ ≥ 1/√2.

    Measuring the response, point by point

    The Frequency response tab starts empty: its points are measured from the run, the way a stepped-sine test measures a structure. Drive the mass and wait. Once the start has died away, the swing is steady, and a point lands at the drive's frequency: the response's amplitude and lag over the force's, each found as the one Fourier coefficient at that frequency over the last three periods, and counted steady when the three before agree with it to 0.5 %. Change the frequency and wait again, or press Sweep, which steps through frequencies across the resonance and dwells at each until its point lands. A swing still growing, or beating, never lands one. Points from before a change of m, k or c stay, faded: the curve they belonged to has moved. Show the theory draws the curve the points lie on.

    The half-power bandwidth

    The power a damper takes is ½cΩ²|X|², and across a narrow peak, where Ω hardly changes, that goes as |X|²: so the points where |X| falls to 1/√2 of its peak are the half-power points. They lie at r² = 1 − 2ζ² ± 2ζ√(1 − ζ²), about 2ζfn apart, so

    \zeta \approx \frac{f_2 - f_1}{2f_n} = \frac{1}{2Q}

    A lightly damped peak is tall and narrow. This is how damping is read off a measured frequency response, and why a test needs fine frequency lines to see it.

    Switching on: transients and beats

    From rest, the steady response alone would start with the wrong position and velocity, so a free vibration at ωd is added to make up the difference. It dies away in a few time constants and leaves the steady state. Undamped, it never dies: driven near resonance the two swings drift in and out of step, and the motion beats at the difference frequency |f − fn|. Exactly at resonance there is no steady state:

    x(t) = \frac{F}{2m\omega_n}\,t\sin\omega_n t

    grows without bound, and only damping (or the spring breaking) stops it.

    A rotating unbalance

    A machine of mass m whose rotor has an unbalance me at radius e feels the force meeΩ² cos Ωt, up and down on its mount (and as much sideways, which the mount holds):

    That is the forced response with F = meeΩ², a force that grows with speed, so

    \frac{m|X|}{m_e e} = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}

    It is 0 at rest, 1/2ζ at resonance, and tends to 1 far above: the machine swings by mee/m, in anti-phase with the unbalance, so that the two turn round their shared center of mass. Fans, pumps and washing machines start up through this peak (the Vibration isolation page, later in this group, runs one).

    The Nyquist plot

    Drawing H as a point in the complex plane at each frequency (Im H against Re H, the Frequency response tab's lowest plot) turns the resonance into nearly a circle of diameter 1/cωn, which crosses the imaginary axis at fn, where the points race round fastest. (The mobility, iΩH, makes an exact circle.) Modal testing fits circles like this to find each mode's frequency and damping.

    The phase plane

    The state of the oscillator is two numbers, position and velocity. Plotting ẋ/ωn against x turns undamped motion into a circle (its energy, ½kx² + ½mẋ², is constant) and damped motion into a spiral, losing a fixed fraction of its radius each turn. Overdamped, the path runs straight into the origin along the slow root's direction.

    Other damping: where the energy goes

    The viscous damper, a force proportional to velocity, is a model chosen because it makes the equations linear and easy. Real structures lose energy in other ways: internal friction in the material, rubbing in joints and bolted connections, the shearing of rubber and polymers, the air around them. Each has its own force law, and they differ in how the loss depends on the amplitude and on the frequency. The Damper box under the oscillator's gives this mass and spring four of them, each matched so that at fn it takes the same energy per cycle as the viscous damper c (its ζ), and runs the one you pick live, beside a viscous ghost (gray) plucked and driven as it is. Pick another law as it runs, and the motion carries on with the new damping, from where it is.

    The hysteresis loop

    Cycle the oscillator's spring and damper through x = X sin Ωt and plot the force they carry against x (the Loop & loss tab draws the run's own last cycle, and every model's beside it). A spring alone traces the line kx out and back. Anything that loses energy traces a loop, because the force is larger going one way than coming back, and the area inside it is the energy lost per cycle:

    W_d = \oint F\,\mathrm{d}x

    A material test machine draws exactly this: the loop's tilt is the stiffness, its width the damping. The peak energy the spring stores in the cycle is U = ½kX², and the ratio of the two is what the rest of the page compares.

    Viscous damping

    F = cẋ. Over a harmonic cycle the damping force is cΩX cos Ωt, a quarter cycle ahead of x, so the loop is an ellipse and

    W_d = \pi c\,\Omega X^2

    The loss grows in proportion to the frequency. Written as a complex stiffness, the force is K*X with

    K^* = K' + iK'' = k + i\Omega c

    The real part K′ is the storage stiffness, in phase with the motion; the imaginary part K″ the loss stiffness, a quarter cycle ahead. For any of the models here the loss per cycle is Wd = πK″X².

    Hysteretic (structural) damping

    Tests on most metals, and on built-up structures, find a loss per cycle that hardly depends on frequency. The model for it is a complex stiffness that doesn't depend on frequency either:

    K^* = k(1 + i\eta), \qquad W_d = \pi\eta k X^2

    η is the loss factor. At resonance it gives the same peak as a viscous damper with η = 2ζ, so the two are often used interchangeably (as the matching here does), but away from resonance they differ: the hysteretic loss is the same at every frequency. Its frequency response,

    H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\eta k}

    peaks exactly at fn, at 1/ηk; the viscous one peaks a little lower, at fn√(1 − 2ζ²), and a little higher, 1/(2ζk√(1 − ζ²)). It works for steady harmonic motion (and in frequency-domain analysis, where it is standard: the Rigid body on mounts page, later, puts it in its mounts), but it has no honest time-domain form: for a real force to give a real response, K″ must change sign with the frequency, k(1 + iη sgn Ω), and that stiffness makes the mass respond before it is struck (crandall). So in time the page runs its stand-in: a viscous damper with the same loss at the frequency it runs at, c = ηk/Ω, the drive's while it drives (a change of the drive's frequency changes it, live) and fn's when it rings free. Swept, its points land on the hysteretic curve, as it is matched at each frequency in turn; what it can't do is respond to two frequencies at once with two losses.

    Coulomb friction

    A dry contact pushes back with a force of fixed size against the sliding, Ff sgn ẋ, the law Coulomb measured (coulomb): the mass below slides on its floor, and the floor pushes it back against its velocity, whatever its speed.

    The loop is a rectangle (with the spring, a parallelogram) and

    W_d = 4F_f X

    proportional to X, not X². Matching it to a viscous damper gives the equivalent viscous damping

    c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{4F_f}{\pi\Omega X}

    so friction is heavy damping for small motions and light for large ones. Released, each half cycle is a swing about the point Ff/k off center on the side friction pushes to, so the swing decays in a straight line, losing 4Ff/k of amplitude a cycle, and stops dead at the first turn within Ff/k of the center, where the spring can no longer beat the friction (later, the Simulation page's friction steps it exactly). Under a harmonic force F it can't limit a resonance at all once F > 4Ff/π: the force then puts in πFX per cycle at resonance, more than the 4FfX friction takes out, at every amplitude. Den Hartog's exact solution of the sliding motion has the same threshold (den-hartog-1931).

    Viscoelastic materials: the Zener element

    Rubber, polymers and damping layers store and lose energy in a way that depends strongly on frequency. The simplest model that captures it is the standard linear solid (zener): a spring k0 in parallel with a spring k1 and a dashpot c1 in series, the series pair being a Maxwell arm (maxwell).

    With the relaxation time τr = c1/k1,

    K^*(\Omega) = k_0 + k_1\,\frac{i\Omega\tau_r}{1 + i\Omega\tau_r}
    • Slowly (Ωτr ≪ 1) the dashpot gives way and only k0 carries load: the relaxed stiffness.
    • Fast (Ωτr ≫ 1) the dashpot locks: k0 + k1, the unrelaxed stiffness.
    • In between the stiffness climbs and the loss factor peaks, at Ωτr = 1/√(1 + k1/k0), where it is
    \eta_{\max} = \frac{k_1}{2\sqrt{k_0(k_0 + k_1)}}

    Far below its peak the Zener element acts like a viscous damper (η ∝ Ω), far above it like a stiffer spring with little loss. Real materials have many such arms with a spread of τr, and temperature shifts them all; designers put a damping material's loss peak where the structure's modes are (nashif).

    The loss factor, compared

    For any of them the loss factor

    \eta = \frac{K''}{K'} = \frac{W_d}{2\pi U}, \qquad U = \tfrac{1}{2}K'X^2

    measures damping without saying where it comes from; at resonance η = 2ζ = 1/Q.

    • Viscous: η = cΩ/k, rising with frequency.
    • Hysteretic: constant.
    • Friction: 4Ff/πkX, constant in frequency but falling with amplitude.
    • Zener: a peak.

    Equivalent viscous damping

    Whatever the force law, a loss of Wd a cycle at amplitude X and frequency Ω takes as much as the viscous damper

    c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{W_d}{\pi\Omega X^2}

    which is how a nonlinear damper is put into a linear analysis, one amplitude at a time: the friction's ceq above is this with Wd = 4FfX. A body moving fast through air or water feels a drag that grows as the velocity squared, F = aẋ|ẋ|; over a cycle it takes

    W_d = \tfrac{8}{3}\,a\,\Omega^2 X^3, \qquad c_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{8a\Omega X}{3\pi}

    the opposite of friction: light damping for small motions, heavy for large ones (thomson). Each law's ceq holds only at the amplitude it was worked out for; the matching on this page holds friction's at X.

    Measuring the loss factor

    Three measurements give the same number for light damping, whatever the mechanism:

    • The loop: its area over 2π times the peak stored energy, η = Wd/2πU.
    • The decay: the logarithmic decrement is δ ≈ 2πζ, so η ≈ δ/π (friction's decay, a straight line, has no single δ: that itself says the loss isn't viscous).
    • The bandwidth: the half-power points of the response are 2ζfn apart, so η ≈ (f2 − f1)/fn = 1/Q.

    A vibration losing Wd a cycle, Ω/2π cycles a second, loses on average the power P = ΩηU: its loss factor times its frequency times its energy. The Vibro-acoustics & SEA page, later, balances whole structures' power this way.

    How the page runs the other laws

    The mass is stepped as it runs, exactly for each law: the viscous damper and the hysteretic stand-in by the matrix exponential (Handling it, above); friction from one stick or slip to the next, each event found within its step; the Zener element with a third state, where its arm's spring meets its dashpot. Changing the law or c as it runs is a live change, by the same rules, and the Energy tab's books close through it. The run's loop is the force its spring and damping carry, f − mẍ, against x over its last cycle; the faint loops, the loss factors and the theory's frequency responses come from each model's complex stiffness (friction's true force for its loop, its describing function elsewhere: √(F² − (4Ff/π)²)/|k − Ω²m|). The response's points are measured from the run, steady swing by steady swing, as the viscous mass's are; the phasors are the driven steady state's.

    A worked example: the page as it opens

    The page opens on m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m, at rest, with its drive set to 1 N at 3 Hz and off. Each step below can be checked on the page: pluck it from 10 mm for the free decay, then switch the drive on.

    1. Natural frequency. ωn = √(400/1) = 20 rad/s, so fn = 20/2π = 3.18 Hz.
    2. Damping ratio. cc = 2√(400 × 1) = 40 N·s/m, so ζ = 2/40 = 0.05: 5 %.
    3. The free decay. ωd = 20√(1 − 0.05²) = 19.97 rad/s, only 0.13 % below ωn. The envelope's time constant is τ = 1/(0.05 × 20) = 1 s, and δ = 2π × 0.05/√(1 − 0.05²) = 0.315, so each peak is e0.315 = 1.37 times the next. The readout under the time plot measures the same from the peaks.
    4. Driven at 3 Hz. Ω = 2π × 3 = 18.85 rad/s and r = 18.85/20 = 0.942. Then 1 − r² = 0.1117 and 2ζr = 0.0942, so
    |X| = \frac{1/400}{\sqrt{0.1117^2 + 0.0942^2}} = \frac{2.5\ \mathrm{mm}}{0.1462} = 17.1\ \mathrm{mm} \varphi = \tan^{-1}\frac{0.0942}{0.1117} = 40.1^\circ

    That is 6.84 times the static 2.5 mm, lagging the force by 40.1°: once the swing is steady, the readout under the time plot says it swings ±17.1 mm, lagging by 40.1°, and the point lands on the Frequency response tab. At resonance, driven at 3.18 Hz, it would be (F/k)/2ζ = 2.5 mm/0.1 = 25 mm (Q = 10 times the static), lagging by 90°, with the half-power points 2ζfn = 0.32 Hz apart, at 3.01 and 3.33 Hz.

    A worked example: the other laws on the defaults

    On the page's defaults, m = 1 kg, k = 400 N/m and c = 2 N·s/m (ζ = 0.05), each law is matched to the damper at fn (friction at X = 10 mm).

    1. The oscillator. ωn = √(k/m) = 20 rad/s, fn = 3.18 Hz, and the viscous damper is c = 2ζ√(km) = 2 N·s/m. The peak stored energy is U = ½kX² = ½ × 400 × 0.01² = 20 mJ.
    2. The loss to match. η = 2ζ = 0.1, so each model takes Wd = 2πηU = 12.57 mJ a cycle at fn; the viscous damper's πcωnX² = π × 2 × 20 × 0.01² is the same.
    3. Friction. 4FfX = 12.57 mJ gives Ff = 0.314 N. Released from 10 mm it loses 4Ff/k = 3.14 mm a cycle, 1.57 mm a half cycle: its turns are at 8.43, 6.86, 5.29, 3.72, 2.15 and 0.58 mm, the last inside Ff/k = 0.785 mm, so it sticks after three cycles, 0.58 mm off center (575 µm, carried exactly, as the readout under the time plot gives it). The viscous swing instead keeps e−δ = 0.730 of itself a cycle, with δ = 2πζ/√(1 − ζ²) = 0.315.
    4. Driven by 1 N. The hysteretic peak is (F/k)/η = 25.0 mm, the viscous one 25.03 mm. Friction's threshold is 4Ff/π = 0.4 N, so 1 N runs it away.
    5. The Zener element. With its loss peak at fn it must peak at η = 0.1, so k1/k0 = 0.221 (from ηmax above). Its storage stiffness at fn must be k, which sets k0 = 363.8 N/m and k1 = 80.4 N/m, and Ωτr = 1/√1.221 at 20 rad/s gives τr = 45.2 ms.

    These are the numbers the readouts give: under the Oscillator box, with each law picked (Ff = 314 mN, τr = 45.2 ms), under the loop (the viscous damper's 12.6 mJ, driven to 10 mm at fn by 0.4 N) and under the time plot (each peak 3.14 mm lower, stuck 575 µm off center).

    Things to try

    Each of these sets up the oscillator and the plots (Things to try, under the controls).

  • One mass on a spring and a damper is set by fn = √(k/m)/2π, ζ = c/2√(km) and 1/k.
  • Underdamped, it rings at ωd ≈ ωn inside an envelope e−ζωnt; critically damped it returns fastest without overshoot; overdamped it creeps.
  • The log decrement δ ≈ 2πζ and the half-power width Δf ≈ 2ζfn both measure damping.
  • Driven, it follows the force below resonance, opposes it above, and lags by 90° at resonance, where only damping limits it to Q = 1/2ζ times the static response.
  • A switched-on force adds a free vibration that dies away; undamped, it beats instead.
  • A change made while it runs is a physical event: a spring change costs ½Δk x², a captured mass keeps the momentum and loses energy, and the energy books close through each.
  • The area of the force–displacement loop is the energy lost per cycle; the loss factor η = K″/K′ = Wd/2πU measures it, and at resonance η = 2ζ.
  • Viscous damping loses energy in proportion to frequency, hysteretic damping not at all; they agree at resonance, which is why η = 2ζ swaps between them.
  • Hysteretic damping is a frequency-domain model: it has no causal free decay, and in time it runs as a stand-in, a viscous damper matched at the frequency it runs at.
  • Friction loses 4FfX per cycle: it decays in a straight line, stops off center, and can't bound a resonance driven harder than 4Ff/π.
  • A viscoelastic material stiffens across its relaxation frequency, where its loss peaks: viscous below, elastic above.
  • Any loss can be stood in for by ceq = Wd/πΩX², at one amplitude; the loop, the decay and the bandwidth all measure η.
