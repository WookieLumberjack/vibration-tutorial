The keys:

Who it's for, and what it assumes

The course is written for engineering students from about the third year of a degree, and for engineers who want a refresher or a place to try things out. It assumes calculus and linear differential equations, complex numbers (eiωt, and a response's size and phase as one complex number), and matrices with their eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The rest comes later, on the page that first needs it: the Laplace transform on Transient response, Fourier series on Periodic forcing, Lagrange's equations on Deriving the equations, the statistics of random signals on Random vibration; having met them before helps.

Some pages quote design criteria from standards (API, ISO, ASTM, the Eurocodes), with the edition they come from. They are there to show how such a criterion works, not to design to: the figures are for teaching, and a design should go to the current standard itself.

One mass on a spring

Every vibration on these pages is built from one oscillator: a mass m on a spring k and a damper c, pushed by a force f(t), with x measured from where it rests:

m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = f(t)

The spring pulls back in proportion to the stretch, the damper in proportion to the velocity, and whatever is left over accelerates the mass. Three numbers describe it, whatever m, k and c are:

  • the natural frequency ωn = √(k/m), fn = ωn/2π: how fast it swings when nothing damps it. A stiffer spring is faster, a heavier mass slower;
  • the damping ratio ζ = c/cc, the damper against the critical damping cc = 2√(km) = 2mωn;
  • the static compliance 1/k, how far a steady force pushes it.

Dividing by m gives the form every later page uses:

\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \frac{f}{m}

Free vibration

Pull the mass to x0 and let go. Below critical damping (ζ < 1) it swings at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²), inside an envelope that shrinks exponentially:

x(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(x_0\cos\omega_d t + \frac{v_0 + \zeta\omega_n x_0}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_d t\right)

The envelope falls by e in the time constant τ = 1/ζωn. For the 0.5 % to 5 % damping of most structures ωd is within 0.1 % of ωn: damping shortens the ringing, hardly changes its pitch.

Under, critical and over

Tick Compare ζ = 0.1, 1 and 2 to see the three cases together:

  • Underdamped (ζ < 1): it overshoots and rings down.
  • Critically damped (ζ = 1): it returns as x(t) = \bigl(x_0 + (v_0 + \omega_n x_0)\,t\bigr)\,e^{-\omega_n t} Released from rest it returns without crossing zero, and faster than any heavier damping does. Door closers and instrument needles are made near it.
  • Overdamped (ζ > 1): two plain decays, es1t and es2t. The slow one, s1 = −ωn(ζ − √(ζ² − 1)), gets slower as the damping grows: a mass in treacle creeps back.

Measuring damping from a decay

Successive peaks of a free decay, one damped period apart, keep the same ratio. Its logarithm is the logarithmic decrement

\delta = \ln\frac{x_k}{x_{k+1}} = \frac{2\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \approx 2\pi\zeta

So ζ ≈ δ/2π. Over n cycles, δ = (1/n) ln(x0/xn), which averages out the noise of a real record. The readout under the decay does exactly this on the curve's peaks. The phase plane shows it too: each turn of the spiral is eδ times smaller than the last.

The poles

Try x = est in the free equation: ms² + cs + k = 0, so

s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm i\omega_d \qquad (\zeta < 1) s = -\omega_n\left(\zeta \mp \sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}\right) \qquad (\zeta > 1)

These two poles say everything about the free motion: the real part is the decay rate, the imaginary part the frequency. Below critical they sit on a circle of radius ωn at an angle sin⁻¹ζ from the imaginary axis; at ζ = 1 they meet on the real axis and then split along it. Every mode of every later page is such a pair (the state-space method's eigenvalues λ are these s).

Forced response

Under f = F cos Ωt, once the start has died away, the mass swings at the drive's frequency with x = Re(X eiΩt), and X = H(Ω)F with the receptance

H(\Omega) = \frac{1}{k - \Omega^2 m + i\Omega c} = \frac{1/k}{1 - r^2 + 2i\zeta r}, \qquad r = \frac{\Omega}{\omega_n}
  • Well below resonance the spring carries the force: X ≈ F/k, in phase.
  • Well above it the mass does: X ≈ −F/mΩ², 180° behind, smaller the faster.
  • At resonance (r = 1) the spring's force and the mass's cancel and only the damper resists: |X| = (F/k)/2ζ, lagging by exactly 90°.

The ratio |X|k/F is the dynamic amplification; at resonance it is the quality factor Q = 1/2ζ. The displacement's own peak is a little lower, at r = √(1 − 2ζ²), and there is none at all for ζ ≥ 1/√2.

The half-power bandwidth

The power a damper takes is proportional to |X|², so the points where |X| falls to 1/√2 of its peak are the half-power points. They lie at r² = 1 − 2ζ² ± 2ζ√(1 − ζ²), about 2ζfn apart, so

\zeta \approx \frac{f_2 - f_1}{2f_n} = \frac{1}{2Q}

A lightly damped peak is tall and narrow. This is how damping is read off a measured frequency response, and why a test needs fine frequency lines to see it.

Switching on: transients and beats

From rest, the steady response alone would start with the wrong position and velocity, so a free vibration at ωd is added to make up the difference. It dies away in a few time constants and leaves the steady state. Undamped, it never dies: driven near resonance the two swings drift in and out of step, and the motion beats at the difference frequency |f − fn|. Exactly at resonance there is no steady state:

x(t) = \frac{F}{2m\omega_n}\,t\sin\omega_n t

grows without bound, and only damping (or the spring breaking) stops it.

A rotating unbalance

A machine of mass m whose rotor has an unbalance me at radius e feels the force meeΩ² cos Ωt. So

\frac{m|X|}{m_e e} = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}

It is 0 at rest, 1/2ζ at resonance, and tends to 1 far above: the machine swings by mee/m, in anti-phase with the unbalance, so that the two turn round their shared centre of mass. Fans, pumps and washing machines start up through this peak (the Vibration isolation page, later in this group, runs one).

The Nyquist plot

Drawing H as a point in the complex plane at each frequency (Im H against Re H) turns the resonance into nearly a circle of diameter 1/cωn, which crosses the imaginary axis at fn, where the points race round fastest. (The mobility, iΩH, makes an exact circle.) Modal testing fits circles like this to find each mode's frequency and damping.

The phase plane

The state of the oscillator is two numbers, position and velocity. Plotting ẋ/ωn against x turns undamped motion into a circle (its energy, ½kx² + ½mẋ², is constant) and damped motion into a spiral, losing a fixed fraction of its radius each turn. Overdamped, the path runs straight into the origin along the slow root's direction.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the oscillator and the plots (Things to try, top left).

  • One mass on a spring and a damper is set by fn = √(k/m)/2π, ζ = c/2√(km) and 1/k.
  • Underdamped, it rings at ωd ≈ ωn inside an envelope e−ζωnt; critically damped it returns fastest without overshoot; overdamped it creeps.
  • The log decrement δ ≈ 2πζ and the half-power width Δf ≈ 2ζfn both measure damping.
  • Driven, it follows the force below resonance, opposes it above, and lags by 90° at resonance, where only damping limits it to Q = 1/2ζ times the static response.
  • A switched-on force adds a free vibration that dies away; undamped, it beats instead.
