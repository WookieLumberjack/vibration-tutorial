Added mass

A body that accelerates in a fluid has to accelerate some of the fluid too: the fluid in front must get out of the way, the fluid behind must fill in, and it flows round the sides to do so. Treat the fluid as ideal (no viscosity) and incompressible, and the body's velocity U sets up a potential flow, u = ∇φ with ∇²φ = 0, whose speed everywhere is proportional to U. Its kinetic energy is then proportional to U² too, and written

Tfluid = ½ ma U²,

with ma the added mass. The force needed to accelerate the body is (m + ma) dU/dt: the fluid's pressure on the body, ρ ∂φ/∂t, pushes back in phase with its acceleration, exactly as more mass would. (In a steady flow an ideal fluid exerts no drag at all, d'Alembert's paradox; only acceleration costs force.) It is usually written as a coefficient on the fluid the body displaces, ma = CaρfV:

  • a sphere, φ = −UR³cos θ/2r²: Ca = ½, half the fluid it displaces;
  • a circular cylinder moving across its axis (per length), φ = −UR²cos θ/r: Ca = 1, all of it.

The cylinder's flow falls off as R²/r², the sphere's as R³/r³: a two-dimensional body stirs the fluid further out. Other shapes have their own coefficients (a flat plate moving broadside, a strip of width 2a, has ρπa² per length though it displaces nothing), and a body's added mass is different in each direction and for turning.

Wet and dry

The fluid adds mass but no stiffness, so a body on springs k vibrates in the fluid at

fwet = fdry √(m/(m + ma)) = fdry / √(1 + Caρf/ρs),

which depends only on the body's density against the fluid's. A steel body in water loses a few per cent; a light one (a float, a thin-walled tube, a composite panel) loses much more; anything in air loses next to nothing, unless it is very light for its size (a balloon, a membrane, a loudspeaker's cone). Added mass is also why a modal test in air doesn't give a ship's, or an offshore structure's, frequencies in water.

Confined fluid

Put a still wall round the body, a concentric pipe at Ro, and the fluid the body pushes has to squeeze through the gap to get round it. The flow is again in closed form, φ = cos θ (Ar + B/r) with ∂φ/∂r = U cos θ on the body and 0 at the wall, and its energy gives (Fritz, 1972)

Ca = (Ro² + R²) / (Ro² − R²),

1.67 with the pipe at twice the radius, 5.55 at 1.2 times, and without bound as the gap g closes, about R/g: the fluid in the gap moves about R/g times as fast as the body (all it pushes aside has to get through), and its energy goes as the square of that speed. A sphere in a spherical shell has (2/3)πρR³ (Ro³ + 2R³)/(Ro³ − R³). Tubes in a heat exchanger's baffles, fuel rods in their channels and pumps' wear rings are designed with it; close to a wall, the added mass can outweigh the structure.

A plate in water

A plate vibrating in a mode pushes fluid only where it moves, and a mode with several cells pushes and pulls side by side, so the fluid mostly sloshes from one cell to the next. For a baffled plate with fluid on one side, an incompressible fluid's pressure for a surface acceleration of wavenumber k is ρ a(k)/|k|, and each mode's added mass is

ma = (ρf/4π²) ∫∫ |Φ̂(k)|² / |k| d²k,

Φ̂ the mode shape's Fourier transform (the same transform as Wallace's integral for radiation, taken at no frequency at all: incompressible fluid is sound with c → ∞). The fluid it carries reaches about a cell's size from the plate, so higher modes carry less. Over the mode's own modal mass ρphab/4 it is the AVMI factor (added virtual mass incremental factor) β, and

fwet = fdry / √(1 + β), β ∝ (ρf/ρp)(a/h),

large for a thin plate: a 3 mm steel panel in water carries eight times its own modal mass. (Lamb's 1920 result for a clamped circular plate of radius a, β = 0.669 (ρf/ρp)(a/h), is the classic case.) Fluid on both sides doubles β. The fluid also couples modes of the same symmetry, which this page leaves out: the wet modes are then mixtures of the dry ones, and that shifts their frequencies a little more.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the body or the plate (Things to try, top left).

  • A body accelerating in a fluid moves fluid with it: its kinetic energy ½maU² makes the body behave as if its mass were m + ma.
  • In open fluid a sphere carries half the fluid it displaces, a cylinder all of it.
  • The fluid adds mass but no stiffness: fwet = fdry/√(1 + Caρf/ρs), set by the densities' ratio.
  • A wall close to the body forces the fluid through the gap, and the added mass grows without bound as the gap closes.
  • A plate's mode carries fluid out to about a cell's size, so its AVMI factor β falls with the mode's order, and grows with ρfa/ρph.
