Added mass

A body that accelerates in a fluid has to accelerate some of the fluid too: the fluid in front must get out of the way, the fluid behind must fill in, and it flows round the sides to do so. Treat the fluid as ideal (no viscosity) and incompressible, and the body's velocity U sets up a potential flow: the fluid's velocity is the gradient of a potential φ, which obeys Laplace's equation,

\mathbf{u} = \nabla\varphi, \qquad \nabla^2\varphi = 0,

with the fluid's velocity normal to the body equal to the body's. The equation is linear, so the flow's speed everywhere is proportional to U, its kinetic energy to U², and it is written

T_\mathrm{fluid} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m_a U^2,

with ma the added mass. Whatever force speeds the fluid up does work on it at the rate its energy grows, maU dU/dt, and it does it through the body; so the force needed to accelerate the body of mass ms is (ms + ma) dU/dt. The fluid's pressure pushes back in phase with the acceleration, exactly as more mass would. In a steady flow an ideal fluid exerts no drag at all, d'Alembert's paradox dalembert-1752: only acceleration costs force. The added mass is usually written as a coefficient on the fluid the body displaces,

m_a = C_a\,\rho_f V:
  • a sphere of radius R, φ = −UR³cos θ/2r² (r, θ measured from its centre and its line of motion): Ca = ½, half the fluid it displaces;
  • a circular cylinder moving across its axis (per length), φ = −UR²cos θ/r: Ca = 1, all of it.

The cylinder's flow falls off as R²/r², the sphere's as R³/r³: a two-dimensional body stirs the fluid further out. Other shapes have their own coefficients (a flat strip of width a moving broadside carries ρfπa²/4 per length, though it displaces nothing), and a body's added mass is different in each direction and for turning.

Wet and dry

The fluid adds mass but no stiffness, so a body on springs that vibrates at fdry in vacuum vibrates in the fluid at

f_\mathrm{wet} = f_\mathrm{dry}\sqrt{\frac{m_s}{m_s + m_a}} = \frac{f_\mathrm{dry}}{\sqrt{1 + C_a\rho_f/\rho_s}},

since ms = ρsV and ma = CaρfV share the volume: it depends only on the body's density against the fluid's. A steel body in water loses a few per cent; a light one (a float, a thin-walled tube, a composite panel) loses much more; anything in air loses next to nothing, unless it is very light for its size (a balloon, a membrane, a loudspeaker's cone). Added mass is also why a modal test in air doesn't give a ship's, or an offshore structure's, frequencies in water.

Confined fluid

Put a still wall round the body, a concentric pipe at Ro, and the fluid the body pushes has to squeeze through the gap to get round it. The flow is again in closed form, φ = cos θ (Ar + B/r) with ∂φ/∂r = U cos θ on the body and 0 at the wall, and its energy gives fritz

C_a = \frac{R_o^2 + R^2}{R_o^2 - R^2}:

1.67 with the pipe at twice the radius, 5.55 at 1.2 times, and without bound as the gap closes. With Ro = R + δ and δ small it is about R/δ: the fluid in the gap moves about R/δ times as fast as the body (all it pushes aside has to get through a slot δ wide), and its energy goes as the square of that speed, over a layer only δ thick. A sphere in a spherical shell has Ca = ½(Ro³ + 2R³)/(Ro³ − R³). Tubes in a heat exchanger's baffles, fuel rods in their channels and pumps' wear rings are designed with it; close to a wall, the added mass can outweigh the structure.

A plate in water

A plate vibrating in a mode pushes fluid only where it moves, and a mode with several cells pushes and pulls side by side, so the fluid mostly sloshes from one cell to the next and goes no further out than about a cell.

Put numbers on it for a baffled plate with fluid on one side. A pattern of surface acceleration that varies across the plate as eiκ·x (a wavenumber κ) sets up a flow that dies away as e−|κ|z from the plate, and a pressure ρf/|κ| times the acceleration on it: the fluid it carries is a layer 1/|κ| thick. Adding up the mode shape's parts, each mode's added mass is

m_a = \frac{\rho_f}{4\pi^2}\iint\frac{|\hat\Phi(\boldsymbol\kappa)|^2}{|\boldsymbol\kappa|}\,\mathrm{d}^2\kappa,

Φ̂ the mode shape's Fourier transform (the same transform as Wallace's integral wallace for radiation, taken at no frequency at all: incompressible fluid is sound that travels infinitely fast). Higher modes have smaller cells, so they carry less. Over the mode's own modal mass ρphab/4 it is the AVMI factor (added virtual mass incremental factor kwak) β, and

f_\mathrm{wet} = \frac{f_\mathrm{dry}}{\sqrt{1 + \beta}}, \qquad \beta \propto \frac{\rho_f}{\rho_p}\,\frac{a}{h}:

the fluid's layer, a cell's size and so a fraction of a deep, against the plate's thickness h. It is large for a thin plate: a 3 mm steel panel in water carries eight times its own modal mass. Lamb's result for a clamped circular plate of radius a lamb-1920, β = 0.669 (ρf/ρp)(a/h), is the classic case. Fluid on both sides doubles β. The fluid also couples modes of the same symmetry, which this page leaves out: the wet modes are then mixtures of the dry ones, and that shifts their frequencies a little more.

Sloshing

A liquid with a free surface has modes of its own, set by gravity: the surface tilts one way and the other, and the liquid flows from the high side to the low. Take a rectangular tank of length L (along the motion), filled to depth d, with x along it and z up from the still surface. The potential φ = cosh(κ(z + d)) cos κx obeys Laplace's equation and lets no liquid through the bottom. It lets none through the walls (∂φ/∂x = 0 at x = 0 and L) for

\kappa_n = n\pi/L,

n half waves along the tank. At the surface the pressure is atmospheric: the surface's height η rises at the liquid's upward speed, ∂η/∂t = ∂φ/∂z, and gravity pulls it back, ∂φ/∂t + gη = 0 (Bernoulli's equation, linearized). Together, at a frequency ωn, they ask ωn²φ = g ∂φ/∂z, which the cosh meets for

\omega_n^2 = g\,\kappa_n\tanh(\kappa_n d).
  • Shallow (d ≪ L): tanh κnd ≈ κnd, and ωn = κn√(gd): a long wave running at √(gd) from wall to wall and back, f1 = √(gd)/2L. Its flow is nearly the same at every depth.
  • Deep (d ≳ L/2): tanh κnd ≈ 1, and ωn = √(gκn), whatever the depth. The flow dies away as eκnz below the surface, within about L/nπ; below that the liquid stays still.

A sloshing tank's frequencies are low (a tank a metre long sloshes below 1 Hz, a ship's tank or a building's water tower at a fraction of a hertz) and its damping is slight, about 0.5 % for water in a bare tank. Fuel in a rocket's or an aircraft's tanks, liquid cargo in a ship or a road tanker, and a tank on a building in an earthquake are designed with it.

The spring–mass analogue

Shake the tank sideways and only the odd modes are excited: they tilt the surface, while the even ones raise it at both walls together, and a sideways push does no work on them. The force on the walls is exactly that of a spring–mass analogue graham-rodriquez: a mass m0 fixed to the tank, and for each odd mode a mass mn on a spring kn = mnωn².

With mL = ρfLBd the liquid's mass (B the tank's breadth),

m_n = m_L\,\frac{8\tanh(\kappa_n d)}{n^3\pi^3\,d/L}, \qquad m_0 = m_L - \sum m_n.

The masses fall as 1/n³, so the first mode, or the first two, is all a model needs. In shallow liquid almost all of it sloshes: tanh κnd ≈ nπd/L makes mn = 8mL/n²π², and those add up to mL (Σ 1/n² over odd n is π²/8). In deep liquid only the top, about 8mLL/π³d, sloshes and the rest is m0. The tank's force over its acceleration is mL far below the first mode (it all moves together), resonant at each odd mode, and m0 far above them all (the surface can't follow, and the liquid is then a body's added mass with its free surface held flat). Analogues for upright cylinders and other shapes are tabulated the same way abramson, with the masses' heights to give the moments too.

A tuned liquid damper

Put such a tank on a structure whose mode, of mass M and stiffness k, sways too much at fs, and its first sloshing mode is a tuned mass damper of mass m1: tall buildings and towers, footbridges and ships' anti-roll tanks use them. The depth tunes it (f1 rises with d until the liquid is deep), and screens or baffles across the tank damp it. The rest of the liquid rides along as mass, so the structure on its own sways at fs′ = fs√(M/(M + mL − m1)), and the mass ratio is μ = m1/(M + mL − m1). Den Hartog's tuning den-hartog for a force F on it is then

f_d = \frac{f_s'}{1 + \mu}, \qquad \zeta_d = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1 + \mu)^3}},

which holds the peak of its sway X to

\frac{|X|k}{F} = \sqrt{1 + 2/\mu}.

The water's own damping is far too little: the structure's peak splits in two and each is nearly as high. Real dampers are nonlinear when the waves grow large (they break, and the frequency shifts), which this linear model leaves out; they're tuned and tested on shaking tables.

Worked example

The page opens on a steel rod (7850 kg/m³) 25 mm in radius, in water, on springs that give it 20 Hz in vacuum; a 600 × 400 × 3 mm steel plate; a tank 1 m long and 1 m wide filled to 0.5 m; and a 20 t structure at 0.5 Hz with 1 % damping.

  1. The rod. Per metre, ms = 7850 × π × 0.025² = 15.41 kg and ma = 1000 × π × 0.025² = 1.963 kg, so fwet = 20/√(1 + 1000/7850) = 18.84 Hz.
  2. In a pipe. Tick the wall (at 1.5 times the radius): Ca = (2.25 + 1)/(2.25 − 1) = 2.6, so ma = 5.11 kg and 20/√(1 + 2.6 × 0.1274) = 17.33 Hz.
  3. The plate, dry. D = 210×10⁹ × 0.003³/(12 × 0.91) = 519.2 N·m and ρph = 23.55 kg/m², so mode (1, 1) is at (π/2)(1/0.6² + 1/0.4²)√(519.2/23.55) = 66.6 Hz.
  4. The plate, wet. Its modal mass is 23.55 × 0.6 × 0.4/4 = 1.413 kg; the page's integral gives 11.9 kg of water, so β = 8.43 and 66.6/√9.43 = 21.7 Hz. Water on both sides: β = 16.86, and 15.8 Hz.
  5. The tank. κ1 = π/m, tanh(π × 0.5) = 0.91715, so ω1² = 9.81 × π × 0.91715 = 28.27 /s² and f1 = 5.317/2π = 0.846 Hz.
  6. Its analogue. mL = 500 kg; m1 = 500 × 8 × 0.91715/(π³ × 0.5) = 236.6 kg, m3 = 9.55 kg, and the page's sum leaves m0 = 250 kg.
  7. As a damper. On the structure, μ = 236.6/(20 000 + 500 − 236.6) = 0.0117 and fs′ = 0.5√(20 000/20 263) = 0.497 Hz, so Den Hartog asks for 0.497/1.0117 = 0.491 Hz: the tank's 0.846 Hz is far off, and the peak only falls from 50.0 to the page's 47.7.
  8. Tuned (the trial A tuned liquid damper: 2 × 2 m, filled to 0.4 m). mL = 1600 kg, tanh(0.2π) = 0.5569, so f1 = √(9.81 × π/2 × 0.5569)/2π = 0.466 Hz and m1 = 1600 × 8 × 0.5569/(π³ × 0.2) = 1149 kg. μ = 1149/20 451 = 0.0562, and fs′ = 0.5√(20 000/20 451) = 0.4945 Hz, so Den Hartog's 0.4945/1.0562 = 0.468 Hz is met, with ζd = 13.4 % (the trial's 13 %). The peak √(1 + 2/0.0562) = 6.05; the page's 5.58 is lower, as the structure's own 1 % helps.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the body, the plate, the tank or the damper (Things to try, top left).

  • A body accelerating in a fluid moves fluid with it: its kinetic energy ½maU² makes the body behave as if its mass were ms + ma.
  • In open fluid a sphere carries half the fluid it displaces, a cylinder all of it.
  • The fluid adds mass but no stiffness: fwet = fdry/√(1 + Caρf/ρs), set by the densities' ratio.
  • A wall close to the body forces the fluid through the gap, and the added mass grows without bound as the gap closes.
  • A plate's mode carries fluid out to about a cell's size, so its AVMI factor β falls with the mode's order, and grows with ρfa/ρph.
  • A liquid's free surface sloshes at ωn² = gκn tanh(κnd): κn√(gd) when shallow, √(gκn) when deep, whatever the depth.
  • A tank's force under a sideways motion is a rigid mass m0 plus a mass on a spring for each odd mode, the first by far the largest.
  • A tuned liquid damper is the first sloshing mode used as a tuned mass damper: its depth tunes it, and it needs screens to damp it.
