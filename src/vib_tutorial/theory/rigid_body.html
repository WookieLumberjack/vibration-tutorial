Turning instead of sliding Two ways to the equations

Why a rigid body in 3D

The pendulum and the car's pitch turn about one fixed axis, where the angular momentum is a moment of inertia times the rate, one number times another. A body free to turn any way, a box tossed in the air, a satellite, a spinning top, a rotor on its bearings, needs more: its angular velocity ω and angular momentum L are vectors, and they needn't point the same way. This page builds the equations for that from Newton's, with no gravity and no support, so only the turning is left: the body is free in space, turning about its centre of mass G.

The inertia tensor

Cut the body into bits of mass m, each at r from G. Turning at ω, a bit moves at ω × r, and its moment of momentum about G is r × m(ω × r). Add them up, with a × (b × c) = b(a·c) − c(a·b):

\mathbf{L} = \sum m\,\mathbf{r}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}) = \sum m\left(|\mathbf{r}|^2\boldsymbol{\omega} - \mathbf{r}\,(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega})\right) = \mathbf{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega},

linear in ω, so a 3 × 3 matrix, the inertia tensor about G:

\mathbf{I} = \sum m\left(|\mathbf{r}|^2\,\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{r}\,\mathbf{r}^{\mathsf T}\right), I_{xx} = \sum m(y^2+z^2),\quad I_{xy} = -\sum m\,xy.

On its diagonal are the moments of inertia about each axis (mass times distance squared from it, as the pendulum's); off it the products of inertia, which are zero when the mass is spread evenly either side of each axis, as in a uniform box about its centre. The kinetic energy is E = ½Σm|ω × r|² = ½ω·Iω = ½ω·L.

For the page's shapes, about their centres: a box of sides a, b, c has Ixx = m(b² + c²)/12 and so on round; a cylinder of radius a and length c, ma²/2 about its axis and m(3a² + c²)/12 across it; a sphere, 2ma²/5 about every axis. The page's default box, 1 kg and 30 × 20 × 10 cm, has 4.167, 8.333 and 10.83 ×10⁻³ kg·m².

A point mass mp at p from the shape's centre moves G toward it, to mpp/(m + mp), and the tensor about the new G is the shape's own moved there by the parallel-axis theorem, I + m(|d|²1 − ddᵀ) (d from G to the shape's centre), plus the point mass's own mp(|r|²1 − rrᵀ). Off the body's axes it brings products of inertia: the Inertia tab writes the tensor out.

Principal axes

I is symmetric, so it has three real eigenvalues and three perpendicular eigenvectors: the principal moments I₁ ≤ I₂ ≤ I₃ and the principal axes. With the eigenvectors as the columns of a rotation matrix P,

\mathbf{I} = P\,\mathrm{diag}(I_1, I_2, I_3)\,P^{\mathsf T},

and in the principal axes the tensor is diagonal: no products of inertia. Spun about a principal axis, L = Ikω is along ω; about any other axis it isn't, and the body wobbles. A body with a symmetry has its principal axes along it (a box's are its edges' directions); a sphere's moments are all equal, so every axis is principal and L is always along ω.

Derivatives in turning axes

Newton's second law for turning, summed over the bits (their internal forces cancel in pairs), is dL/dt = τ, the torque about G, in fixed axes. But in fixed axes I changes as the body turns. Write L in axes that turn with the body instead: its axes are the columns of a rotation matrix B, so L = BLb, and in them Ibody is constant, since the mass doesn't move in them. A unit vector fixed in the body moves at ω × it, so dB/dt applied to any v is ω × Bv, and

\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = B\frac{d\mathbf{L}_b}{dt} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{L}:

a vector's rate in fixed axes is its rate in the turning axes plus ω × it, the change that comes only from the axes turning under it. In any frame turning at Ω the same holds with Ω for ω. This is the transport theorem, and the Components tab writes it out at any instant with the numbers, in fixed axes, the body's own or its principal axes.

Euler's equations

In the body's axes, Lb = Ibodyω with Ibody constant, so dL/dt = τ becomes

\mathbf{I}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\mathbf{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \boldsymbol{\tau},

everything in the body's axes. In its principal axes I is diagonal and the three components are Euler's equations euler-1765:

I_1\dot\omega_1 + (I_3 - I_2)\,\omega_2\omega_3 = \tau_1, I_2\dot\omega_2 + (I_1 - I_3)\,\omega_3\omega_1 = \tau_2, I_3\dot\omega_3 + (I_2 - I_1)\,\omega_1\omega_2 = \tau_3.

The products of two rates are the gyroscopic terms: even with no torque ω changes, unless it lies along a principal axis (two of the three rates zero) or the moments are equal. Torque-free, two things stay fixed: L in space (its rate is the torque), so |L|, and the energy E = ½ω·L (its rate is τ·ω, the torque's power).

The intermediate axis

Spin the body at ω₀ about principal axis 1 and nudge it: ω₂ and ω₃ small. To first order ω₁ stays ω₀ and the second and third equations give

\ddot\omega_2 = \frac{(I_3 - I_1)(I_1 - I_2)}{I_2 I_3}\,\omega_0^2\,\omega_2.

If I₁ is the largest or the smallest moment the two brackets have opposite signs, the factor is negative, and the nudge just circles: the body wobbles at ω₀√((I₁ − I₂)(I₁ − I₃)/(I₂I₃)). If I₁ is the middle one the factor is positive and the nudge grows as eλt: the body turns over, and back, again and again. This is the intermediate-axis or tennis-racket theorem (and, seen on a wing nut in orbit, the Dzhanibekov effect) ashbaugh.

The page's box, spun at 6 rad/s about its middle axis, has λ = 6√((8.333 − 4.167)(10.83 − 8.333)/(4.167 × 10.83)) = 2.88 /s; nudged 1° (0.01745 rad) off it, the nudge reaches about a radian in ln(57.3)/2.88 = 1.4 s, when it turns over. About its greatest axis it wobbles instead, at 6√((10.83 − 4.167)(10.83 − 8.333)/(4.167 × 8.333)) = 4.16 rad/s.

Torques held in the body or in space

A torque τ changes L by its impulse, ∫τ dt, and the energy by its work, ∫τ·ω dt. Held in space (a push that keeps its direction), the impulse just adds up. Held in the body (a thruster fixed to it), the torque turns with the body: across a spin it points every way in turn, and L only wobbles round, which is why a spinning spacecraft fires its thrusters in pulses timed to the spin. The Check tab compares the simulation's energy and L with what the torque put in.

Writing down how it's turned

Where the body points needs three numbers, and there are several ways to write them, each with its use. The Orientation tab writes the running body's all four ways at once.

A rotation matrix B, whose columns are the body's axes in space: nine numbers, tied together by BᵀB = 1 (six conditions) so that three are free. It takes a vector's components from the body's axes to space's, v = Bvb, and turns compose by multiplying matrices. It has no awkward places, but stepped numerically it drifts from being a rotation and has to be put right.

Euler's angles, three turns in a row: here z-x-z, as on the Gyroscope page later. Turn φ about Z, which carries x to the line of nodes n̂; then θ about n̂, which tips the body's z from Z; then ψ about the body's own z:

B = R_z(\varphi)\,R_x(\theta)\,R_z(\psi).

Three numbers, as few as can be, and easy to picture: a top's precession, tilt and spin. But at θ = 0 (or 180°) the first and third turns are about the same axis, only φ + ψ counts, and a way to turn is lost: gimbal lock. Near it the angles' rates blow up whatever the body does,

\dot\varphi = \frac{\omega_1\sin\psi + \omega_2\cos\psi}{\sin\theta}, \dot\theta = \omega_1\cos\psi - \omega_2\sin\psi,\quad \dot\psi = \omega_3 - \dot\varphi\cos\theta,

(ω in the body's axes) so a simulation that steps the angles fails there. Every set of three angles has such a place: aircraft use z-y-x (yaw, pitch, roll), which locks at a pitch of ±90°.

A quaternion. Euler's rotation theorem euler-1776 says every orientation is a single turn χ about a single axis n̂. Hamilton's quaternion hamilton stores it as four numbers,

q = \left(\cos\tfrac{\chi}{2},\ \sin\tfrac{\chi}{2}\,\hat{\mathbf n}\right),\quad |q| = 1,

with q and −q the same turn. Turns compose by Hamilton's product, q ⊗ p, and the body turning at ω moves q at q̇ = ½q ⊗ (0, ω): smooth everywhere, no locked place, and only one condition to keep (|q| = 1, by dividing by it). That is why the page steps q, and why spacecraft and games do.

A homogeneous transform adds a place to a turn: the 4 × 4 matrix

T = \begin{bmatrix} B & B\mathbf{d} \\ 0\;0\;0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}:

the turn, and in its fourth column where the frame's origin is in space. Here the frame is the shape's own, with its origin at the shape's centre, which sits at Bd from G. A point of the body p, written (p, 1), goes to (Bp + Bd, 1) in space, and a direction, written (v, 0), only turns. Frames chained one after another (a robot arm's links, a camera on a moving car) multiply. The tab carries the point mass through T.

The simulation

The page keeps the body's orientation as a unit quaternion q, from which B follows, and L in space, and steps q̇ = ½q ⊗ (0, ω) and dL/dt = τ by classical Runge–Kutta (fourth order), with ω = Ibody−1BᵀL worked out at every stage and q made unit length after each step. The step is 1/400 of a turn at the body's rate (at most 5 ms), and a torque's end falls on a step's, so its impulse is exact.

Worked example

The page's defaults: a 1 kg box, 30 × 20 × 10 cm, spun at 6 rad/s about its middle axis and nudged 1° toward its greatest.

  1. The moments. About its centre, Ixx = 1 × (0.2² + 0.1²)/12 = 4.167×10⁻³, Iyy = 1 × (0.3² + 0.1²)/12 = 8.333×10⁻³ and Izz = 1 × (0.3² + 0.2²)/12 = 10.83×10⁻³ kg·m², with no products: x, y, z are its principal axes 1, 2, 3.
  2. ω and L apart. ω = 6(0, cos 1°, sin 1°) = (0, 5.999, 0.1047) rad/s, so L = Iω = (0, 0.04999, 0.001134) N·m·s, |L| = 0.05001 N·m·s, and the energy ½ω·L = 0.1500 J: the readout's numbers. ω is 1° from y but L is tan⁻¹(10.83/8.333 × tan 1°) = 1.30°, so they are 0.30° apart, and the readout says so.
  3. The flip. As in The intermediate axis, λ = 2.88 /s and the 1° grows to a radian in about 1.4 s; the Components tab's ω₂ drops through zero soon after.
  4. An off-centre mass (the fourth trial): 300 g at p = (100, 80, 0) mm. G moves 0.3/1.3 of the way, to (23.1, 18.5, 0) mm. By parallel axes Ixx = 4.167×10⁻³ + 1 × 0.01846² + 0.3 × (0.08 − 0.01846)² = 5.644×10⁻³ kg·m², and the product Ixy = −1 × 0.02308 × 0.01846 − 0.3 × (0.1 − 0.02308)(0.08 − 0.01846) = −1.846×10⁻³ kg·m², which is −(mmp/(m + mp))xy = −(0.3/1.3) × 0.1 × 0.08: the Inertia tab's bold −0.00185. Its principal moments are 5.036, 11.25 and 14.62 ×10⁻³ kg·m², axis 1 turned 18.2° from x toward y.

Things to try

Each of these sets the body up and lets it go (Things to try, top left).

  • A body's angular momentum about its centre of mass is L = Iω, with I = Σm(|r|²1 − rrᵀ) a 3 × 3 tensor: moments on its diagonal, products of inertia off it.
  • Its eigenvectors are the principal axes, in which it is diagonal; spun about one, L is along ω, about any other it isn't.
  • A vector's rate in fixed axes is its rate in turning axes plus Ω × it; in the body's axes, where I is constant, dL/dt = τ becomes Euler's equations, Iω̇ + ω × Iω = τ.
  • Torque-free, L and the energy are kept; spun about its middle principal axis a body turns over and back, about the least or greatest it only wobbles.
  • A torque changes L by its impulse; held in the body across a spin, it turns with it and L only wobbles.
  • An orientation can be written as a rotation matrix, three Euler angles (which lock where two of their turns line up, and whose rates blow up near it), a unit quaternion (one turn about one axis, never singular: what simulations step) or, with a place, a 4 × 4 homogeneous transform.
