Correlation

A model of a structure (finite elements, or here a chain of masses and springs) is never quite right: a joint stiffer than drawn, a mass left out. A modal test measures the real thing, and correlation compares the two mode by mode. Here the Simulation page's chain is the structure, and the model is a copy of it with errors set on the left: since both are known, what the correlation says can be checked against what is wrong.

The modal assurance criterion

The MAC compares two shapes, whatever their scale:

MAC(ψ, φ) = |ψHφ|² / (ψHψ φTφ),

1 when they are the same shape, 0 when they are orthogonal. It is worked out at the sensors only: the test knows nothing else. Every test mode is paired with the model mode it matches best, best pair first, and only above a MAC of 0.5. Pairing by frequency alone goes wrong once the model's errors move modes past each other; the shapes don't. A good correlation has MACs above about 0.9 on the diagonal, small ones off it, and frequency errors of a few per cent.

What the shapes don't see

A model uniformly too stiff, or too heavy, has exactly the right shapes and wrong frequencies. A single spring 30 % too stiff barely changes the shapes either: on a chain the MAC stays near 0.99. Frequency errors are the sharper evidence, and the pattern of them over the modes says where the error is: a spring changes a mode's frequency in proportion to how much it stretches in it, a mass in proportion to how much it moves. That is what sensitivity-based updating uses.

Sensors

With too few sensors, or badly placed ones, different modes look alike: their shapes, seen at those points only, are nearly parallel. The test's auto-MAC (its shapes against each other) shows it: its off-diagonal terms rise. Sensors are placed to keep them small. A sensor at a node of a mode sees nothing of it.

COMAC

The coordinate MAC turns the comparison round: per sensor, over all the pairs, how well the test's and the model's shapes agree there. Where it drops the shapes disagree, which can point to a region of the model to look at. It is a weak locator: a stiffness error changes the shapes everywhere a little, and the test's own noise is spread over every sensor.

The test's own errors

The test is not exact either: its noise and its curve fit give each mode an error of its own, and weakly excited modes may be missed. The correlation can't be finer than that: with no error at all in the model, the MACs and frequency errors left are the test's.

Updating by sensitivities

Updating changes the model's parameters p (here its masses, springs or dampers) until it matches the test. The residual r is test minus model: each pair's frequency, relatively, and if asked its shape at the sensors and its damping ratio. How the model's frequencies move with each parameter is its sensitivity; with mass-normalized φ,

∂ωr²/∂p = φrT(∂K/∂p − ωr² ∂M/∂p)φr,

so a spring changes ln fr by half its share of the mode's strain energy, and a mass by minus half its share of the kinetic energy (the Sensitivity tab). A spring that hardly stretches in a mode can't be seen in that mode's frequency. Each Gauss–Newton step solves the linearized problem, the slopes J of the residual against ln p, by least squares,

min ‖r + JΔ‖² + λ²‖θ + Δ‖²,

θ the change in ln p so far, and repeats until nothing moves. The Updating tab follows each parameter, as a fraction of the structure's true value, step by step.

What the data can't settle

With fewer independent residuals than parameters some combinations of them change nothing measured: J's singular values show them, near 0. Masses and springs together are the classic case: scaling every one by the same factor leaves every frequency and shape as it was, so a test of modes alone can't fix the model's overall mass. The least-squares step then takes the smallest change it can, and the answer fits the test but needn't be the structure. Regularization (λ) adds a penalty on the change: it settles those directions by keeping the model near where it started, at the price of the fit. Too much, and the model keeps its errors. A measured mass, or fewer parameters chosen with engineering judgment (the joints, not every element), are better than a large λ. And the test's own errors go into the model: the updated parameters are only as good as the modes they were fitted to.
