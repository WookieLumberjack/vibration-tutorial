Correlation

A model of a structure (finite elements, or here a chain of masses and springs) is never quite right: a joint stiffer than drawn, a mass left out. A modal test measures the real thing, and correlation compares the two mode by mode. Here the Simulation page's chain is the structure, and the model is a copy of it with errors set on the left: since both are known, what the correlation says can be checked against what is wrong.

The page opens on the error drawn: the model's third spring 30 % too stiff, against a hammer test of the structure. Correlation says how far the model is from the test; updating, at the end, changes the model's parameters until it matches. Both work with modes: frequencies fr, damping ratios and shapes.

The modal assurance criterion

The MAC allemang-brown compares a test shape ψ with a model shape φ, whatever their scale:

\mathrm{MAC}(\psi, \phi) = \frac{|\psi^{H}\phi|^2}{(\psi^{H}\psi)(\phi^{T}\phi)}

1 when they are the same shape, 0 when they are orthogonal. It is worked out at the sensors only: the test knows nothing else. Every test mode is paired with the model mode it matches best, best pair first, and only above a MAC of 0.5. Pairing by frequency alone goes wrong once the model's errors move modes past each other; the shapes don't. A good correlation has MACs above about 0.9 on the diagonal, small ones off it, and frequency errors of a few per cent.

What the shapes don't see

A model uniformly too stiff, or too heavy, has exactly the right shapes and wrong frequencies: every fr scales by √(stiffness factor/mass factor). A single spring 30 % too stiff barely changes the shapes either: on a chain the MAC stays near 0.99. Frequency errors are the sharper evidence, and the pattern of them over the modes says where the error is: a spring changes a mode's frequency in proportion to how much it stretches in it, a mass in proportion to how much it moves (below).

Sensors

With too few sensors, or badly placed ones, different modes look alike: their shapes, seen at those points only, are nearly parallel. The test's auto-MAC (its shapes against each other) shows it: its off-diagonal terms rise. Sensors are placed to keep them small. A sensor at a node of a mode sees nothing of it.

COMAC

The coordinate MAC turns the comparison round: per sensor j, over all the pairs r, how well the test's and the model's shapes agree there,

\mathrm{COMAC}_j = \frac{\bigl(\sum_r |\psi_{jr}\,\phi_{jr}|\bigr)^2}{\sum_r |\psi_{jr}|^2\,\sum_r \phi_{jr}^2}

with each test shape first scaled to its model shape. Where it drops the shapes disagree, which can point to a region of the model to look at. It is a weak locator: a stiffness error changes the shapes everywhere a little, and the test's own noise is spread over every sensor.

The test's own errors

The test is not exact either: its noise and its curve fit give each mode an error of its own, and weakly excited modes may be missed. The correlation can't be finer than that: with no error at all in the model, the MACs and frequency errors left are the test's.

Updating by sensitivities

Updating changes the model's parameters p (here its masses, springs or dampers) until it matches the test. How a model frequency moves with a parameter is its sensitivity; with mass-normalized φr fox-kapoor,

\frac{\partial \omega_r^2}{\partial p} = \phi_r^{T}\left(\frac{\partial K}{\partial p} - \omega_r^2\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial p}\right)\phi_r

For the chain this is short. A spring kj joins masses j − 1 and j and stretches by φj r − φj−1, r in mode r (with φ0r = 0 at the wall), so

\frac{\partial \ln f_r}{\partial \ln k_j} = \frac{k_j\,(\phi_{jr} - \phi_{j-1,r})^2}{2\,\omega_r^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln f_r}{\partial \ln m_j} = -\frac{m_j\,\phi_{jr}^2}{2}

half the spring's share of the mode's strain energy, and minus half the mass's share of its kinetic energy (the Sensitivity tab). The shares add up to 1 in each mode, so the springs' sensitivities add to ½ and the masses' to −½. A spring that hardly stretches in a mode can't be seen in that mode's frequency:

The residual ε is test minus model: each pair's frequency, relatively, and if asked its shape at the sensors and its damping ratio. Its slopes against θ, the change in ln p so far, make the matrix J. Each Gauss–Newton step nocedal-wright solves the linearized problem by least squares,

\min_{\Delta}\; \|\varepsilon + J\Delta\|^2 + \lambda^2 \|\theta + \Delta\|^2

takes the step Δ, and repeats until nothing moves (the slopes change as the model does, so one step isn't enough). The Updating tab follows each parameter, as a fraction of the structure's true value, step by step.

What the data can't settle

With fewer independent residuals than parameters some combinations of them change nothing measured: J's singular values show them, near 0. Masses and springs together are the classic case: scaling every one by the same factor leaves every frequency and shape as it was (the sensitivities above add to ½ − ½ = 0), so a test of modes alone can't fix the model's overall mass. The least-squares step then takes the smallest change it can, and the answer fits the test but needn't be the structure. Regularization (λ, Tikhonov's tikhonov-arsenin) adds a penalty on the change: it settles those directions by keeping the model near where it started, at the price of the fit. Too much, and the model keeps its errors. A measured mass, or fewer parameters chosen with engineering judgment (the joints, not every element), are better than a large λ. And the test's own errors go into the model: the updated parameters are only as good as the modes they were fitted to friswell-mottershead.

Worked example

The page opens with the model's k3 1.3 times the structure's, on the uniform 4-mass chain (1 kg, 400 N/m; modes at 1.105, 3.183, 4.877 and 5.982 Hz). Exactly, model against structure:

  1. The frequencies. The model's are 1.129, 3.312, 4.906 and 6.379 Hz: +2.2, +4.1, +0.6 and +6.6 %, the first trial's numbers. All too high, mode 3 least.
  2. The shapes. MACs of 0.999, 0.993, 0.992 and 0.988: every pair above 0.98, nothing the shapes alone would flag.
  3. The sensitivities. In mode 1 (ω1 = 6.946 rad/s) the mass-normalized shape is 0.2280, 0.4285, 0.5774 and 0.6565, so k3 stretches 0.5774 − 0.4285 = 0.149 and ∂ln f1/∂ln k3 = 400 × 0.149²/(2 × 6.946²) = 0.092. Over the four modes, 0.092, 0.167, 0.026 and 0.216: the figure's shares, halved. Mode 3's is small, so k3 hardly moves it.
  4. Where the error is. The springs' columns of sensitivities are (0.216, 0.167, 0.092, 0.026) for k1, (0.167, 0, 0.167, 0.167) for k2, (0.092, 0.167, 0.026, 0.216) for k3 and (0.026, 0.167, 0.216, 0.092) for k4. Only k3's is smallest at mode 3 and largest at mode 4, as the errors are.
  5. Linearized. To first order a factor 1.3 shifts each frequency by 1.3 to the power of its sensitivity: +2.4, +4.5, +0.7 and +5.8 %, against the exact +2.2, +4.1, +0.6 and +6.6. Close, not exact: the reason Gauss–Newton steps again.

One step. Fit the change in ln k3 alone to the four frequency errors by least squares, with Sr the sensitivities above:

\theta = \frac{\sum_r S_r \ln\bigl(f_r^{\,\text{model}}/f_r^{\,\text{test}}\bigr)}{\sum_r S_r^2} = \frac{0.0226}{0.0833} = 0.271

So the model's k3 is e0.271 = 1.31 times the structure's, found in one linear step from the frequencies alone; the truth is 1.3.

Masses too. Each mode's spring sensitivities add to 0.5 and its mass sensitivities to −0.5: scaling every mass and spring by 1.1 moves no frequency at all, the direction the data can't see.

Against the hammer test rather than the exact structure, the numbers shift a little by the test's own errors, as the trials' notes say.
