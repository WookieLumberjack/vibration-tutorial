Is it linear? Homogeneity

Everything a modal test fits assumes the structure is linear: double the force and every response doubles, so the FRF X/F is the same at any level. That's homogeneity, and the first check is to measure the FRF at two or three levels and lay them over each other. A linear structure's coincide; a nonlinear one's move with the level. Joints, contacts, friction and large deflections all do it, and only at the levels where they act: a gap that never closes is invisible. Test at the levels the structure works at.

Two more checks come almost free. Reciprocity maxwell-1864: a linear structure's Hjk = Hkj, so driving at k and measuring at j must give what driving at j and measuring at k does; a nonlinear one's needn't. And the excitation: a random force spreads its energy over every frequency and every level, so the FFT's averaging hands back a "best linear" FRF, smeared, and the nonlinearity shows only as a coherence γ² below 1 at the peaks. A sine at one level and frequency at a time hides nothing, which is why the checks below use one.

Stepped sine, up and down

A stepped sine holds each frequency until the response is steady, reads its amplitude and phase, and steps on. Swept up and then down, a linear structure gives the same curve both ways. A hardening spring's peak leans to higher frequencies as the level rises, so the response has two steady states over a band: swept up it rides the upper branch until that ends and drops off; swept down it climbs onto it late. The two sweeps disagree between the jumps, which no linear structure can do. (The Duffing & Mathieu page, later, follows one nonlinear oscillator duffing through its jump, chaos and all.)

Harmonic balance

The page adds one element to the chain, alongside one of its springs: a cubic spring, or a gap. With the chain's own friction, these are the force laws it can have:

It finds each step's steady state by harmonic balance with one harmonic, x = Re(X eiΩt): each nonlinear force is replaced by its first harmonic, its describing function gelb-vandervelde, and the chain's equations become

(K - \Omega^2 M + i\,\Omega\,C)\,X + N(X) = F

with N(X) the elements' first harmonics. Each is worked out on the amplitude A of the motion it sees (a spring's stretch, the travel of friction's mass):

  • A cubic spring. Since cos³θ = ¾cos θ + ¼cos 3θ, the first harmonic of k3(A cos θ)³ is ¾k3A³cos θ: an added stiffness ¾k3A², growing with the amplitude. (The ¼cos 3θ left over is the 3X of Distortion.)
  • A gap of clearance g and spring kg adds nothing while A ≤ g, and then a stiffness that rises toward kg as the gap becomes a small share of the swing (below).
  • Coulomb friction coulomb Ff takes 4FfA a cycle (four strokes of A against it). A damper takes πceqΩA²; setting the two equal gives the equivalent damper below, weaker as the motion grows.
k_{eq} = k_g\Bigl[1 - \frac{2}{\pi}\Bigl(\arcsin\frac{g}{A} + \frac{g}{A}\sqrt{1 - \frac{g^2}{A^2}}\Bigr)\Bigr] c_{eq} = \frac{4F_f}{\pi\,\Omega A}

Why the peak leans: the added stiffness moves each mode's frequency as an updated spring would (Updating by sensitivities): a spring at the wall stretches φ1r in mode r, so

\omega^2 \approx \omega_r^2 + \tfrac34 k_3 A^2\,\phi_{1r}^2

and as A grows with the level so does the frequency the peak sits at: the backbone the peaks lie on. Each step starts Newton from the last step's X, as the structure starts from where it is; where that branch ends, the stable solution nearest it is where it jumps to (the real Jacobian's determinant changes sign at each fold, so the unstable middle branch has it negative).

Distortion

One harmonic leaves out the rest: a nonlinear element turns a sine into a distorted wave. The Distortion tab steps the chain in time at one level and frequency (the exact FOH update, the nonlinear forces an input linear over each step, found by Newton) until a cycle repeats itself, and splits the steady response into harmonics. A symmetric nonlinearity (a cubic spring, a gap, friction: the same force reversed when the motion is) makes odd harmonics, 3X and 5X; an asymmetric one (a contact on one side) makes even ones too. Harmonics where the input was a pure sine are a second test of linearity.

Which force made this response?

Sometimes the force is what's wanted, and it can't be measured: a wheel on a rail, a gust, an impact inside a machine. The response can. With the force on one mass, sampled every h and linear between samples, the exact FOH update, zk = Φzk−1 + Γ0fk−1 + Γ1fk, read by a sensor as yk = c zk + d fk, makes every sample a sum over the force's samples so far: y = G f, with

G_{kk} = c\,\Gamma_1 + d, \qquad G_{kj} = c\,\Phi^{\,k-j-1}(\Phi\Gamma_1 + \Gamma_0) \quad (j < k)

and zero above the diagonal (a response can't come before its force). The entries are the Markov parameters juang, the sampled impulse response: each column is it, shifted down to start at its own sample.

Identifying the force is solving for f. (d is 0 for a displacement; an acceleration feels the force on its own mass at once, d = 1/m there.)

Why the plain inverse fails

Written in G's singular directions (G = Σsiuiviᵀ),

f = G^{-1}y = \sum_i \frac{u_i^{T} y}{s_i}\,v_i

and G's singular values si fall over many decades: the chain filters out whatever is faster than its modes, and a displacement far from the force hardly sees its last samples. The noise's share of each direction is divided by si, so the smallest turn noise into a force thousands of times too large. The Picard plot hansen-picard (the Singular values tab) shows it: |uiᵀy| falls with si while the signal lasts, then levels off at the noise, and beyond that point dividing by si only amplifies noise.

Regularization

Truncated SVD keeps the first k directions and drops the rest. Tikhonov tikhonov-arsenin minimizes ‖Gf − y‖² + λ²‖f‖², whose solution is

f_\lambda = \sum_i \frac{s_i^2}{s_i^2 + \lambda^2}\,\frac{u_i^{T} y}{s_i}\,v_i

each direction weighted by si²/(si² + λ²): kept where si ≫ λ, dropped where si ≪ λ, half at si = λ. The L-curve hansen-lcurve plots the residual ‖Gf − y‖ against the force's size ‖f‖ as λ varies, on log axes: too small a λ and ‖f‖ explodes with the noise, too large and the residual grows as the force is smoothed away. Its corner balances them. With the noise's size known, the discrepancy principle picks the λ whose residual is that size: fitting the data closer than its noise only fits the noise. No λ brings back what G filtered out, though: a sensor that feels the force directly (an accelerometer on the force's own mass) helps far more than any choice of λ.

Worked example

The trials' chain: three 1 kg masses on 1000 N/m springs with 4 N·s/m dampers, the force on mass 3. Mode 1 is at 2.240 Hz (ω1 = 14.07 rad/s, ω1² = 198.1), its mass-normalized shape 0.3280, 0.5910 and 0.7370, and since the dampers are 0.004 × the springs its damping ratio is ζ = 0.004 × 14.07/2 = 0.02814.

  1. Linear. Near its peak mode 1 alone gives |X3/F| = 0.7370²/(2 × 0.02814 × 198.1) = 0.0487 m/N, the first trial's curve at every level.
  2. The cubic spring's lean. With k3 = 2 × 10⁶ N/m³ beside spring 1, at 0.5 N the swept peak is at 2.319 Hz, where mass 1 (spring 1's stretch, from the wall) swings 9.82 mm. The added stiffness is ¾ × 2 × 10⁶ × 0.00982² = 144.6 N/m, and with φ11² = 0.1076 the backbone puts the peak at √(198.1 + 144.6 × 0.1076) = √213.7 = 14.62 rad/s = 2.326 Hz: one sweep step (0.0094 Hz) from where it is. The peak's height stays 0.0483 m/N: a spring moves the peak, it doesn't damp it.
  3. Friction's fade. With 0.1 N of friction on mass 3 and 0.2 N of force, the peak's X3 is 3.55 mm at 2.234 Hz (Ω = 14.04 rad/s), so ceq = 4 × 0.1/(π × 14.04 × 0.00355) = 2.55 N·s/m. On mass 3 it adds 2.55 × 0.7370² = 1.39 to the mode's own 0.004 × 198.1 = 0.79, and the peak is 0.5432/(14.07 × 2.18) = 0.0177 m/N, as swept. At 2 N, X3 = 91.2 mm, ceq = 0.099 N·s/m adds only 0.054, and the peak is 0.0456 m/N, near the linear 0.0487.
  4. The force's samples. 300 samples over four of mode 1's periods (1.79 s), h = 5.95 ms. Measured as mass 1's displacement, G's singular values run from 7.7 × 10⁻³ down to 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰, 7.7 decades, with two more at round-off. The 2 % noise is 1.70 × 10⁻⁴ m in all.
  5. The L-curve's corner is at λ = 5.44 × 10⁻⁵: 37 of the 300 singular values are above it. Its residual, 1.63 × 10⁻⁴ m, is just under the noise's size, and the impact comes back within 15 %, its peak 0.95 N against 0.997 N.
  6. The discrepancy principle asks the residual to be the noise's 1.70 × 10⁻⁴ m exactly: λ = 1.27 × 10⁻⁴, 34 directions above it, 18 % off. Here the corner does slightly better; both are far from the plain inverse.
  7. An accelerometer on mass 3 instead: G's first entry is 0.98, nearly 1/m = 1, its singular values span 3.0 decades, and λ = 0.024 recovers the impact within 6 %.

Things to try

Each of these loads a chain into the Simulation page and sets up the test.

  • A linear structure's FRF is the same at every level: measure at two or three and compare. Reciprocity and coherence are further checks.
  • Swept up and down, a leaning peak jumps at different frequencies each way.
  • A describing function is an element's first harmonic: a cubic spring adds ¾k3A², friction a damper 4Ff/πΩA.
  • Hardening springs and gaps lean the peak up as the level rises; friction damps less.
  • Symmetric nonlinearities make odd harmonics from a pure sine.
  • y = Gf: G's tiny singular values turn noise into force, so the plain inverse is useless.
  • Tikhonov or truncated SVD keeps the directions the data can carry; the L-curve's corner picks λ.
  • A sensor that feels the force directly beats any regularization.
