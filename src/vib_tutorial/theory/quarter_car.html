The PSD and H G Hᴴ Isolation's damping trade-off

A road as a random input

A road's roughness is a random process in space: its height's PSD Gd(n), with n in cycles per metre. ISO 8608 fits measured roads to Gd(n) = Gd(n₀)(n/n₀)−2 with n₀ = 0.1 cycles/m, and grades them by Gd(n₀): class A (a new motorway) 16 × 10−6 m³, then four times more per class, to H. Driven at speed v, a wavelength 1/n passes at f = vn, so the height's PSD in time is Gz(f) = Gd(f/v)/v ∝ v/f²: the road's vertical velocity is white noise, of intensity ∝ v.

The quarter-car

One corner of a car: the body ms on the suspension (ks, cs), on the wheel mu, on the tyre kt, on the road. It has two modes: body bounce, near √(kskt/(ks + kt)/ms)/2π (1 to 1.5 Hz in a car), and wheel hop, near √((ks + kt)/mu)/2π (10 to 15 Hz). Each output's PSD is |H(f)|² Gz(f), its mean square the area under it: the Random vibration page's H G Hᴴ with one input. Three outputs matter:

  • Body acceleration: ride comfort (ISO 2631 weights it to how people feel it; the page leaves it unweighted).
  • Dynamic tyre load over the static load: road holding. A tyre can only push, so it leaves the road when the load swings below −1 × static; for a Gaussian load that happens a fraction Φ(−W/σ) of the time.
  • Suspension travel: the room the suspension needs (three times the RMS to stay off the bump stops).

The conflict

A soft damper isolates the body above its bounce but lets the wheel hop; a firm one controls the wheel but passes the road to the body. So the best damper for comfort (ζ ≈ 0.2 on the body) is lighter than the best for grip (ζ ≈ 0.5), and no passive setting is best for both. A softer spring helps comfort at every damper but needs more travel; a heavier wheel costs grip. Plotting comfort against grip for every setting draws the conflict diagram: passive suspensions live on its curves, and active and semi-active ones (the Active control page's skyhook) are how to get below them.
