A road as a random input

A road's roughness is a random process in space: its height's PSD Gd(n), with the spatial frequency n in cycles per metre. ISO 8608 iso8608 fits measured roads to

G_d(n) = G_d(n_0)\,(n/n_0)^{-2}, \qquad n_0 = 0.1\ \text{cycles/m}

and grades them by Gd(n0): class A (a new motorway) 16 × 10⁻⁶ m³, then four times more per class, to H. Driven at speed v, a wavelength 1/n passes at f = vn, and a band dn of the road becomes a band v dn in time, so the height's PSD in time is

G_z(f) = \frac{G_d(f/v)}{v} = \frac{G_d(n_0)\,n_0^2\,v}{f^2}

Its velocity's PSD, (2πf)2Gz, is flat: the road's vertical velocity is white noise, of intensity Gv = 4π2Gd(n0)n02v, in proportion to the speed.

The quarter-car

One corner of a car: the body ms on the suspension (ks, cs), on the wheel mu, on the tyre kt, on the road zr.

It has two modes. In body bounce the wheel barely moves against the stiff tyre, so the body rides the suspension and tyre in series; in wheel hop the body barely moves, so the wheel bounces between the suspension and the tyre in parallel:

f_{\text{bounce}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_s k_t}{(k_s + k_t)\,m_s}}, \qquad f_{\text{hop}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_s + k_t}{m_u}}

1 to 1.5 Hz and 10 to 15 Hz in a car. Each output's PSD is |H(f)|2Gz(f), its mean square the area under it: the Random vibration page's HGHᴴ with one input. Three outputs matter:

  • Body acceleration: ride comfort.
  • Dynamic tyre load kt(zr − xu) over the static load W = (ms + mu)g: road holding. A tyre can only push, so it leaves the road when the load swings below −W; for a Gaussian load of RMS σt that happens a fraction Φ(−W/σt) of the time, Φ the standard normal distribution.
  • Suspension travel xs − xu: the room the suspension needs (three times the RMS to stay off the bump stops).

The travel has a short answer. With the wheel taken as rigid, the body is a single oscillator on a base whose velocity is white, and the area under its relative displacement's PSD is

\sigma_{\text{travel}}^2 \approx \frac{G_v\,m_s}{4c_s}

whatever the spring: a firmer damper is the only passive way to need less room.

Weighting for comfort

People don't feel every frequency alike. ISO 2631-1 iso2631 weights the vertical acceleration of a seated body by a filter Wk before taking its RMS, the weighted acceleration aw: the weighting is largest round 4 to 8 Hz, where the body's own organs and spine resonate griffin, and falls away above and below. The car's body bounce, at about 1 Hz, is below that band and counts for less than its share of the plain RMS; the harshness the tyre and the damper pass through between 4 and 15 Hz counts for more. So the plain RMS this page shows overstates what a soft suspension's bounce costs and understates a firm damper's harshness: with the weighting, the best damper for comfort tends to move softer. The standard also says how a weighted RMS reads (from not uncomfortable to extremely uncomfortable), and how to add the seat, the back and the feet.

The conflict

A soft damper isolates the body above its bounce but lets the wheel hop; a firm one controls the wheel but passes the road to the body. So the best damper for comfort (ζ ≈ 0.2 on the body) is lighter than the best for grip (ζ ≈ 0.5), and no passive setting is best for both. A softer spring helps comfort at every damper but needs more travel; a heavier wheel costs grip. Plotting comfort against grip for every setting draws the conflict diagram: passive suspensions live on its curves, and active and semi-active ones (the Active control page's skyhook, later) are how to get below them.

The whole car: wheelbase filtering

A car's rear wheels follow its front ones over the same road, a time τ = L/v later, L the wheelbase: the bounce–pitch car with yr(t) = yf(t − τ). At a frequency f the two inputs differ in phase by 2πfτ, so their mean, which drives bounce, and their half-difference, which drives pitch, are the front's times

\Bigl|\frac{1 + \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i} f\tau}}{2}\Bigr| = |\cos \pi f\tau|, \qquad \Bigl|\frac{1 - \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i} f\tau}}{2}\Bigr| = |\sin \pi f\tau|

Bounce is not driven at all where the wheelbase holds an odd number of half wavelengths, f = (2j + 1)/2τ: one wheel on a crest, the other in a trough, the car only pitches. Pitch is not driven where it holds a whole number of wavelengths, f = j/τ. A quarter-car can't see this: it is why a car feels different at speeds whose filter nulls fall on its body modes, and why Olley's rule sets the front's frequency below the rear's.

Worked example

The page opens on a quarter of a family car: ms = 300 kg, ks = 20 kN/m, cs = 1.5 kN·s/m, mu = 40 kg, kt = 200 kN/m, at 72 km/h (20 m/s) on a class C road, Gd(n0) = 256 × 10⁻⁶ m³. The road's velocity is white at

G_v = 4\pi^2 \times 256\times 10^{-6} \times 0.1^2 \times 20 = 2.02\times 10^{-3}\ (\text{m/s})^2/\text{Hz}

The springs in series are 18.2 kN/m, so the body bounces near √(18\,200/300)/2π = 1.24 Hz, and the wheel hops near √(220\,000/40)/2π = 11.8 Hz; the body's damping ratio is 1500/2√(20\,000 × 300) = 0.31. The travel estimate is √(2.02 × 10⁻³ × 300/6000) = 10.1 mm RMS, against the full model's 10.7 mm (the tyre's give adds the rest). The page's readout: body acceleration 1.48 m/s² RMS, travel 10.7 mm, and the tyre's load swinging by 24 % of its static 3334 N.

A wheelbase of 2.7 m at 20 m/s gives τ = 0.135 s: bounce isn't driven at 3.7 Hz, nor pitch at 7.4 Hz.

Things to try

Each of these sets up the road and the car (Things to try, top left).

  • A road is a PSD in space; at speed v its vertical velocity is white, of intensity in proportion to v.
  • A quarter-car has body bounce (about 1 Hz) and wheel hop (about 10 Hz); each output's PSD is |H|2Gz.
  • The best damper for comfort is lighter than the best for grip: passive suspensions trade one for the other.
  • Soft springs buy comfort with sag; travel goes as √(ms/cs); unsprung mass costs grip.
  • Comfort is weighted towards 4 to 8 Hz; the wheelbase filters bounce and pitch at speeds set by L/v.
