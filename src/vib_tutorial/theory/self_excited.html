Poles The jump

Motion that feeds itself

Forced vibration needs a force at the frequency of the motion; free vibration dies away. A self-excited vibration has neither: a steady source (a power supply, the wind, a current, a rotating shaft) feeds it at its own frequency, because the force depends on the motion itself. Near rest the effect is a damping term, and when the source's share makes the net damping negative, the rest state is unstable: the smallest disturbance grows.

Hopf bifurcation and limit cycles

As the parameter (ε, the wind speed) rises past its critical value, a pair of complex poles crosses into the right half plane: a Hopf bifurcation. Linear theory says the motion then grows without end; in practice a nonlinearity, damping that rises with amplitude, stops it on a limit cycle, a closed orbit that motions on both sides approach. Averaging (the energy each cycle puts in against what it takes out) gives its size. If it grows from zero at the onset the bifurcation is supercritical; if the curve folds back (as galloping's does) the system has hysteresis: two stable motions at one speed, and jumps between them, as with the Duffing oscillator's jump.

Van der Pol

ẍ + ω²x = ω(ε − x²/xr²)ẋ: negative damping ε at small amplitude, positive past xr√ε. For small ε the motion is nearly a sine of amplitude 2xr√ε at ω; for large ε it becomes a relaxation oscillation, slow creeps and fast snaps, its period growing like ε/ω. Van der Pol wrote it for a valve oscillator; it describes any system whose energy source pushes harder at small motion than large.

Galloping

A bluff section moving across a wind U at ẏ sees the wind at an angle α = ẏ/U. If the force that angle makes points the way it is moving, the wind does work on it every cycle. The quasi-steady force coefficient's slope at α = 0 decides: Den Hartog's criterion, unstable when dCL/dα + CD < 0. For a square, Parkinson and Smith's fit is CFy = A₁α − A₃α³ + A₅α⁵ − A₇α⁷ with A₁ = 2.69, so the wind adds damping −½ρUDA₁ and galloping starts at

Ug = 4mζω / (ρDA₁),

proportional to the mass–damping (Scruton) number. Above it the amplitude grows roughly in proportion to U, with no upper limit; the square's higher terms give two stable branches over a range of speeds. Iced power lines, tall square towers and bridge decks gallop.

Vortex-induced vibration

A cylinder in a flow sheds vortices alternately from each side at fs = St U/D (St ≈ 0.2), with a fluctuating lift at that frequency. When fs nears fn (reduced velocity U/fnD ≈ 1/St = 5) the structure's motion takes control of the shedding: over a band of speeds the vortices shed at fn instead (lock-in). The page couples the structure to a van der Pol wake oscillator (Facchinetti, de Langre and Biolley) driven by the structure's acceleration. The amplitude is self-limiting, about a diameter at most, and the lock-in band widens as the mass ratio falls. Risers, chimneys and cables are protected by helical strakes that break up the shedding.

Flutter

A wing section in plunge h and pitch θ: the lift, proportional to θ and to the dynamic pressure, couples the two modes. As the speed rises their frequencies approach, and at the flutter speed the two modes merge into one whose damping crosses zero: the wind feeds a combined bending–torsion motion with the right phase between them. Moving the centre of mass forward onto the elastic axis (mass balance) removes the inertial coupling and raises the flutter speed. Separately, the pitch spring alone is overcome at the divergence speed, a static instability. The page uses quasi-steady lift (the lift follows the angle at once); real flutter analysis uses unsteady aerodynamics (Theodorsen), which changes the numbers but not the coalescence.
