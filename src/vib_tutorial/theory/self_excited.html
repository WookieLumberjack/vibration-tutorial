Poles The jump

Motion that feeds itself

Forced vibration needs a force at the frequency of the motion; free vibration dies away. A self-excited vibration has neither: a steady source (a power supply, the wind, a current, a rotating shaft) feeds it at its own frequency, because the force depends on the motion itself. Near rest the effect is a damping term, and when the source's share makes the net damping negative, the rest state is unstable: the smallest disturbance grows.

Hopf bifurcation and limit cycles

As the parameter (ε below, or the wind speed) rises past its critical value, a pair of complex poles crosses into the right half plane: a Hopf bifurcation. Linear theory says the motion then grows without end; in practice a nonlinearity, damping that rises with amplitude, stops it on a limit cycle, a closed orbit that motions on both sides approach. Averaging gives its size: guess a sine of amplitude a, and find where the energy the source puts in over a cycle equals what the damping takes out. If it grows from zero at the onset the bifurcation is supercritical; if the curve folds back (as galloping's does) the system has hysteresis: two stable motions at one speed, and jumps between them, as with the Duffing oscillator's jump.

Van der Pol

Van der Pol's oscillator van-der-pol-1926 is the simplest:

\ddot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \omega_n\Bigl(\varepsilon - \frac{x^2}{x_r^2}\Bigr)\dot{x}.

At small amplitude the right-hand side is negative damping, ζ = −ε/2; past xr√ε it turns positive. Averaging over x = a cos ωnt, the energy in goes as ε⟨ẋ²⟩ = εa²ωn²/2 and the energy out as ⟨x²ẋ²⟩/xr² = a⁴ωn²/8xr², and they balance at

a = 2x_r\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

For small ε the motion is nearly that sine, at ωn; for large ε it becomes a relaxation oscillation, slow creeps and fast snaps, its period growing like ε/ωn. Van der Pol wrote it for a valve oscillator; it describes any system whose energy source pushes harder at small motion than large.

Galloping

A bluff section of size D moving across a wind U at ẏ sees the wind come at it at an angle α = ẏ/U. If the force across the wind that angle makes points the way it is moving, the wind does work on it every cycle.

The lift CL acts across the wind it sees and the drag CD along it; resolved onto the direction of motion, to first order in α, they give a force coefficient −(dCL/dα + CD)α. That is Den Hartog's criterion den-hartog: the section gallops if

\frac{\mathrm{d}C_L}{\mathrm{d}\alpha} + C_D < 0.

A circle can't (its lift has no slope); a square, an iced cable or a D-section can. For a square, Parkinson and Smith parkinson-smith fitted the measured force to

C_{Fy} = A_1\alpha - A_3\alpha^3 + A_5\alpha^5 - A_7\alpha^7,

with A1 = 2.69. Near rest the force is ½ρU²DA1ẏ/U: a negative damping ½ρUDA1 that grows with the wind, against the structure's 2mζωn (per length). They balance at the onset

U_g = \frac{4m\zeta\omega_n}{\rho D A_1},

proportional to the mass–damping (Scruton) number. Above it the amplitude grows roughly in proportion to U, with no upper limit; averaging the square's higher terms gives two stable branches over a range of speeds. Iced power lines, tall square towers and bridge decks gallop.

Vortex-induced vibration

A cylinder in a flow sheds vortices alternately from each side at fs = St U/D (St ≈ 0.2), with a fluctuating lift at that frequency. When fs nears fn (reduced velocity Ur = U/fnD ≈ 1/St = 5) the structure's motion takes control of the shedding: over a band of speeds the vortices shed at fn instead (lock-in). The page couples the structure to a wake oscillator facchinetti, a van der Pol oscillator q for the wake's lift, driven by the structure's acceleration:

\ddot{q} + \varepsilon_w\Omega_f(q^2 - 1)\dot{q} + \Omega_f^2 q = \frac{A_w}{D}\,\ddot{y}, (m + m_a)\ddot{y} + (c + \gamma\Omega_f\rho D^2)\dot{y} + ky = \tfrac{1}{4}\rho U^2 D\,C_{L0}\,q,

Ωf = 2π St U/D the shedding's own frequency, ma the added mass, and εw = 0.3, Aw = 12, γ = 0.8, CL0 = 0.3 fitted to experiments. Left alone the wake swings at Ωf with q ≈ 2; near fn the structure's acceleration pulls it into step. The amplitude is self-limiting, about a diameter at most, and the lock-in band widens as the mass ratio m* = m/(ρπD²/4) falls. Risers, chimneys and cables are protected by helical strakes that break up the shedding.

Flutter

A wing section of semichord b, in plunge h (down) and pitch θ (nose up) on springs kh and kθ at its elastic axis. The lift acts at the quarter chord, eb ahead of the axis, and the centre of mass sits xcgb behind it.

With S = mxcgb the static unbalance and I = mr²b² the inertia about the axis (r the radius of gyration in semichords),

m\ddot{h} + S\ddot{\theta} + k_h h = -L, \qquad S\ddot{h} + I\ddot{\theta} + k_\theta\theta = eb\,L, L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho U^2\,(2b)\,2\pi\,(\theta + \dot{h}/U):

the lift of a thin wing at the angle it sees, the pitch plus the plunge's own ḣ/U, following it at once (quasi-steady). The lift, proportional to θ and to the dynamic pressure, couples the two modes. As the speed rises their frequencies approach, and at the flutter speed UF the two modes merge into one whose damping crosses zero: the wind feeds a combined bending–torsion motion with the right phase between them. Moving the centre of mass forward onto the elastic axis (mass balance) removes the inertial coupling S and raises the flutter speed. Separately, the lift's moment eb·dL/dθ uses up the pitch spring at the divergence speed, a static instability:

U_D = \sqrt{\frac{k_\theta}{2\pi\rho b^2 e}}.

Real flutter analysis uses unsteady aerodynamics (Theodorsen theodorsen), in which the lift lags the motion; that changes the numbers but not the coalescence.

Worked example

Each model on the page's defaults.

  1. Van der Pol (1 Hz, ε = 0.2, xr = 10 mm). At rest ζ = −0.1, so a start grows; it settles at 2 × 10 × √0.2 = 8.94 mm.
  2. Galloping (a 100 mm square, 5 kg/m, 2 Hz, ζ = 0.5 %, air at 1.225 kg/m³). The structure's damping is 2 × 5 × 0.005 × 4π = 0.628 N·s/m per metre, so Ug = 4 × 5 × 0.005 × 12.57/(1.225 × 0.1 × 2.69) = 3.81 m/s. At the default 6 m/s the wind's ½ × 1.225 × 6 × 0.1 × 2.69 = 0.989 beats it, and averaging finds three amplitudes: 0.51 D and 1.28 D, stable, with 0.91 D unstable between.
  3. Vortex shedding (a 50 mm cylinder, 20 kg/m, 1 Hz, in water). m* = 20/(1000 × π × 0.05²/4) = 10.2, and ma = 1.96 kg/m. At Ur = 5.5 the current is 5.5 × 1 × 0.05 = 0.275 m/s, and the shedding alone would be at 0.2 × 5.5 = 1.1 fn: inside lock-in, which starts near 1/St = 5.
  4. Flutter, at rest (b = 0.15 m, 10 kg/m, xcg = 0.2, r = 0.5, e = 0.3, plunge 3 Hz, pitch 6 Hz). S = 10 × 0.2 × 0.15 = 0.3 kg·m/m and I = 10 × (0.5 × 0.15)² = 0.05625 kg·m²/m, so kh = 10 × (6π)² = 3553 N/m² and kθ = 0.05625 × (12π)² = 79.94 N·m/m. The inertia couples them even still: det(K − ωr²M) = 0 gives 0.4725ωr⁴ − 999.3ωr² + 284 000 = 0, so 2.93 and 6.71 Hz.
  5. Flutter and divergence. UD = √(79.94/(2π × 1.225 × 0.15² × 0.3)) = 39.2 m/s; the page finds flutter far sooner, at 19.1 m/s (17.7 m/s with the centre of mass on the axis, and none with it 0.2 semichords ahead).

Things to try

Each of these picks a model and sets it up (Things to try, top left).

  • Self-excitation is negative damping drawn from a steady source; the rest state loses stability at a Hopf bifurcation.
  • A nonlinearity sets the size of the limit cycle that follows; averaging the energy per cycle finds it.
  • Galloping starts at Ug ∝ mζ (Den Hartog: dCL/dα + CD < 0) and grows with the wind; vortex-induced vibration locks in over a band of speeds and limits itself.
  • Flutter is two modes coupled by the flow until one's damping crosses zero; mass balance raises its speed.
